📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On June 25, 1950, North Korean tanks crossed the 38th parallelTHUR-tee-ayth PAIR-uh-lelThe line of latitude that divided Korea into a communist north and an American-backed south., the line that had split Korea into a communist north and an American-backed south since 1945. Within days they had taken the southern capital of Seoul. President Truman was at home in Independence, Missouri, when Secretary of State Dean Acheson called with the news, and he flew back to Washington at once. The White House was being rebuilt, so he met his advisers across the street at Blair House. Over two evenings, their talk kept returning to the 1930s, when the democracies had let one small aggression follow another until the world was at war. Truman saw a test of the policy he had just set out. If the United States did nothing, he thought, other aggressors would learn that force pays. Within a week he had ordered American air and naval forces into the fight, and then ground troops.

He sent American forces, and he did it in a way that raises a question for a republic. He never asked Congress to declare war. Congress voted the money and extended the draft, but it never voted on the war itself. Truman called the conflict a police actionpuh-LEESE AK-shunTruman's name for the Korean War, which was fought for the United Nations without a declaration of war by Congress. carried out for the United Nations. The Security Council had voted to help South Korea, and in the end sixteen nations sent troops, though the United States and South Korea carried most of the fighting. The Soviet Union, which could have vetoed that decision at the Security Council, had been boycotting the council and was not in the room. Contingency matters here: if the Soviet delegate had been at his seat, there would have been no United Nations force.

The first months were a disaster. Poorly trained American troops, many from occupation duty in Japan, were pushed back into a small corner of the southeast. The first unit to fight, about 540 men known as Task Force Smith, met the North Korean tanks near Osan on July 5. Their weapons could not stop the armor, and within hours the survivors were in retreat. Through July the retreat went on, until General Walton Walker's Eighth Army made a stand around the port of Pusan. The line there bent but held through August and early September. Every mile the North Koreans had advanced stretched their supply lines thinner, and American aircraft hit those lines by day.

Then, in September, General Douglas MacArthur took a gamble. He landed Marines far behind the enemy lines at the port of Inchon, and the Inchon landingIN-chon LAND-ingThe September 1950 surprise landing of Marines behind North Korean lines that turned the tide of the Korean War. broke the North Korean army. Most naval planners thought the place a terrible choice. The channel was narrow, the harbor was ringed with mud flats and a sea wall, and the tide rose and fell as much as thirty feet, so ships could reach the beach on only a few days each month. MacArthur argued that those very problems meant the enemy would never expect an attack there. The Marines went ashore on September 15, and by the end of the month Seoul was back in South Korean hands. Success changed the goal. Instead of simply driving the invaders out, the United Nations now backed an effort to reunite all of Korea. By October, UN troops had crossed the 38th parallel and were racing north. In mid-October Truman flew across the Pacific to Wake Island to meet the general, the only time the two men ever met. He asked whether China or the Soviet Union might enter the war. MacArthur answered that the chance was small, and that Chinese troops who tried it would be slaughtered. He promised that the boys would be home by Christmas.

China had other plans. Its leaders had warned that they would not allow an American army at their border on the Yalu River. In early October, the Chinese foreign minister, Zhou Enlai, even sent word through India's ambassador that China would fight if American troops crossed the parallel. Washington did not believe it. MacArthur doubted that they would fight. Yet Chinese soldiers were already crossing the Yalu in secret, marching at night and hiding by day. In late November 1950, hundreds of thousands of Chinese soldiers attacked, and the UN forces were thrown back in the longest retreat in American military history. In the northeast, Marines and soldiers around the Chosin Reservoir were surrounded in weather far below zero. They fought their way out to the coast in December, carrying their wounded. Seoul fell again in January. By early 1951, the front line had settled near the 38th parallel, and the war had become a bloody stalemate.

The general who steadied the army was Matthew Ridgway. He had taken over the Eighth Army after Walker died in a jeep accident that December, and by March his troops had retaken Seoul. MacArthur wanted to widen the war. He asked to bomb Chinese bases north of the Yalu and to use Chinese Nationalist troops. Truman and his advisers disagreed. They feared that a wider war with China might bring in the Soviet Union and turn Korea into a third world war. China and the Soviet Union had signed a treaty of alliance in 1950, and American leaders thought the greater danger lay in Europe, where Soviet armies faced thin NATO forces. They preferred a limited warLIM-ih-tid worA war fought for a narrow goal, with limits on the weapons used and the places attacked., which is a war fought for a narrow goal, with restraints on the weapons used and the places attacked. In March 1951, as Truman prepared to offer the enemy a chance to talk, MacArthur issued a statement of his own that threatened China, and Truman dropped the plan. MacArthur argued that there was "no substitute for victory." Then he went further and criticized the president's policy in public, and wrote a letter to Joseph Martin, the Republican leader of the House, who read it aloud on the House floor. On April 11, 1951, Truman fired him.

He did not do it in a rage. For several days he talked it over with George Marshall, his secretary of defense, with Bradley, with Acheson, and with the diplomat Averell Harriman. The Joint Chiefs agreed that the general had to go. The plan was to have an Army official hand MacArthur the order in Tokyo, but word leaked to the press, and Truman moved up the announcement to a news conference held in the middle of the night. MacArthur, who had run the occupation of Japan since 1945, heard the news on the radio. Ridgway took his place. Truman's reason, as he later put it, was that the general would not respect the authority of the president.

The dispute touched a basic rule of the Constitution. The president is commander in chief, and the military serves the civilian government. MacArthur was a national hero, and the public was furious at Truman at first. The general had not set foot in the United States for fourteen years, and when he came home, crowds met him in San Francisco and New York. But the Senate's hearings, which ran for weeks that spring, showed that MacArthur's own fellow generals backed the president's policy. Omar Bradley, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, said that widening the war would involve the nation "in the wrong war, at the wrong place, at the wrong time." Many historians see this as a test the republic passed: a popular general lost an argument with an elected president, and no one tried to override the result. MacArthur gave a farewell speech to Congress, which you will read this week, and then he left public life.

The fighting continued for two more years while peace talks dragged on. The talks began in July 1951 at Kaesong and later moved to the village of Panmunjom. Meanwhile soldiers died for hills with names like Heartbreak Ridge, and the lines hardly moved. The sticking point was what to do with prisoners of war who did not want to go home. The communist side demanded that every prisoner be sent back, and the UN refused to force men to return. In the fall of 1952, Dwight Eisenhower, running for president, promised to go to Korea, and he went in December. When he took office in 1953, he let it be known that he might use stronger measures. Stalin died in March 1953, and by June the two sides had agreed on how to handle the prisoners. An armistice was signed on July 27, 1953. South Korea's president, Syngman Rhee, who wanted to fight on to unite the country, refused to sign. The border stayed close to where it had been at the start, with a buffer strip two and a half miles wide. About 36,000 Americans had died, along with many more Koreans and Chinese. No peace treaty was signed, and the border is still one of the most heavily armed in the world.

Fear of communism shaped life at home as well. The FBI looked into employees' records, and a loyalty board could dismiss a worker on the word of informants whom the worker was not always allowed to face. The House Un-American Activities Committeehows un-uh-MAIR-ih-kun ak-TIV-ih-teez kuh-MIT-eeA House committee that held public hearings on communist influence in the 1940s and 1950s. It is often called HUAC., known as HUAC, held public hearings on communist influence in government and in Hollywood. In 1947, ten writers and directors, who became known as the Hollywood Ten, refused to answer its questions about their politics. They were convicted of contempt of Congress and sent to prison, and the studios would not hire them again. Soon others in the film business were quietly blacklisted too. Some of the fear had a real basis. The Soviet Union did run spies in the United States, as records opened later show.

In 1948, a former communist named Whittaker Chambers, by then an editor at Time magazine, accused a former State Department official, Alger HissAL-jer hissA former State Department official accused of spying for the Soviets. Chambers then led investigators to a hollowed-out pumpkin on his Maryland farm, where he had hidden rolls of microfilm, and the newspapers called them the Pumpkin Papers. Richard Nixon, a young Californian on the committee, pressed the case hard, and it made his name. One trial ended with a jury that could not agree. At the second, Hiss was convicted of lying under oath about it in 1950. The charge was perjury and not spying, because the time limit for a spying charge had run out.

Atomic spying brought the next shock. In early 1950, a British physicist named Klaus Fuchs confessed that he had passed atomic secrets to the Soviets. Investigators followed the trail through an American courier, Harry Gold, to David Greenglass, a machinist who had worked at the Los Alamos laboratory. Greenglass named his sister Ethel and her husband Julius. In 1951, Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were convicted of passing atomic secrets to the Soviets, and the judge sentenced both to death. They were executed in June 1953, leaving two young sons. The RosenbergsROH-zen-burgzJulius and Ethel Rosenberg, a married couple convicted in 1951 of passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union. They were executed in 1953. case divided Americans then and still does, though later evidence showed that Julius at least was guilty.

Then a Wisconsin senator named Joseph McCarthyJOH-zef muh-KAR-theeA Wisconsin senator who made unproven charges of communism against many Americans in the early 1950s. turned the fear into a crusade. In February 1950, he claimed in a speech to a Republican women's club in Wheeling, West Virginia, that he had a list of communists working in the State Department. The number he gave changed from one speech to the next, and he never produced a convincing list. Newspapers printed his charges as he made them, and the replies, when they came, ran later and smaller. After the elections of 1952, he became chairman of a Senate subcommittee with the power to summon witnesses, and he kept making charges against more and more people. The term McCarthyismmuh-KAR-thee-iz-umThe practice of accusing people of disloyalty without proof. came to mean accusing people of disloyalty without proof. Careers were destroyed, and many Americans learned to keep their opinions quiet. Few politicians dared to oppose him. Even Congress was swept along. In 1950 it passed a law requiring communist organizations to register with the government. Truman called the law a danger to free speech and vetoed it, but Congress passed it over his veto.

One who did oppose him was Senator Margaret Chase Smith of Maine, a Republican, who gave a "Declaration of Conscience" speech on the Senate floor in June 1950. It took nerve, because McCarthy belonged to her own party and had shown what he could do to his critics. She did not name McCarthy, but everyone knew who she meant. She said that the right to criticize and to hold unpopular beliefs was part of what made America different. Six other Republican senators signed on with her. The real turn came in 1954. In March, the CBS reporter Edward R. Murrow aired a program built from film of McCarthy's own speeches and questioning. Then McCarthy attacked the Army on live television. The Army answered that his staff had pressed for special treatment for a drafted aide, and the hearings, which ran for weeks, showed him as a bully. In one famous moment, when McCarthy went after a young lawyer from the firm of the Army's counsel, Joseph Welch asked him whether he had no sense of decency left, and the room applauded. The Senate voted to censure him that December, by 67 to 22. He died in 1957.

The lesson of the period is hard, because both fear and carelessness did harm. A free republic has to protect itself from real spies. It also has to protect the rights of its people, including the right to be wrong. The great test of a republic's health, according to the philosopher Hegel, is how it handles war. A second test is how it handles fear. In the 1950s, the United States did well on the first and, in the case of McCarthy, only partly on the second. The next lesson turns from the fear of the 1950s to its prosperity.
