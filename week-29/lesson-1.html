📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On June 25, 1950, North Korean tanks crossed the 38th parallelTHUR-tee-ayth PAIR-uh-lelThe line of latitude that divided Korea into a communist north and an American-backed south., the line that had split Korea into a communist north and an American-backed south since 1945. Within days they had taken the southern capital of Seoul. Truman saw a test of the policy he had just set out. If the United States did nothing, he thought, other aggressors would learn that force pays. He sent American forces, and he did it in a way that raises a question for a republic. He never asked Congress to declare war. Truman called the conflict a police actionpuh-LEESE AK-shunTruman's name for the Korean War, which was fought for the United Nations without a declaration of war by Congress. carried out for the United Nations. The Soviet Union, which could have vetoed that decision at the Security Council, had been boycotting the council and was not in the room. Contingency matters here: if the Soviet delegate had been at his seat, there would have been no United Nations force.

The first months were a disaster. Poorly trained American troops, many from occupation duty in Japan, were pushed back into a small corner of the southeast. Then, in September, General Douglas MacArthur took a gamble. He landed Marines far behind the enemy lines at the port of Inchon, and the Inchon landingIN-chon LAND-ingThe September 1950 surprise landing of Marines behind North Korean lines that turned the tide of the Korean War. broke the North Korean army. By October, UN troops had crossed the 38th parallel and were racing north. MacArthur promised that the boys would be home by Christmas.

China had other plans. Its leaders had warned that they would not allow an American army at their border on the Yalu River. MacArthur doubted that they would fight. In late November 1950, hundreds of thousands of Chinese soldiers attacked, and the UN forces were thrown back in the longest retreat in American military history. By early 1951, the front line had settled near the 38th parallel, and the war had become a bloody stalemate.

MacArthur wanted to widen the war. He asked to bomb Chinese bases north of the Yalu and to use Chinese Nationalist troops. Truman and his advisers disagreed. They feared that a wider war with China might bring in the Soviet Union and turn Korea into a third world war. They preferred a limited warLIM-ih-tid worA war fought for a narrow goal, with limits on the weapons used and the places attacked., which is a war fought for a narrow goal, with restraints on the weapons used and the places attacked. MacArthur argued that there was "no substitute for victory." Then he went further and criticized the president's policy in public, and wrote a letter that a congressman read aloud in the House. On April 11, 1951, Truman fired him.

The dispute touched a basic rule of the Constitution. The president is commander in chief, and the military serves the civilian government. MacArthur was a national hero, and the public was furious at Truman at first. But the Senate's hearings showed that MacArthur's own fellow generals backed the president's policy. Omar Bradley, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, said that widening the war would involve the nation "in the wrong war, at the wrong place, at the wrong time." Many historians see this as a test the republic passed: a popular general lost an argument with an elected president, and no one tried to override the result. MacArthur gave a farewell speech to Congress, which you will read this week, and then he left public life.

The fighting continued for two more years while peace talks dragged on. The sticking point was what to do with prisoners of war who did not want to go home. When Dwight Eisenhower took office in 1953, he let it be known that he might use stronger measures, and an armistice was signed on July 27, 1953. The border stayed close to where it had been at the start. About 36,000 Americans had died, along with many more Koreans and Chinese. No peace treaty was signed, and the border is still one of the most heavily armed in the world.

Then a Wisconsin senator named Joseph McCarthyJOH-zef muh-KAR-theeA Wisconsin senator who made unproven charges of communism against many Americans in the early 1950s. turned the fear into a crusade. In February 1950, he claimed in a speech that he had a list of communists working in the State Department. He never produced a convincing list. He kept making charges against more and more people, and the term McCarthyismmuh-KAR-thee-iz-umThe practice of accusing people of disloyalty without proof. came to mean accusing people of disloyalty without proof. Careers were destroyed, and many Americans learned to keep their opinions quiet. Few politicians dared to oppose him.

One who did was Senator Margaret Chase Smith of Maine, a Republican, who gave a "Declaration of Conscience" speech on the Senate floor in June 1950. She did not name McCarthy, but everyone knew who she meant. She said that the right to criticize and to hold unpopular beliefs was part of what made America different. Six other Republican senators signed on with her. The real turn came in 1954, when McCarthy attacked the Army on live television. The hearings showed him as a bully, and the Senate voted to censure him that December, by 67 to 22. He died in 1957.

The lesson of the period is hard, because both fear and carelessness did harm. A free republic has to protect itself from real spies. It also has to protect the rights of its people, including the right to be wrong. The great test of a republic's health, according to the philosopher Hegel, is how it handles war. A second test is how it handles fear. In the 1950s, the United States did well on the first and, in the case of McCarthy, only partly on the second. The next lesson turns from the fear of the 1950s to its prosperity.
