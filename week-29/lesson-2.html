📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Many Americans who lived through the Depression and the war expected hard times to return when the fighting stopped. They did not. Between 1945 and 1960, the United States entered the greatest period of widespread prosperity that any large nation had ever seen. Wages rose, jobs were plentiful, and the average family had far more money than its parents had ever had. A family that had crowded into a city apartment in 1940 might now own a house, a car, and a refrigerator. In 1958, the economist John Kenneth Galbraith wrote a book about it and called it The Affluent Society. Most Americans would have agreed with the title. Galbraith himself was uneasy, though. He pointed out that families were buying more and more for themselves while public schools, parks, and roads stayed shabby.

Several causes worked together. The country had been spared the bombing that wrecked Europe and Asia, so its factories were intact, and the world needed what they made. Families had saved money during the war, when there was little to buy, and now they wanted to spend it. No new cars for civilians had rolled off the assembly lines from 1942 to 1945, and refrigerators and washing machines had been just as scarce, so a long line of buyers was waiting. The GI Bill, which you met earlier in this course, sent millions of veterans to college and helped them buy homes. Strong unions pushed wages up in the steel, auto, and other industries, and by the middle of the decade about one worker in three belonged to a union. In 1950, the United Auto Workers and General Motors signed a five-year contract that tied raises to the cost of living and added pensions and health insurance. A magazine called it the Treaty of Detroit, and other companies soon followed its example. The government also spent heavily on defense, more than half of the federal budget in most years of the 1950s. The Cold War, in other words, was one of the engines of the economy.

Nothing shows the change better than housing. After years of Depression and war, the country was short of homes. A builder named William Levitt found a way to build them fast. On potato fields on Long Island, he used the assembly-line ideas of Henry Ford. Crews moved from lot to lot, and each did one job: laying foundations, framing, roofing, or painting. At LevittownLEV-it-townA planned suburb on Long Island, built quickly with assembly-line methods, that became the model for postwar suburbs., a house could be finished in a day. The homes were small and nearly identical, but a veteran could buy one for about seven thousand dollars, with little money down. The loan rules made that possible. Before the New Deal, most home loans ran for only a few years and ended with one huge payment that many families could not make. The Federal Housing Administration, created in 1934, backed loans that ran for thirty years with the same payment each month, and the GI Bill promised to repay part of a veteran's loan if he could not. Banks were willing to lend, and a monthly payment could be lower than the rent on a city apartment. Levitt later built similar towns in Pennsylvania and New Jersey, and other builders copied his methods across the country. Levittown and places like it spread, and the suburbsSUB-urbzResidential communities on the edge of a city., which are residential communities on the edge of a city, became the new American neighborhood. By 1960, more Americans lived in suburbs than in either cities or on farms. Homeownership rose from under 45 percent in 1940 to over 60 percent in 1960.

What might the postwar boom have looked like if the government had cut defense spending sharply after the war? Decide first and jot down two effects before you open this.
The lesson names several other causes: factories the war had spared, savings that families wanted to spend, help for veterans, and strong unions. So the boom might still have happened, but with fewer jobs in defense factories and a different mix of industries. This is reasoning from the lesson, not a certain result.

Families grew too. Couples who had delayed marriage during the war now married young and had children in large numbers. The average bride was about twenty years old. The result was the baby boomBAY-bee boomThe sharp rise in births in the United States from about 1946 to 1964., a sharp rise in births that lasted from about 1946 to 1964. About seventy-six million Americans were born in those years. The peak came in 1957, with more than four million births in that one year. Parents turned to Dr. Benjamin Spock's book on baby care, which had first appeared in 1946 and sold in the millions, and which told mothers to trust their own good sense. Schools had to be built by the thousands, and companies saw a giant new market.

Americans bought things as never before. Most families owned a car, and by the end of the decade, most had a television. In 1950, fewer than one home in ten had a set; ten years later, nearly nine in ten did. Shows like I Love Lucy and Father Knows Best showed a national audience a certain picture of the good life. When Lucille Ball's character had a baby on her show in January 1953, more people tuned in than watched Eisenhower take the oath of office the next day. Food changed to fit the new habits, and in 1953 Swanson began selling frozen TV dinners. Advertising told people what they needed. Credit cards appeared, and one of the first, from the Diners Club, was meant for restaurant meals. Shopping centers spread. In 1955, a salesman named Ray Kroc opened a hamburger restaurant in Illinois that grew into the McDonald's chain, and Disneyland opened in California that summer.

Many people felt pressure to look and act as their neighbors did, and critics complained about conformitykun-FOR-mih-teeThe habit of going along with the group instead of thinking and acting for yourself., the habit of going along with the group instead of thinking and acting for yourself. Popular books such as The Man in the Gray Flannel Suit and The Organization Man described the men who rode the train each morning, in matching suits, to jobs in large companies. Religion also grew in public life, and preachers such as Billy Graham filled stadiums. Congress added "under God" to the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954, and Eisenhower signed the change on Flag Day. In 1956, "In God We Trust" became the national motto, partly to stress the difference from atheistic communism.

The Cold War reached all the way into the kitchen. In July 1959, Vice President Richard Nixon went to Moscow to open an American exhibition that included a full-size model of a suburban house. In the kitchen of that house, with reporters crowding around, he and the Soviet leader, Nikita Khrushchev, began to argue. Nixon pointed to the washing machine and the many goods an ordinary American worker could choose to buy. Khrushchev scoffed and said that the Soviet Union would soon catch up and pass the United States. The newspapers called it the Kitchen Debate. Each man was claiming that his country's way of life left ordinary people better off.

The car shaped the country. In 1956, Eisenhower signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act, which launched the Interstate Highway SystemIN-ter-stayt HY-way SIS-tumThe national network of high-speed roads begun by the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956., a national network of high-speed roads. Its stated purposes were safety, trade, and national defense, since the roads would allow troops and supplies to move quickly if war came. The law promised about forty thousand miles of road and set aside the money, paid by a tax on gasoline. The federal government paid ninety percent of the cost, and the states paid the rest. Eisenhower had a personal reason to care. In 1919, as a young Army officer, he had joined a truck convoy that took two months to cross the country on roads that were often mud. As a general in 1945, he had seen what Germany's superhighways could do. The highways tied the nation together. They also made it easy to live far from work, and they cut through many city neighborhoods, usually poor ones. Shoppers followed commuters to the edge of town, and many railroads and streetcar lines lost the riders they needed to survive.

Eisenhower himself fit the mood of the time. He had commanded the largest invasion in history, and he was a calm and popular president, who won a second term in 1956 by a wide margin over the same opponent, Adlai Stevenson, that he had beaten four years earlier. He kept most New Deal programs, and in 1954 he signed a law that brought millions more workers under Social Security. He balanced the budget when he could, and he held back military spending against the demands of his own generals, preferring to lean on nuclear weapons, which cost less than large armies. On the evening of January 17, 1961, three days before he left office, he went on radio and television with a farewell address. He reminded Americans that before the world wars the country had no permanent arms industry, and that now about three and a half million people worked in defense. He warned that a large permanent arms industry had grown up alongside the military. He called it the military-industrial complexMIL-ih-tair-ee in-DUS-tree-ul KOM-pleksEisenhower's name for the close ties between the armed forces and the industries that make weapons.. "We must never let the weight of this combination endanger our liberties," he said. Here was a general warning a republic about the effects of its own armed strength, a concern with deep roots in the founders' thinking.

⚡ PUT IT IN ORDER
Put these events in order, from earliest to latest. Tap them one at a time.

    In October 1957, the Soviet Union launched SputnikSPUT-nikThe first artificial satellite, launched by the Soviet Union in October 1957., the first artificial satellite, into orbit. Americans were stunned. It was a metal ball about the size of a beach ball, and it did nothing but circle the earth and send out a steady beeping signal. Radio fans across the country tuned in and listened, and many people went out at dusk to look for it. The beeps lasted about three weeks, until its batteries died. A month after the launch the Soviets sent up a second satellite, this one carrying a dog. If the Soviets could put a satellite in space, they might be able to place a nuclear weapon on a rocket aimed at the United States, and in August they had announced a successful test of just such a long-range rocket. Eisenhower tried to stay calm. Then, in December, the Navy's Vanguard rocket rose a few feet off its pad before a live television audience and blew up. Not until January 1958 did the United States put a satellite into orbit of its own.

    Many blamed American schools. Congress responded in 1958 with the National Defense Education ActNASH-uh-nul dih-FENSE ej-oo-KAY-shun aktThe 1958 law that gave federal money for science, math, and language teaching and for student loans., which gave money for science, math, and foreign language teaching, and for student loans. NASA was founded that same year. So Sputnik, another contingent event, pushed the federal government into education in a way it had never been before.

    Not everyone shared in the prosperity. Black families were shut out of much of it. Levittown at first refused to sell to Black buyers, and federal lending practices often favored white neighborhoods and marked Black ones as risky. In 1948 the Supreme Court had ruled that courts could not enforce deeds that barred Black buyers, yet builders, agents, and banks found other ways to keep neighborhoods white. Federal appraisers had drawn maps that rated neighborhoods by risk, and a street where Black families lived was often marked as the worst, so banks refused to lend there. Many Black veterans who tried to use the GI Bill for a home loan or for tuition found local banks and colleges turning them away, especially in the South. Many Black workers stayed in the lowest-paid jobs.

    In the countryside, farm families in Appalachia and the South stayed poor. In 1962, Michael Harrington wrote a book called The Other America, arguing that tens of millions of Americans were poor and mostly unseen, hidden from the middle class by the suburbs and the highways. In 1960, roughly one in five Americans lived in poverty. Women, too, found their choices narrower than the television image suggested, since most of the better-paying jobs went to men. By 1960, nearly two women in five held a paying job, usually as a secretary, teacher, nurse, or clerk, and they were often paid less than men for similar work.

    How widely was the postwar prosperity shared?

    So the affluent society was real, and it was unequal. By the standards of the first-order economic problem, it was a stunning success: production grew, and most families shared in it. By another standard, that of a common life in which every citizen has a fair chance, the picture was mixed. In the next module, you will see how Americans who were left out did not wait quietly. They went to court, to the streets, and to the ballot box.
