📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By early 1947, American leaders needed a plan. They did not want another world war, and they did not want to hand Europe to Stalin. They also could not keep fighting with troops they had already sent home. The plan they chose came from a quiet diplomat named George F. Kennanjorj eff KEN-unAn American diplomat in Moscow whose 1946 cable and 1947 article shaped the strategy of containment.. Kennan had spent years in Moscow, spoke Russian, and had read the country's history and its writers. In February 1946, he sent the State Department a very long cable, which you will read this week. It began with a small question. The Treasury Department had asked the embassy why the Soviets had refused to join the new world financial institutions. Kennan, who was running the embassy while the ambassador was away, answered with far more than anyone expected. Some weeks earlier, Stalin had given a speech in which he said that the capitalist system made wars likely, and Kennan's cable landed in a Washington ready to listen. The Secretary of the Navy, James Forrestal, had copies made and passed them around the government. He argued that Soviet leaders, driven by an old Russian sense of insecurity as well as by communist belief, believed they were in a permanent struggle with the capitalist world, and that they would push forward wherever nobody stopped them. But he also believed that they were cautious, and that they would back away when they met firm resistance.

In July 1947, Kennan published his argument in a magazine under the signature "X." He said that the United States should follow a policy of "long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containmentkun-TAYN-mentThe American strategy of holding back the spread of Soviet power without starting a war." of Soviet expansion. He wrote that the Americans should meet Soviet pressure with firm counterpressure at one point after another, wherever it appeared. The article appeared in the magazine Foreign Affairs. Kennan signed it with the letter X because he was a government official, but within weeks everyone in Washington knew who the author was. Containment did not mean rolling back communism where it already ruled, and it did not mean war. The Americans would not try to free the countries Stalin already held. They would try to stop him from taking more. It meant holding the line, and waiting. Kennan thought that a system built on fear and force would eventually weaken from the inside. He was right, though it took more than forty years.

The first test came in Greece and Turkey. Britain had been helping the Greek government fight a communist rebellion, and Turkey was under Soviet pressure, since the Soviets wanted a share in controlling the narrow straits that link the Black Sea to the Mediterranean. In Greece, the rebels fought from the mountains of the north. In February 1947, after a bitter winter that had strained the British economy to its limit, the British told Washington that they were too poor to continue. Dean Acheson, an Under Secretary of State, took the problem to congressional leaders in a meeting with the president. He warned that if Greece fell, the trouble could spread from country to country, as one rotten apple spoils a barrel. Truman acted. On March 12, 1947, he asked Congress for four hundred million dollars in aid and made a larger promise: that the United States would "support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures." This promise became the Truman DoctrineTROO-mun DOK-trinPresident Truman's 1947 promise to support free peoples resisting takeover by armed minorities or outside pressure.. Senator Arthur Vandenberg, a leading Republican and former isolationist, told Truman that to win Congress he would have to "scare hell out of the American people." Truman did, and Congress passed the aid in May, by wide margins. That mattered, because the new Republican Congress, elected in 1946, had promised to cut spending.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST
In February 1947, Britain told Washington that it was too poor to keep helping Greece, and Turkey was under Soviet pressure.
What might have happened if the United States had said no? Decide first and jot down two possible results before you open this.
The Greek government would have been fighting a rebellion without its main helper, and Turkey would have faced Soviet pressure alone. Other results were possible too, and we cannot know how things would have turned out. This is reasoning from what we know, not a certain result.

Aid to two countries was only the beginning. Europe was hungry, cold, and broke, and many Europeans were voting for communists in elections. Secretary of State George Marshall, the general who had directed the army's war effort, announced a much bigger idea in a Harvard speech in June 1947. The United States would help pay for the rebuilding of Europe, if Europeans would plan it together. The speech was short, and it did not name the Soviet Union. Britain's foreign secretary, Ernest Bevin, heard about it on the radio and moved at once, together with France, to organize a reply. The Europeans met in Paris to draw up their needs, and the Soviet foreign minister, Molotov, came and then walked out of the talks. Sixteen nations eventually took part. The Marshall PlanMAR-shul planThe American program, begun in 1948, that gave about thirteen billion dollars to help rebuild the economies of Western Europe. provided about thirteen billion dollars between 1948 and 1952, mostly as grants. Britain, France, and West Germany received the most. Much of the money was spent on American food, fuel, and machinery, which helped American farmers and factories too. Congress hesitated until the communist takeover in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 convinced many members to vote yes. Stalin refused to take part and forced the countries he controlled to refuse too. The Marshall Plan worked. Western European factories started up again, trade grew, and communist parties lost ground. A Marshall Plan office, led by a businessman named Paul Hoffman, decided how the money was divided and spent. It was an unusual moment in history: the strongest country in the world used its wealth to rebuild its former enemies as well as its friends.

At home, Congress reorganized the government for a long struggle. The National Security ActNASH-uh-nul sih-KYOOR-ih-tee aktThe 1947 law that created the Department of Defense, the National Security Council, and the CIA. of 1947 created the Department of Defense to unify the armed forces, a National Security Council, headed by the president and made up of his top foreign policy and defense officials, to advise him, and the Central Intelligence AgencySEN-trul in-TEL-uh-jents AY-jen-seeThe U.S. agency created in 1947 to gather and study information from other countries. It is often called the CIA. to gather information abroad. The same law made the Air Force a separate service, and it gave the CIA no police powers at home. A republic had never kept this kind of permanent machinery in peacetime, and some Americans worried about what it meant for liberty.

The next test was Berlin. The city lay more than a hundred miles inside the Soviet zone of Germany, but it was divided into four sectors, and the Western powers held three of them. In June 1948, the Western zones began to share a new currency, and four days later Stalin cut off every road, rail line, and canal into West Berlin. About two million people in the city had food for only a few weeks. The Soviets offered food to West Berliners who would register in the Soviet sector, but very few did. General Lucius Clay, the American commander in Germany, wanted to stand firm, and he argued that if the West gave up Berlin, western Germany would be next. Truman rejected a withdrawal, and he rejected an attempt to force a road through with troops, which might have started a war.

Instead, American and British pilots flew supplies in. The Western powers had an agreement allowing them to fly through three air corridors, and the Soviets never shot at the planes. The planes carried food and, as winter came, coal. The Berlin Airliftber-LIN AIR-liftThe American and British effort in 1948 and 1949 to supply West Berlin by air after the Soviet Union cut off all roads, rails, and canals into the city. lasted for more than ten months, and at its height a plane landed in Berlin about every minute. In all, the airlift took more than a quarter of a million flights. One American pilot, Lieutenant Gail Halvorsen, began dropping handkerchief parachutes with candy for the city's children, whose nickname for the planes was "raisin bombers," and soon schoolchildren in the United States were mailing him sweets to drop. Many of the people being fed had been enemies of the United States only three years earlier. In May 1949, Stalin gave up the blockade, though the planes kept flying until September to build up a stock of supplies in case it began again. It was a victory for the policy: the West had held its ground without firing a shot.

The Berlin crisis also pushed Americans to take a step that George Washington had warned against. In April 1949, the United States joined eleven other countries, including Canada, Britain, France, and Italy, in the North Atlantic Treaty. The treaty was signed in Washington on April 4, 1949. The Europeans were asking for it. In 1948, Britain, France, and three small neighbors had signed a defense pact of their own, and they urged Washington to join. The treaty created NATONAY-tohThe North Atlantic Treaty Organization, a military alliance formed in 1949 in which an attack on one member is treated as an attack on all., the North Atlantic Treaty Organization. Its key promise was that an attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all. Each member would decide for itself what action to take, a point meant to reassure senators that Congress would still have its say about war. A year earlier, the Senate had paved the way with a resolution by Vandenberg that favored joining such alliances. The Senate approved it in July 1949, by 82 to 13. It was the first time since the French alliance of 1778 that the United States had made a binding military alliance in peacetime. Supporters argued that war was more likely without the alliance. Opponents, including Senator Robert Taft of Ohio, argued that it risked dragging the country into wars that Congress had not chosen.

Late in 1949, two shocks hit within weeks of each other. In August, the Soviet Union tested its own atomic bomb, years before most experts had expected. An American plane collecting air samples over the Pacific found traces of radioactivity, and scientists worked out that the Soviets had set off a bomb. On September 23, Truman told the American people. For four years, the United States had been the only nation with the bomb, and many Americans had counted on that to keep them safe.

In October, the communists led by Mao Zedongmow dzuh-DONGThe communist leader who won China's civil war in 1949 and ruled the country until 1976. won the civil war in China, which meant that the world's most populous country was now communist. China had fought on the American side in the war, and many Americans thought of it as a friend they had helped. Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China in Beijing on October 1, and the defeated Nationalist government under Chiang Kai-shek fled to the island of Taiwan. Chiang's government had lost the trust of many Chinese as prices soared and corruption spread, even though the United States had sent it large amounts of aid. Many Americans asked who had "lost" China, and some critics in Congress blamed the administration for not doing more to help Chiang. The truth was that the United States had never had the power to decide the outcome, but the question became a political weapon at home.

Truman asked his advisers to rethink strategy. Their answer, in April 1950, was a secret paper called NSC-68en-es-see SIX-tee-aytA secret 1950 government paper that called for a large increase in American military spending to face the Soviet Union.. Paul Nitze, who now ran the State Department's planning office, wrote most of it. It said that the Soviet Union was determined to dominate the world, and that the United States should greatly increase its military spending. It said that the Soviet Union and its allies were building up their armed forces faster than the free world was, and that the gap had to be closed. Truman worried about the cost, and he asked for estimates instead of approving it. The paper was not made public for many years. Within three months of its being delivered, the Korean War began, and the argument for it seemed proven.

⚡ PUT IT IN ORDER
Put these events in order, from earliest to latest. Tap them one at a time.

    Was containment wise? Its defenders say that it solved the two first-order problems that every state faces. It protected the country against attack, and it did so without a third world war. It rebuilt the free economies of Europe, and those economies became rich trading partners. Its critics offer several arguments. Some say that the Soviet threat was overstated, and that the United States turned a rivalry into a permanent confrontation. Kennan himself later said that his ideas had been read as mostly military when he had meant them to be mostly political. Others say that containment gave the president too much power to act without Congress. You will weigh these views in this week's discussion. Notice, as you do, how many different outcomes were possible. If Truman had given up on Berlin, or if Congress had refused Greece, the map of Europe might look very different today.
