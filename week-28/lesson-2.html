📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By early 1947, American leaders needed a plan. They did not want another world war, and they did not want to hand Europe to Stalin. They also could not keep fighting with troops they had already sent home. The plan they chose came from a quiet diplomat named George F. Kennanjorj eff KEN-unAn American diplomat in Moscow whose 1946 cable and 1947 article shaped the strategy of containment.. Kennan had spent years in Moscow, and in February 1946 he sent the State Department a very long cable, which you will read this week. He argued that Soviet leaders believed they were in a permanent struggle with the capitalist world, and that they would push forward wherever nobody stopped them. But he also believed that they were cautious, and that they would back away when they met firm resistance.

In July 1947, Kennan published his argument in a magazine under the signature "X." He said that the United States should follow a policy of "long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containmentkun-TAYN-mentThe American strategy of holding back the spread of Soviet power without starting a war." of Soviet expansion. Containment did not mean rolling back communism where it already ruled, and it did not mean war. It meant holding the line, and waiting. Kennan thought that a system built on fear and force would eventually weaken from the inside. He was right, though it took more than forty years.

The first test came in Greece and Turkey. Britain had been helping the Greek government fight a communist rebellion, and Turkey was under Soviet pressure. In February 1947, the British told Washington that they were too poor to continue. Truman acted. On March 12, 1947, he asked Congress for four hundred million dollars in aid and made a larger promise: that the United States would "support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures." This promise became the Truman DoctrineTROO-mun DOK-trinPresident Truman's 1947 promise to support free peoples resisting takeover by armed minorities or outside pressure.. Senator Arthur Vandenberg, a leading Republican and former isolationist, told Truman that to win Congress he would have to "scare hell out of the American people." Truman did, and Congress passed the aid.

Aid to two countries was only the beginning. Europe was hungry, cold, and broke, and many Europeans were voting for communists in elections. Secretary of State George Marshall announced a much bigger idea in a Harvard speech in June 1947. The United States would help pay for the rebuilding of Europe, if Europeans would plan it together. The Marshall PlanMAR-shul planThe American program, begun in 1948, that gave about thirteen billion dollars to help rebuild the economies of Western Europe. provided about thirteen billion dollars between 1948 and 1952, mostly as grants. Stalin refused to take part and forced the countries he controlled to refuse too. The Marshall Plan worked. Western European factories started up again, trade grew, and communist parties lost ground. It was an unusual moment in history: the strongest country in the world used its wealth to rebuild its former enemies as well as its friends.

At home, Congress reorganized the government for a long struggle. The National Security ActNASH-uh-nul sih-KYOOR-ih-tee aktThe 1947 law that created the Department of Defense, the National Security Council, and the CIA. of 1947 created the Department of Defense to unify the armed forces, a National Security Council to advise the president, and the Central Intelligence AgencySEN-trul in-TEL-uh-jents AY-jen-seeThe U.S. agency created in 1947 to gather and study information from other countries. It is often called the CIA. to gather information abroad. A republic had never kept this kind of permanent machinery in peacetime, and some Americans worried about what it meant for liberty.

The next test was Berlin. The city lay deep in the Soviet zone of Germany, but it was divided into four sectors, and the Western powers held three of them. In June 1948, after the Western zones began to share a new currency, Stalin cut off every road, rail line, and canal into West Berlin. About two million people in the city had food for only a few weeks. Truman rejected a withdrawal, and he rejected an attempt to force a road through with troops, which might have started a war. Instead, American and British pilots flew supplies in. The Berlin Airliftber-LIN AIR-liftThe American and British effort in 1948 and 1949 to supply West Berlin by air after the Soviet Union cut off all roads, rails, and canals into the city. lasted for more than ten months, and at its height a plane landed in Berlin about every minute. In May 1949, Stalin gave up the blockade. It was a victory for the policy: the West had held its ground without firing a shot.

The Berlin crisis also pushed Americans to take a step that George Washington had warned against. In April 1949, the United States joined eleven other countries, including Canada, Britain, France, and Italy, in the North Atlantic Treaty. The treaty created NATONAY-tohThe North Atlantic Treaty Organization, a military alliance formed in 1949 in which an attack on one member is treated as an attack on all., the North Atlantic Treaty Organization. Its key promise was that an attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all. The Senate approved it in July 1949, by 82 to 13. It was the first time since the French alliance of 1778 that the United States had made a binding military alliance in peacetime. Supporters argued that war was more likely without the alliance. Opponents, including Senator Robert Taft, argued that it risked dragging the country into wars that Congress had not chosen.

Late in 1949, two shocks hit within weeks of each other. In August, the Soviet Union tested its own atomic bomb, years before most experts had expected. And in October, the communists led by Mao Zedongmow dzuh-DONGThe communist leader who won China's civil war in 1949 and ruled the country until 1976. won the civil war in China, which meant that the world's most populous country was now communist. Many Americans asked who had "lost" China. The truth was that the United States had never had the power to decide the outcome, but the question became a political weapon at home.

Truman asked his advisers to rethink strategy. Their answer, in April 1950, was a secret paper called NSC-68en-es-see SIX-tee-aytA secret 1950 government paper that called for a large increase in American military spending to face the Soviet Union.. It said that the Soviet Union was determined to dominate the world, and that the United States should greatly increase its military spending. The paper was not made public for many years. Within three months of its being delivered, the Korean War began, and the argument for it seemed proven.

Was containment wise? Its defenders say that it solved the two first-order problems that every state faces. It protected the country against attack, and it did so without a third world war. It rebuilt the free economies of Europe, and those economies became rich trading partners. Its critics offer several arguments. Some say that the Soviet threat was overstated, and that the United States turned a rivalry into a permanent confrontation. Kennan himself later said that his ideas had been read as mostly military when he had meant them to be mostly political. Others say that containment gave the president too much power to act without Congress. You will weigh these views in this week's discussion. Notice, as you do, how many different outcomes were possible. If Truman had given up on Berlin, or if Congress had refused Greece, the map of Europe might look very different today.
