The Reagan Years and the Soviet Squeeze

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By 1980, many Americans were tired. The 1970s had brought a lost war, a scandal, high inflation, long gas lines, and a hostage crisis in Iran. Fifty-two Americans were held for 444 days after Iranian students seized the embassy in Tehran. Jimmy Carter's attempt at a rescue failed in the desert, when a helicopter and a transport plane collided in the dark and eight American servicemen died. Voters wanted a change, and they found it in Ronald ReaganRON-uld RAY-gunThe 40th president, from 1981 to 1989. He was a former actor and governor of California.. Reagan was a former actor and a two-term governor of California. He had grown up in small towns in Illinois, worked as a radio announcer, and once voted for Franklin Roosevelt as a Democrat, before turning toward the Republican Party in the 1960s. He spoke in plain words, and he told Americans that their country was a "shining city on a hill," and that its best days were ahead. In a debate a week before the election, he asked viewers a simple question: were they better off than they had been four years earlier? Inflation was running at about thirteen percent, and families trying to buy a house were being quoted mortgage rates above fifteen percent. In November 1980, he won the presidency in a landslide. The hostages in Iran were freed on the day he took office.

Reagan's ideas were simple, and he repeated them often. Years in radio, film, and television had taught him how to talk to a crowd, and admirers and critics alike called him the Great Communicator. Government, he said, had grown too large and taxes too high. A strong military was needed to face the Soviets. And America should believe in itself. In his first inaugural address, he said, "Government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem." His economic program rested on supply-side economicssuh-PLY-side ek-uh-NOM-iksThe idea that cutting taxes gives people more reason to work, save, and invest, which will make the economy grow.. This is the idea that cutting taxes, especially on businesses and high earners, would give people more reason to work, save, and invest, and that growth would follow. In 1981, Congress cut income tax rates by about a quarter over three years, and it lowered the top rate from seventy percent to fifty. Supporters said that the economy would grow so much that the government would collect nearly as much money as before. Critics called it a trick that would only help the rich.

Reagan's good luck, and his skill, helped him in his first months. On March 30, 1981, a gunman shot him outside a Washington hotel. The bullet stopped about an inch from his heart, and he lost so much blood that he came close to dying. Reagan was badly hurt, but he joked with the doctors, and the country admired his calm. He told his wife that he had forgotten to duck, and he told the surgeons that he hoped they were all Republicans. With that goodwill, he won a large tax cut and cuts in domestic spending from a Congress controlled in part by Democrats. Democrats held the House, so he needed some of their votes, and in July he went on television to ask citizens to call and write their representatives. The calls and telegrams poured in. His success showed another part of his skill: he could make a deal. He often struck bargains with the House Speaker, Tip O'Neill, a Democrat, even while the two men fought bitterly in public. O'Neill liked to say that after six o'clock in the evening they were friends. In 1983 the two joined in rescuing Social Security, which was running short of money. The plan raised the retirement age very slowly and made some benefits taxable.

He could also be hard. In August 1981, the air traffic controllers, who worked for the federal government, went on strike for higher pay and shorter hours, although federal law forbade strikes by government workers. Reagan gave them forty-eight hours to return to work. Most did not, and about eleven thousand of them were fired. Supervisors and military controllers kept the airports running, and it took years to train new ones. Supporters said that he had shown that the law had to be obeyed. Unions said that it was an attack on working people, and many labor leaders never forgave him.

The early results were rough. The Federal Reserve, led by Paul VolckerPAWL VOLE-kerThe head of the Federal Reserve from 1979 to 1987. He raised interest rates sharply to bring down inflation., raised interest rates sharply to break inflation. Volcker, whom Carter had appointed in 1979, had the Fed squeeze the supply of money so tightly that banks were charging their best customers twenty percent. Few people could afford to borrow, so builders, car dealers, and farmers all suffered, and the economy fell into a deep recession in 1981 and 1982. Unemployment reached nearly ten percent. Republicans lost seats in the 1982 elections, but Reagan stood by Volcker. Yet inflation, which had been above thirteen percent, dropped to about four percent by 1983. Then a long recovery began. The economy grew for most of the decade, and millions of new jobs were created.

There were costs too. The deficit, the gap between what the government spends and what it collects, grew very large, because tax cuts were not matched by cuts in spending and the military was growing. Reagan agreed to some tax increases in 1982 and again later. In 1986, working with leaders of both parties, he signed a tax law that cut the top rate to twenty-eight percent and closed many loopholes. Even so, the national debt tripled in the decade, from about nine hundred billion dollars to nearly three trillion. Economists still argue about how much credit or blame belongs to Reagan, to Volcker, and to forces no president controls.

In foreign policy, Reagan rejected the quiet approach of détente. He believed the Soviet Union was a weak and cruel system that could be pressured into change, and he said so. He called for a huge buildup of the military, including new missiles, bombers, and warships. The Navy set a goal of six hundred ships. In March 1983, he gave a speech in Orlando, Florida, to a group of evangelical ministers in which he called the Soviet Union an "evil empire." You will read part of the speech this week. Critics at home called it reckless. Others replied that it put plain moral language to the conflict, as Reagan wished to do. He also kept a hope for a better path, and he said that he wanted to bargain from strength.

Later that month, Reagan announced a program that he hoped might end the nuclear threat. He had long been troubled that the only protection America had against a Soviet missile attack was the promise to destroy the attacker in return. He wanted a shield instead of a threat. The Strategic Defense Initiativestruh-TEE-jik dih-FENS ih-NISH-uh-tivReagan's 1983 plan for a system that would shoot down enemy missiles before they hit the United States. Critics nicknamed it "Star Wars.", which critics nicknamed "Star Wars," would use new technology, including lasers and satellites, to shoot down enemy missiles before they reached American soil. Scientists doubted that it could work, and it never did in Reagan's time. But the Soviets took it seriously. They feared that they could not afford to match it, and that it would upset the balance that had kept the peace. They also lagged far behind in computers, which would have to guide such a shield, and they knew it. Historians still disagree about whether the program was a bluff, a mistake, or a clever pressure on Moscow.

The world was dangerous in 1983. In September, a Soviet fighter shot down a Korean passenger jet, Flight 007, that had strayed into Soviet air space, and all 269 people aboard were killed, including a member of Congress. Reagan called it a massacre. In October, a truck bomb destroyed a barracks in Beirut, Lebanon, where American Marines had been sent as peacekeepers in a civil war, and 241 American service members died. Reagan pulled the Marines out a few months later. In the years that followed, militants in Lebanon began to kidnap Americans.

Reagan's approach to the wider Cold War became known as the Reagan DoctrineRAY-gun DOK-trinThe policy of helping anti-communist fighters in places such as Afghanistan and Nicaragua instead of only holding communism back.. Instead of just holding communism back, the United States would help anti-communist fighters in places like Afghanistan, Angola, and Nicaragua. In Afghanistan, which the Soviets had invaded in 1979, American money and missiles went to rebels who fought the Red Army for a decade. In 1986, the rebels began to receive Stinger missiles, which a single soldier could fire from his shoulder, and Soviet helicopters that had ruled the sky began to fall. Nearly fifteen thousand Soviet soldiers died, and far more Afghans. It bled the Soviets and gave them their own Vietnam. The last Soviet troops left in February 1989. In Nicaragua, a leftist government had come to power in 1979, and the United States backed rebels called the Contras. Congress, wary of another Vietnam, banned military aid to them. The ban was written into laws named for Representative Edward Boland of Massachusetts, and by 1984 it covered all American military help. This led to the biggest scandal of Reagan's presidency.

In the Iran-Contra affairih-RAHN KON-trah uh-FAIRA scandal in which Reagan's staff secretly sold weapons to Iran and used some of the money to help the Contra rebels in Nicaragua, against a law passed by Congress., members of the president's staff secretly sold weapons to Iran, a country the United States had labeled a supporter of terrorism, in hopes of freeing American hostages held in Lebanon. The sales were run by a Marine officer on the National Security Council staff, Oliver North, and approved by the national security adviser, John Poindexter. They then used part of the profits to pay for the Contras, in violation of the law that Congress had passed. A Lebanese magazine first reported the arms sales in November 1986, and when the whole story came out, Reagan's popularity fell. He first denied it and then admitted that "what began as a strategic opening to Iran deteriorated" into trading arms for hostages.

A commission that Reagan himself had appointed, led by Senator John Tower, said that he had run his staff too loosely and had not kept track of what they were doing. In the summer of 1987, Congress held televised hearings. North, in his Marine uniform, defended what he had done, and he admitted that his office had shredded documents. Some Americans saw a patriot, and others saw a man who had broken the law. Fourteen people were charged with crimes, and several were convicted, though some convictions were overturned or pardoned. Reagan was never shown to have known about the diversion of money. Still, the affair raised an old question: who is responsible when a president's staff acts outside the law? You will notice an echo of the last lesson.

By the mid-1980s, though, a more hopeful story was beginning. At home, the economy was strong, and Reagan won reelection in 1984 with a win in forty-nine states, with the slogan "Morning in America." His opponent, Walter Mondale, who had been Carter's vice president, carried only his home state of Minnesota and the District of Columbia. Mondale had chosen Geraldine Ferraro as his running mate, the first woman on a major party's national ticket. Abroad, the Soviet economy was failing. It had been weak for years, held back by central planning and by the cost of keeping up with the Americans. Money from oil, which the Soviets sold abroad, had kept the system afloat in the 1970s, when prices were high. In the mid-1980s, oil prices dropped sharply, from about thirty dollars a barrel to under fifteen, and the Soviet government lost billions. It was also bogged down in Afghanistan, and its people were losing faith in the system. The old men who ruled the country were dying one after another. Three leaders died in less than three years, the last in March 1985. A new leader was about to take power in Moscow, and he would change everything.

For a classical republican, the Reagan years offer lessons on both sides. Reagan's optimism, his clear purpose, and his skill at persuasion helped to restore the nation's confidence. Yet the large deficits, and the habit of taking quick fixes, suggested that the country was spending more than it earned. Contingency matters here too. The Cold War might have ended very differently if the Soviets had picked another leader or if the oil price had not collapsed. The next lesson takes up the story of how it ended.
