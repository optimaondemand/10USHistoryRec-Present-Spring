📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By 1980, many Americans were tired. The 1970s had brought a lost war, a scandal, high inflation, long gas lines, and a hostage crisis in Iran. Fifty-two Americans were held for 444 days after Iranian students seized the embassy in Tehran. Jimmy Carter's attempt at a rescue failed in the desert. Voters wanted a change, and they found it in Ronald ReaganRON-uld RAY-gunThe 40th president, from 1981 to 1989. He was a former actor and governor of California.. Reagan was a former actor and a two-term governor of California. He spoke in plain words, and he told Americans that their country was a "shining city on a hill," and that its best days were ahead. In November 1980, he won the presidency in a landslide.

Reagan's ideas were simple, and he repeated them often. Government, he said, had grown too large and taxes too high. A strong military was needed to face the Soviets. And America should believe in itself. In his first inaugural address, he said, "Government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem." His economic program rested on supply-side economicssuh-PLY-side ek-uh-NOM-iksThe idea that cutting taxes gives people more reason to work, save, and invest, which will make the economy grow.. This is the idea that cutting taxes, especially on businesses and high earners, would give people more reason to work, save, and invest, and that growth would follow. Supporters said that the economy would grow so much that the government would collect nearly as much money as before. Critics called it a trick that would only help the rich.

Reagan's good luck, and his skill, helped him in his first months. On March 30, 1981, a gunman shot him outside a Washington hotel. Reagan was badly hurt, but he joked with the doctors, and the country admired his calm. With that goodwill, he won a large tax cut and cuts in domestic spending from a Congress controlled in part by Democrats. His success showed another part of his skill: he could make a deal. He often struck bargains with the House Speaker, Tip O'Neill, a Democrat, even while the two men fought bitterly in public.

The early results were rough. The Federal Reserve, led by Paul VolckerPAWL VOLE-kerThe head of the Federal Reserve from 1979 to 1987. He raised interest rates sharply to bring down inflation., raised interest rates sharply to break inflation, and the economy fell into a deep recession in 1981 and 1982. Unemployment reached nearly ten percent. Yet inflation, which had been above thirteen percent, dropped to about four percent by 1983. Then a long recovery began. The economy grew for most of the decade, and millions of new jobs were created. There were costs too. The deficit, the gap between what the government spends and what it collects, grew very large, because tax cuts were not matched by cuts in spending and the military was growing. The national debt tripled in the decade. Economists still argue about how much credit or blame belongs to Reagan, to Volcker, and to forces no president controls.

In foreign policy, Reagan rejected the quiet approach of détente. He believed the Soviet Union was a weak and cruel system that could be pressured into change, and he said so. He called for a huge buildup of the military, including new missiles, bombers, and warships. In March 1983, he gave a speech to a group of evangelical ministers in which he called the Soviet Union an "evil empire." You will read part of the speech this week. Critics at home called it reckless. Others replied that it put plain moral language to the conflict, as Reagan wished to do. He also kept a hope for a better path, and he said that he wanted to bargain from strength.

Later that month, Reagan announced a program that he hoped might end the nuclear threat. The Strategic Defense Initiativestruh-TEE-jik dih-FENS ih-NISH-uh-tivReagan's 1983 plan for a system that would shoot down enemy missiles before they hit the United States. Critics nicknamed it "Star Wars.", which critics nicknamed "Star Wars," would use new technology to shoot down enemy missiles before they reached American soil. Scientists doubted that it could work, and it never did in Reagan's time. But the Soviets took it seriously. They feared that they could not afford to match it, and that it would upset the balance that had kept the peace. Historians still disagree about whether the program was a bluff, a mistake, or a clever pressure on Moscow.

Reagan's approach to the wider Cold War became known as the Reagan DoctrineRAY-gun DOK-trinThe policy of helping anti-communist fighters in places such as Afghanistan and Nicaragua instead of only holding communism back.. Instead of just holding communism back, the United States would help anti-communist fighters in places like Afghanistan, Angola, and Nicaragua. In Afghanistan, which the Soviets had invaded in 1979, American money and missiles went to rebels who fought the Red Army for a decade. It bled the Soviets and gave them their own Vietnam. In Nicaragua, a leftist government had come to power, and the United States backed rebels called the Contras. Congress, wary of another Vietnam, banned military aid to them. This led to the biggest scandal of Reagan's presidency.

In the Iran-Contra affairih-RAHN KON-trah uh-FAIRA scandal in which Reagan's staff secretly sold weapons to Iran and used some of the money to help the Contra rebels in Nicaragua, against a law passed by Congress., members of the president's staff secretly sold weapons to Iran, a country the United States had labeled a supporter of terrorism, in hopes of freeing American hostages held in Lebanon. They then used part of the profits to pay for the Contras, in violation of the law that Congress had passed. When it came out in late 1986, Reagan's popularity fell. He first denied it and then admitted that "what began as a strategic opening to Iran deteriorated" into trading arms for hostages. Fourteen people were charged with crimes, and several were convicted, though some convictions were overturned or pardoned. Reagan was never shown to have known about the diversion of money. Still, the affair raised an old question: who is responsible when a president's staff acts outside the law? You will notice an echo of the last lesson.

By the mid-1980s, though, a more hopeful story was beginning. At home, the economy was strong, and Reagan won reelection in 1984 with a win in forty-nine states, with the slogan "Morning in America." Abroad, the Soviet economy was failing. It had been weak for years, held back by central planning and by the cost of keeping up with the Americans. It was also bogged down in Afghanistan, and its people were losing faith in the system. A new leader was about to take power in Moscow, and he would change everything.

For a classical republican, the Reagan years offer lessons on both sides. Reagan's optimism, his clear purpose, and his skill at persuasion helped to restore the nation's confidence. Yet the large deficits, and the habit of taking quick fixes, suggested that the country was spending more than it earned. Contingency matters here too. The Cold War might have ended very differently if the Soviets had picked another leader or if the oil price had not collapsed. The next lesson takes up the story of how it ended.
