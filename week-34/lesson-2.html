📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In March 1985, the Soviet Politburo chose a new leader. Mikhail Gorbachevmih-KYLE GOR-buh-chofThe last leader of the Soviet Union, from 1985 to 1991. He tried to reform the system, and the reforms helped bring it down. was fifty-four, young for a Soviet ruler, and he was the first to have grown up after Stalin's terror. He knew that the system was in trouble. Stores were empty, factories made goods that no one wanted, and the army was costing a huge share of the national income. Oil prices had fallen, and the Soviet Union depended on oil for much of the money it earned from other countries. Gorbachev wanted to save the system, not to end it. He believed that communism could be reformed, and he began with two ideas.

The first was glasnostGLAHZ-nohstA Russian word meaning "openness." It was Gorbachev's policy of letting Soviet citizens discuss and criticize their government., a Russian word meaning "openness." For the first time, Soviet newspapers and writers could criticize the government, and ordinary people could discuss the country's past. The second was perestroikapair-uh-STROY-kuhA Russian word meaning "restructuring." It was Gorbachev's plan to change the Soviet economy and allow some private business., meaning "restructuring." It allowed some private business and gave factory managers more freedom. Both ideas had a surprising effect. Once people were allowed to talk, they began to say aloud what they had long believed, and the party lost much of its power to frighten them. A reform meant to repair the machine instead began to shake it apart.

Gorbachev also wanted to ease the cost of the arms race. He and Reagan met for the first time in Geneva in 1985. A year later, in Reykjavik, Iceland, they came close to agreeing to remove all nuclear weapons, but the talks broke down over Reagan's refusal to give up the Strategic Defense Initiative. Even so, the two men had built trust. In December 1987, they signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treatyin-ter-MEE-dee-it RAYNJ NOO-klee-er FOR-siz TREE-teeA 1987 treaty in which the United States and the Soviet Union agreed to destroy a whole class of nuclear missiles. It also allowed inspections. in Washington. It was the first agreement to destroy a whole class of nuclear weapons, rather than just limit their growth. The treaty also allowed each side to inspect the other's sites. Reagan used an old Russian proverb at the signing: "Trust, but verify."

Reagan's own position on the Soviets had changed too. The man who had called them an evil empire now said that he did not think of Gorbachev that way. Some historians argue that Reagan's firm stance, followed by his willingness to deal, got the best result. Others stress that Gorbachev was the one who made the choices, and that a different Soviet leader might have used force to keep the empire together. Both views are probably partly right, and this is where contingency matters. In June 1987, Reagan stood before the Brandenburg Gate in West Berlin, within sight of the wall that divided the city, and called on Gorbachev to "tear down this wall." You will read the address this week. At the time, it seemed to many like a hopeless demand.

The wall fell sooner than anyone expected. The Soviet Union's allies in Eastern Europe had been held in place for forty years by Soviet tanks. Gorbachev announced that he would no longer use them. In Poland, a free labor union called Solidaritysol-ih-DAIR-ih-teeA free labor union in Poland, led by Lech Walesa, that grew into a movement that helped end communist rule there., led by an electrician named Lech Walesa and backed by the Catholic Church and Pope John Paul II, forced the government to hold elections. In June 1989, Solidarity won nearly every seat it was allowed to contest. In the summer, Hungary opened its border with Austria, and thousands of East Germans fled to the West. Huge crowds gathered in East German cities to demand freedom. The government that had built the Berlin Wall could not stop them.

On the night of November 9, 1989, an East German official, speaking at a news conference, said by mistake that new travel rules took effect "immediately." Thousands of people rushed to the wall. The guards, unsure what to do, did not shoot, and the crossings were opened. People climbed on the wall and swung hammers at it. The sight was shown on televisions around the world. Within weeks, Czechoslovakia's communists gave up power in what became known as the Velvet RevolutionVEL-vit rev-uh-LOO-shunThe peaceful end of communist rule in Czechoslovakia in late 1989. It was called "velvet" because there was almost no violence., so called because it came almost without bloodshed. Romania's revolution that December was violent, and its dictator was executed. By the end of the year, communist rule in Eastern Europe was gone. Germany was officially reunited in October 1990, with the support of the United States.

The collapse spread to the Soviet Union itself. Its fifteen republics, including Ukraine, Lithuania, and Georgia, began to demand independence. In August 1991, communist hard-liners tried to take power in a coup. They placed Gorbachev under house arrest, but ordinary people in Moscow, led by Boris Yeltsin, the leader of the Russian republic, stood in front of the tanks, and the coup failed within three days. It destroyed what was left of the party's authority. On December 25, 1991, Gorbachev resigned. The next day, the Soviet Union ceased to exist, and the red flag was lowered from the Kremlin. Fifteen new nations took its place. The Cold War was over, without a nuclear exchange, and the United States stood as the world's only superpower.

Why did it end? Historians give several answers. Some point to America's strength: its military buildup, its economic growth, and its alliances. Others point to the Soviet system itself, which could not meet the needs of its people. Some credit leaders such as Reagan, Gorbachev, and Pope John Paul II. Others give the credit to ordinary people in Poland, East Germany, and elsewhere who risked their lives. In truth, many causes worked together. The cause that was missing is the one that people feared most. Nuclear war did not come. A classical republican might note that the Western alliance had kept up its guard for four decades, and that its patience and the habits of free societies proved stronger than the discipline of a closed one.

There is a lesson about humility too. Almost no one predicted the end of the Soviet Union, even in 1985, and certainly not in the form it took. Gorbachev, who hoped to save it, helped to end it. Reagan, who is often praised for a plan, could not have known how events would turn out. Contingency means that history can surprise even the experts. In this week's discussion, you will decide how much of the credit each person and factor deserves, and who, in the end, "won."
