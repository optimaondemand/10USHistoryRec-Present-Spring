📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Slavery had ended in 1865, and the Fourteenth Amendment had promised equal protection of the laws. But by the middle of the twentieth century, much of the South was still divided by a system of legal segregation, often called Jim Crow. Black and white Americans attended different schools, rode in different parts of buses, drank from different fountains, and were buried in different cemeteries. The law that supported this system came from an 1896 decision, Plessy v. Ferguson, in which the Supreme Court had said that states could separate the races if the facilities were equal. This was the doctrine of separate but equalSEP-er-it but EE-kwulThe doctrine from Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) that states could separate the races if the facilities were equal.. The case had begun on a train in New Orleans, where a group of Black citizens chose a shoemaker named Homer Plessy to sit in a whites-only car and be arrested on purpose. He was seven-eighths white by ancestry but counted as Black under Louisiana law. The Court ruled against him, seven votes to one. The lone dissenter, Justice John Marshall Harlan, wrote that the Constitution does not know or tolerate classes among citizens. In practice, the facilities for Black Americans were almost never equal. Southern states spent far more on white schools than on Black ones, and many Black children studied in crowded buildings with old books handed down from white schools.

Black Americans had never accepted this system. By the 1930s, a leading organization, the NAACPen-double-ay-see-peeThe National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, an organization founded in 1909 that fought segregation in court., or National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, had chosen to attack it in court. Its lawyers did not begin with a direct attack on Plessy. They went after the "equal" half of the doctrine first, by showing that Southern states could not provide truly equal graduate and professional schools. Each win chipped away at the system. The chief architect of the plan was a lawyer from Baltimore named Thurgood MarshallTHUR-good MAR-shulThe NAACP lawyer who argued Brown v. Board of Education and later became the first Black Supreme Court justice., who argued case after case before the Supreme Court. He had learned the method from his teacher Charles Hamilton Houston, who had rebuilt the law school at Howard University. Marshall knew the problem from the inside, because the University of Maryland's law school had refused to admit him, and in the 1930s he helped a young Black man win the right to attend it. In 1950, the Court looked at the small law school that Texas had set up for a Black applicant in a few rooms, and held that it could not equal the University of Texas, with its famous teachers and its network of graduates. Marshall won most of the cases he argued before the Supreme Court. Years later, Marshall would become the first Black justice on that Court.

The NAACP's attack climaxed in a group of cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, and Washington, D.C., bundled together under the name of the Kansas case, Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka. In Topeka, a railroad welder named Oliver Brown sued because his young daughter Linda had to cross a railroad yard and ride a bus to a Black school, while a white school stood close to their home. The lawyers argued that segregated schools could never be equal, because the separation itself harmed Black children. To show the harm, they pointed to the work of the psychologists Kenneth and Mamie Clark, who had shown Black children a white doll and a Black doll and asked which one was nice. Many of the children chose the white doll. Marshall faced a team of Southern lawyers led by John W. Davis, a famous Wall Street lawyer who had run for president in 1924.

The road to the decision was not smooth. The justices heard the cases in December 1952 and could not agree, so they asked the lawyers to argue again the next year. Then, in September 1953, Chief Justice Fred Vinson died of a heart attack, and President Eisenhower named Earl Warren, a former governor of California, to take his place. Contingency was at work: some historians think that the Court might not have been unanimous had Vinson lived. Warren did not rush the vote. He wanted all nine, since he knew that a divided Court would give resisters an excuse. The last justice to hesitate was Stanley Reed of Kentucky, and Warren talked with him privately until Reed agreed to join. Warren also kept the opinion short and plain, so that newspapers could print it and ordinary readers could follow it. On May 17, 1954, Chief Justice Earl Warren read the Court's unanimous opinion. "In the field of public education," he wrote, "the doctrine of 'separate but equal' has no place." You will read part of the decision this week.

Brown was a landmark, but it did not end segregation. The Court had said what was wrong without saying how fast to fix it. A year later, in a second ruling, it left the details to federal judges in each district and told local school boards to proceed "with all deliberate speedawl dih-LIB-er-it speedThe Supreme Court's vague 1955 instruction to desegregate schools, which Southern officials used to stall.." The phrase was vague, and Southern officials used it to stall. White Citizens' Councils, which began in Mississippi in 1954, did not wear hoods. Their members were local businessmen and officials, and they used pressure instead: they fired people, cut off credit, and punished Black families who signed petitions for integrated schools. In 1956, one hundred and one Southern members of Congress, nineteen senators and eighty-two representatives, signed the Southern ManifestoSUH-thurn man-ih-FES-tohA 1956 statement signed by 101 Southern members of Congress that attacked the Brown decision and promised to resist it., a statement that attacked the decision as an abuse of judicial power and promised to resist it. Some school districts closed their schools instead of integrating. In Prince Edward County, Virginia, the public schools stayed closed for five years, while white children attended private academies. A decade after Brown, only about one Black child in a hundred in the Deep South attended school with white children.

The Court could only declare the law. The next step came from ordinary people. In August 1955, a fourteen-year-old Black boy from Chicago, Emmett Till, went south to visit relatives near the town of Money, Mississippi. He was kidnapped and murdered after he was accused of whistling at a white woman, and his body was found in the Tallahatchie River. His mother, Mamie Till-Bradley, insisted on an open casket at his funeral so that the world would see what had been done. Tens of thousands of people filed past it, and Jet magazine printed the photographs. A jury of white men took about an hour to acquit the two men who were charged. Months later, safe from being tried again, the men told a magazine that they had done it. The case shocked many Americans, North and South.

Then, on December 1, 1955, a seamstress and NAACP secretary named Rosa ParksROH-zuh parksThe Montgomery seamstress and NAACP secretary whose arrest in December 1955 began the bus boycott. boarded a city bus in Montgomery, Alabama. Local law required Black riders to sit in the back and to give up seats to white riders if the front filled up. Black riders paid at the front, and were often told to get off and board again at the back door, and some drivers pulled away before they could get on. When the white section filled, the driver ordered four Black passengers in her row to give up their seats. Three of them moved. When the driver told her to move, she refused, and she was arrested. Parks was not just a tired woman who happened to say no. She was a trained activist who had been planning for years, and she had spent that summer at a workshop for activists. Local leaders had been waiting for the right case. Her arrest was one of several, though, and it was the one that they chose. Nine months earlier, a fifteen-year-old named Claudette Colvin had been arrested for the same act, but the leaders had decided that she was not the right person to build a case around. That night, E. D. Nixon, a railroad porters' union leader and a former head of the Montgomery NAACP, helped to bail Parks out.

Within days, Black leaders in Montgomery organized the Montgomery bus boycottmont-GUM-er-ee bus BOY-kotThe 381-day refusal by Black residents of Montgomery, Alabama, to ride city buses, which began in December 1955.. Jo Ann Robinson, a Black college teacher who led the Women's Political Council, stayed up through the night copying leaflets that called on riders to stay off the buses. On the first morning, the buses rolled by almost empty. That evening, thousands of people packed Holt Street Baptist Church to vote to continue. Instead of riding the buses, about forty thousand Black residents walked, carpooled, or took Black-owned taxis, and churches ran a system of cars to carry people to work. Cooks and maids who worked in white homes walked miles each way. Roughly three out of four bus riders were Black, and Black women, who made up most of the riders, were among the most determined. A young Baptist minister who had just come to town was chosen to lead the new organization. His name was Martin Luther King Jr.MAR-tin LOO-ther king JOON-yerThe Baptist minister who became the most famous leader of the civil rights movement., and he was only twenty-six. King spoke of the Christian duty of love, and also of the republic's own promises. "If we are wrong," he said, "the Supreme Court of this nation is wrong."

At first the boycotters did not even demand an end to segregation. They asked for polite treatment, seating on a first-come basis, and Black drivers on routes through Black neighborhoods. The city refused, and the boycott lasted 381 days. City officials arrested leaders, King's home was bombed, and the white Citizens' Councils pressed on. When the bomb went off in January 1956, King was away, and his wife and baby daughter were unharmed. An angry crowd gathered outside, many of them armed. King told them to go home and not to answer violence with violence. In February, a grand jury indicted eighty-nine boycott leaders under an old law against boycotts. The buses lost so much money that the city nearly went broke.

The boycott ended when the courts finally ruled. In a case brought by four Black women, Browder v. Gayle, a federal court held that bus segregation was unconstitutional, and in November 1956, the Supreme Court agreed. The lawyer who argued the case, Fred Gray, was also Parks's lawyer. The buses of Montgomery were desegregated in December. King became a national figure. In 1957, he helped to found the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, a network of Black churches and ministers that would lead the next phase of the movement.

    That same year, a crisis in Little Rock, Arkansas, tested the power of the federal government. Nine Black students, later called the Little Rock NineLIT-ul rok nyneNine Black students who integrated Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas, in 1957 under the protection of federal troops., were chosen to enter Central High School, and a federal judge had ordered the district to go ahead with its plan. Governor Orval Faubus used the National Guard to keep them out, claiming that he was preventing violence. On the first morning, September 4, one of the nine, fifteen-year-old Elizabeth Eckford, had not heard that the students were to arrive together. She walked up to the school alone, through a crowd that screamed at her, and the Guard turned her away. The head of the state NAACP, Daisy Bates, then took charge of getting the students to school safely. A mob screamed at the students again when they tried to enter later in the month.

    President Eisenhower was not a strong supporter of integration, and he had not publicly endorsed Brown. But he believed that a governor who defied a federal court was attacking the Constitution itself. On September 24, he sent about a thousand paratroopers of the 101st Airborne Division to Little Rock, and he put the Arkansas Guard under federal control. That night he went on television to explain that mob rule could not be allowed to overrule the courts. The nine students attended class under armed guard. One of them, Ernest Green, graduated the next spring. Eisenhower's choice was not about his feelings on integration. It was about the rule of law, and about whether a state could overrule the nation.

    The early movement shows different kinds of power at work. Choose who took each action.
    1. About forty thousand riders walked or carpooled for 381 days.
    2. Used the National Guard to keep nine Black students out of a high school, claiming to prevent violence.
    3. A federal court held bus segregation unconstitutional in a case brought by four women.

    The early movement shows three different kinds of power at work. Courts could change the law, but they could not enforce it alone. Ordinary citizens could show moral courage and economic strength, as the boycott did. And the president could decide whether the nation's laws would be enforced. The next lesson shows what happened when the movement used all three at once.
