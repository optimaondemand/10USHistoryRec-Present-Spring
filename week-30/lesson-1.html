📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Slavery had ended in 1865, and the Fourteenth Amendment had promised equal protection of the laws. But by the middle of the twentieth century, much of the South was still divided by a system of legal segregation. Black and white Americans attended different schools, rode in different parts of buses, drank from different fountains, and were buried in different cemeteries. The law that supported this system came from an 1896 decision, Plessy v. Ferguson, in which the Supreme Court had said that states could separate the races if the facilities were equal. This was the doctrine of separate but equalSEP-er-it but EE-kwulThe doctrine from Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) that states could separate the races if the facilities were equal.. In practice, the facilities for Black Americans were almost never equal. Southern states spent far more on white schools than on Black ones.

Black Americans had never accepted this system. By the 1930s, a leading organization, the NAACPen-double-ay-see-peeThe National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, an organization founded in 1909 that fought segregation in court., or National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, had chosen to attack it in court. Its lawyers did not begin with a direct attack on Plessy. They went after the "equal" half of the doctrine first, by showing that Southern states could not provide truly equal graduate and professional schools. Each win chipped away at the system. The chief architect of the plan was a lawyer from Baltimore named Thurgood MarshallTHUR-good MAR-shulThe NAACP lawyer who argued Brown v. Board of Education and later became the first Black Supreme Court justice., who argued case after case before the Supreme Court. Years later, Marshall would become the first Black justice on that Court.

The NAACP's attack climaxed in a group of cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, and Washington, D.C., bundled together under the name of the Kansas case, Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka. The lawyers argued that segregated schools could never be equal, because the separation itself harmed Black children. On May 17, 1954, Chief Justice Earl Warren read the Court's unanimous opinion. "In the field of public education," he wrote, "the doctrine of 'separate but equal' has no place." You will read part of the decision this week. Warren had worked hard to win all nine votes, since he knew that a divided Court would give resisters an excuse. Contingency was at work: the previous chief justice, Fred Vinson, had died in 1953, and some historians think that the Court might not have been unanimous had he lived.

Brown was a landmark, but it did not end segregation. The Court had said what was wrong without saying how fast to fix it. A year later, in a second ruling, it told local school boards to proceed "with all deliberate speedawl dih-LIB-er-it speedThe Supreme Court's vague 1955 instruction to desegregate schools, which Southern officials used to stall.." The phrase was vague, and Southern officials used it to stall. In 1956, one hundred and one Southern members of Congress signed the Southern ManifestoSUH-thurn man-ih-FES-tohA 1956 statement signed by 101 Southern members of Congress that attacked the Brown decision and promised to resist it., a statement that attacked the decision as an abuse of judicial power and promised to resist it. Some school districts closed their schools instead of integrating. A decade after Brown, only about one Black child in a hundred in the Deep South attended school with white children.

The Court could only declare the law. The next step came from ordinary people. In August 1955, a fourteen-year-old Black boy from Chicago, Emmett Till, was kidnapped and murdered in Mississippi after he was accused of whistling at a white woman. His mother insisted on an open casket at his funeral so that the world would see what had been done, and a jury of white men quickly acquitted the two men who were charged. The case shocked many Americans, North and South.

Then, on December 1, 1955, a seamstress and NAACP secretary named Rosa ParksROH-zuh parksThe Montgomery seamstress and NAACP secretary whose arrest in December 1955 began the bus boycott. boarded a city bus in Montgomery, Alabama. Local law required Black riders to sit in the back and to give up seats to white riders if the front filled up. When the driver told her to move, she refused, and she was arrested. Parks was not just a tired woman who happened to say no. She was a trained activist who had been planning for years, and local leaders had been waiting for the right case. Her arrest was one of several, though, and it was the one that they chose.

Within days, Black leaders in Montgomery organized the Montgomery bus boycottmont-GUM-er-ee bus BOY-kotThe 381-day refusal by Black residents of Montgomery, Alabama, to ride city buses, which began in December 1955.. Instead of riding the buses, about forty thousand Black residents walked, carpooled, or took Black-owned taxis. Black women, who made up most of the riders, were among the most determined. A young Baptist minister who had just come to town was chosen to lead the new organization. His name was Martin Luther King Jr.MAR-tin LOO-ther king JOON-yerThe Baptist minister who became the most famous leader of the civil rights movement., and he was only twenty-six. King spoke of the Christian duty of love, and also of the republic's own promises. "If we are wrong," he said, "the Supreme Court of this nation is wrong." The boycott lasted 381 days. City officials arrested leaders, King's home was bombed, and the white Citizens' Councils pressed on. The buses lost so much money that the city nearly went broke.

The boycott ended when the courts finally ruled. In a case brought by four Black women, Browder v. Gayle, a federal court held that bus segregation was unconstitutional, and in November 1956, the Supreme Court agreed. The buses of Montgomery were desegregated in December. King became a national figure. In 1957, he helped to found the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, a network of Black churches and ministers that would lead the next phase of the movement.

That same year, a crisis in Little Rock, Arkansas, tested the power of the federal government. Nine Black students, later called the Little Rock NineLIT-ul rok nyneNine Black students who integrated Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas, in 1957 under the protection of federal troops., were chosen to enter Central High School. Governor Orval Faubus used the National Guard to keep them out, claiming that he was preventing violence. A mob screamed at the students. President Eisenhower was not a strong supporter of integration, and he had not publicly endorsed Brown. But he believed that a governor who defied a federal court was attacking the Constitution itself. On September 24, he sent about a thousand paratroopers of the 101st Airborne Division to Little Rock, and he put the Arkansas Guard under federal control. The nine students attended class under armed guard. Eisenhower's choice was not about his feelings on integration. It was about the rule of law, and about whether a state could overrule the nation.

The early movement shows three different kinds of power at work. Courts could change the law, but they could not enforce it alone. Ordinary citizens could show moral courage and economic strength, as the boycott did. And the president could decide whether the nation's laws would be enforced. The next lesson shows what happened when the movement used all three at once.
