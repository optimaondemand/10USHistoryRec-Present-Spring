📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By 1960, the Supreme Court had ruled, and the buses of Montgomery were integrated. But in much of the South, daily life remained the same. A new group of Black leaders, many of them college students, decided that waiting for the courts was not enough. They chose direct actiondih-REKT AK-shunOpenly breaking an unjust rule or custom in order to force the public to face it., which means openly breaking an unjust local rule or custom in order to force the public to face it.

On February 1, 1960, four Black students from North Carolina A&T, Ezell Blair Jr., Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil, and David Richmond, sat down at a whites-only lunch counter at a Woolworth's store in Greensboro and asked to be served. They had bought a few things at the store first and kept the receipt, to show that Woolworth's took their money at one counter and refused it at another. They were refused. They stayed until closing time, and they returned the next day with more students. Within weeks, the sit-inSIT-inA protest in which people sit down in a place where they are refused service and stay until they are served or removed. had spread to dozens of cities, about thirty of them in seven states by the end of the month. Others had tried the tactic before, but Greensboro caught fire. Black shoppers and some white students stayed away from the downtown stores, and sales fell. Students sat quietly, dressed neatly, and did not strike back when they were cursed at, spat on, or hit. In April 1960, young activists formed the Student Nonviolent Coordinating CommitteeSTOO-dent non-VY-uh-lent koh-OR-dih-nay-ting kuh-MIT-eeA group of young activists formed in 1960 to carry on sit-ins and other direct action. It is often called SNCC., or SNCC, to carry the work forward. Ella Baker, an experienced organizer who had worked for older civil rights groups, urged them to build their own group instead of joining one of those. By that summer, Woolworth's in Greensboro was serving Black customers at its lunch counter.

In 1961, the Congress of Racial Equality, led by James Farmer, sent mixed groups of Black and white riders on buses through the South to test a court order against segregation in bus stations. These Freedom RidersFREE-dum RY-derzBlack and white activists who rode buses through the South in 1961 to test a ban on segregation in bus stations. were attacked by mobs in Alabama. One bus was burned outside the town of Anniston, and in Birmingham a mob beat riders at the bus station while the police stayed away. When the first riders were too hurt to go on, students from Nashville, led by Diane Nash, decided that the rides must continue, since stopping would tell the mobs that violence worked. President Kennedy was slow to act, since he did not want to anger Southern Democrats, but the violence was on the news every night. He sent federal marshals, and his brother, Attorney General Robert Kennedy, pressed the Interstate Commerce Commission to enforce the ban. Robert Kennedy also asked the riders for a cooling-off period, and Farmer answered that Black Americans had been cooling off for three hundred and fifty years. Before the summer was over, hundreds of riders had been arrested in Mississippi and sent to the state penitentiary at Parchman. That fall, the commission ordered bus stations to end segregation.

The lesson says he was slow to act because he did not want to anger Southern Democrats. The nightly pictures made the violence hard for the country to ignore, and then he sent marshals. Without the pictures he might have waited longer. This is reasoning from the lesson, not a certain result.

The reasons for nonviolence were both moral and practical. For Martin Luther King Jr., nonviolence was grounded in his Christian faith and in the teachings of Mohandas Gandhi, who had led India's campaign for independence without arms. It was a principle, a way of loving one's enemies while refusing to obey an unjust law. It was also a smart strategy. When peaceful protesters were beaten by officers of the law, the injustice of the system was plain for the whole country to see. The method had to be learned. In workshops run by a minister named James Lawson, who had studied Gandhi's work in India, students took turns playing an angry crowd while others practiced sitting still and not answering back. Not every leader held the same views. Some saw it mostly as a tool, to be used because it worked, and some in the movement grew impatient with it by the mid-1960s. You will weigh that question in this week's discussion.

In the spring of 1963, King took the movement to Birmingham, Alabama, which he called the most segregated big city in America. People there called it "Bombingham," because so many bombings of Black homes and churches had never been solved. The city's public safety commissioner, Bull Connorbul KON-erThe Birmingham public safety commissioner who used police dogs and fire hoses on civil rights marchers in 1963., was a determined segregationist. The city had closed its parks rather than integrate them. King had learned from a failed campaign in Albany, Georgia, the year before, where the police chief arrested marchers politely and sent them to jails in other towns, so that no one took pictures of violence. In Birmingham, the leaders thought that Connor, a man with a famous temper, would give the cameras what Albany had not. King and his allies planned marches, boycotts, and sit-ins, and they were arrested in April. King himself was jailed on April 12, which was Good Friday, for marching without a permit. In jail, King answered a group of eight white clergymen who had called the protests "unwise and untimely." His reply, the "Letter from Birmingham Jail," was written in the margins of a newspaper and on scraps of paper that his lawyers carried out. It explained why waiting was not an answer. You will read part of the letter this week. In it, he described the four steps of a nonviolent campaign and separated just laws from unjust ones. A just law, he said, is in line with moral law.

In May, the movement made a bold choice and sent children and teenagers to march. Adult volunteers were running low after weeks of arrests, and some leaders, including King, worried about putting children in danger. They went ahead anyway. On the first day, hundreds of young people were arrested and packed into jails and school buses. The next day, Connor's men answered with police dogs and high-pressure fire hoses. Photographs and film of the scenes went around the world. President Kennedy said that the pictures made him sick. Within days, Birmingham's leaders agreed to open lunch counters and other places to Black customers.

A few weeks later, the president took his stand. In his inaugural speech that January, Governor George Wallace of Alabama had promised to defend segregation forever. On the morning of June 11, 1963, he stood in the doorway of a building at the University of Alabama to keep two Black students, Vivian Malone and James Hood, from enrolling, and he stepped aside only after Kennedy ordered the Alabama National Guard into federal service. That evening, Kennedy gave a televised address, and asked Congress for a broad civil rights law. He called the issue a moral one, "as old as the Scriptures and as clear as the American Constitution." That night, Medgar Evers, a Mississippi NAACP leader, was shot and killed in his driveway.

On August 28, 1963, about 250,000 people gathered in Washington for the March on Washingtonmarch on WOSH-ing-tonThe August 28, 1963, gathering of about 250,000 people at the Lincoln Memorial for jobs and freedom., which was officially called the March for Jobs and Freedom. President Kennedy had first asked the organizers to call it off, fearing that a riot would doom the bill. They came by chartered bus and special train, and about one in four was white. Organizers had hoped for about 100,000. They stood on the National Mall before the Lincoln Memorial, and singers such as Mahalia Jackson and Joan Baez performed. It was one of the largest demonstrations the capital had seen. A. Philip Randolph, the man who had threatened a similar march in 1941, was its elder statesman, and his aide Bayard Rustin organized the day down to the water and sandwiches. The youngest speaker was John Lewis of SNCC, who was twenty-three. King's speech, in which the words "I have a dream" rang out again and again, became one of the most famous in American history. Near the end of his prepared text, the singer Mahalia Jackson called out to him to tell the crowd about the dream, and he set the pages aside. Less than three weeks later, a bomb exploded at the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church in Birmingham and killed four young girls: Addie Mae Collins, Carole Robertson, Cynthia Wesley, and Denise McNair. Then, in November, Kennedy was assassinated, and the bill's fate seemed uncertain.

Lyndon Johnson, the new president, made the bill a memorial to Kennedy, and he used all of his skill as a Senate leader. In the House, a Virginia congressman who chaired the Rules Committee held it up for weeks until supporters forced it out, and the House passed it in February. In the Senate, Southern senators used a filibusterFIL-ih-bus-terA way of blocking a Senate vote by talking without end., a way of blocking a vote by talking without end. It lasted for more than fifty days. Ending a filibuster took a vote of two thirds of the Senate, which had almost never been won on a civil rights bill. Johnson and Senator Hubert Humphrey won over enough Republicans, led by Everett Dirksen of Illinois, and in June 1964, the Senate voted to end debate, by 71 to 29. The Civil Rights Act of 1964SIV-il ryts akt uv nyne-TEEN SIX-tee-forThe law that banned segregation in public places and discrimination in employment. became law on July 2. It banned segregation in hotels, restaurants, and other public places, and it outlawed discrimination in employment and in programs that got federal money. It was among the most significant laws in American history, and it had passed because the movement, the press, and a skillful president had worked together. Notice that it had also depended on Republican votes.

But the right to vote was still blocked in much of the South. Many Black citizens who tried to register were turned away by a literacy testLIT-er-uh-see testA reading or understanding test that officials could grade unfairly to keep Black citizens from voting., a test of reading or understanding that officials could grade unfairly, or they were threatened with violence. Applicants might be told to explain a section of the state constitution, and the registrar alone decided whether the answer was good enough. In Selma, Alabama, only about two percent of eligible Black voters were registered. Local Black citizens and SNCC workers had stood in line at the courthouse for months, and almost none were allowed to sign the book. The county sheriff, Jim Clark, met those who lined up at the courthouse with deputies. In February 1965, in a nearby town, a state trooper shot a young Black man named Jimmie Lee Jackson, who died of his wounds. Leaders decided to march to the state capital in his memory.

That winter, King, who had just won the Nobel Peace Prize, came to Selma. In March 1965, King and local leaders organized a march from Selma to the state capital in Montgomery. On March 7, state troopers attacked the marchers with clubs and tear gas on the Edmund Pettus Bridge, in an event called Bloody Sunday. King was in Atlanta that day. John Lewis, who led the line, was beaten and his skull was fractured. Television carried the pictures to the nation. A second march two days later turned back at the bridge, and that night a white minister from Boston who had come to Selma was beaten and later died. Eight days after Bloody Sunday, Johnson went before Congress and used the movement's own words: "We shall overcome." Two days later, he sent his voting rights bill to the Capitol. Late that month, about 25,000 people reached Montgomery on a third march, this time under federal protection.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965VOH-ting ryts akt uv nyne-TEEN SIX-tee-fyveThe law that banned literacy tests and sent federal examiners to counties that kept Black citizens from voting. became law on August 6, with civil rights leaders looking on. It banned literacy tests, and it sent federal examiners to counties with a history of keeping Black citizens from voting. Those counties also had to get federal approval before changing their voting rules. A constitutional amendment ratified in 1964 had already banned poll taxes in federal elections. Registration among Black Southerners rose quickly. In Mississippi, about seven percent of eligible Black adults were registered in 1964, and by 1968 about six in ten were. Black officeholders appeared across the region within a few years.

⚡ PUT IT IN ORDER
Put these events in order, from earliest to latest. Tap them one at a time.

    By any measure, the movement had changed the republic. It had used peaceful protest to win laws that matched the principles of the Constitution. At the same time, it had a limit. Laws could end legal segregation, but they could not by themselves change the poverty and distrust that were left behind. The anger and impatience that grew from that gap were about to change the movement itself.
