📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In November 1960, John F. Kennedyjon eff KEN-uh-deeThe thirty-fifth president, elected in 1960 and assassinated in November 1963., a young senator from Massachusetts, won one of the closest elections in American history. At forty-three, he was the youngest man ever elected president. His inaugural address in January 1961 promised that the United States would "pay any price, bear any burden" to defend liberty. The test of those words came quickly, and it came in a place only ninety miles from Florida.

In 1959, a rebel leader named Fidel Castrofih-DEL KAS-trohThe communist leader who took power in Cuba in 1959 and ruled it for decades. had overthrown the Cuban dictator Fulgencio Batista. Within two years, Castro had taken American property, put his opponents in jail, and turned to the Soviet Union for money and arms. The Eisenhower administration had begun a secret plan to train Cuban exiles to invade the island. Kennedy inherited the plan and approved it. On April 17, 1961, about fourteen hundred exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs, on Cuba's southern coast. The plan had expected that the Cuban people would rise up, and that American air power would help. Neither happened. Kennedy refused to send in American planes, and Castro's forces captured most of the invaders. The Bay of Pigsbay uv pigzThe failed April 1961 invasion of Cuba by about fourteen hundred American-trained exiles. was a humiliating failure. Kennedy took responsibility on television, and privately he admitted that he had been poorly advised. He also learned to be more careful about what the military and the CIA told him.

Two months later, in Vienna, Kennedy met the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchevnih-KEE-tuh KROOSH-chefThe Soviet leader from 1953 to 1964. for the first time. The meeting went badly. Khrushchev concluded that the young president was weak and could be pushed. He renewed a demand that the Western powers leave Berlin. Kennedy told the nation that Berlin was a vital interest. The real problem for Khrushchev was that thousands of East Germans were fleeing to the West through Berlin each month, including many of its most skilled workers. In the early morning of August 13, 1961, East German soldiers began to string barbed wire across the city. It soon became a concrete wall, and the Berlin Wallber-LIN wawlThe barrier built by East Germany in August 1961 to stop people from fleeing to West Berlin. It stood for twenty-eight years. stood for twenty-eight years. Kennedy did not try to knock it down. Privately, he said that a wall was better than a war.

In the summer of 1962, Khrushchev made a bold and risky move. He began to send medium-range nuclear missiles to Cuba, in secret. His reasons were mixed. He wanted to protect Cuba from an American invasion, and he wanted to even the balance of nuclear weapons, since the United States had missiles in Turkey and Italy that could reach the Soviet Union. He also hoped to gain an advantage in Berlin.

On October 16, 1962, Kennedy was shown photographs, taken by a U-2 spy plane, that showed missile sites being built in Cuba. For thirteen days, the country came close to nuclear war. Kennedy gathered a group of advisers, which later became known as ExCommEKS-komThe Executive Committee of the National Security Council, the group of advisers Kennedy gathered during the Cuban Missile Crisis., short for the Executive Committee of the National Security Council. Their discussions, which Kennedy secretly taped, show a group of serious people who disagreed. The Joint Chiefs wanted air strikes followed by an invasion. Others thought that a strike would lead to a Soviet attack on Berlin, or on the United States itself. A few wanted to do nothing, and to accept that the Soviets had missiles too.

Kennedy chose something in between. He ordered a naval blockade to stop Soviet ships carrying weapons to Cuba. The word "blockade" is an act of war under international law, so he called it a quarantineKWOR-un-teenKennedy's name for the naval blockade of Cuba in 1962, chosen because a "blockade" is an act of war.. On October 22, he went on television and told the nation about the missiles. He warned that any missile launched from Cuba would be treated as an attack by the Soviet Union, requiring a full response. Millions of Americans listened in fear. Some families stocked up on food. The crisis reached its peak over the weekend. On October 24, Soviet ships bound for Cuba stopped short of the quarantine line. Secretary of State Dean Rusk said, "We're eyeball to eyeball, and I think the other fellow just blinked." But the missiles in Cuba were still being finished. On Friday, October 26, Khrushchev sent a long, emotional letter offering to remove the missiles if the United States promised not to invade Cuba. On Saturday, October 27, a second and tougher letter arrived, demanding that the United States remove its missiles from Turkey. The same day, a Soviet missile shot down an American U-2 over Cuba and killed its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson. Some advisers urged an immediate strike. Kennedy held back.

The solution was a mixture of firmness and compromise. Kennedy ignored the second letter and answered the first. His brother, Attorney General Robert Kennedy, told the Soviet ambassador privately that the American missiles in Turkey would be removed within a few months, though this could not be announced as part of a deal. On Sunday, October 28, Khrushchev agreed to take his missiles out of Cuba. The world let out its breath.

How close was it? Later evidence suggests that the danger was greater than those in the room knew. Soviet commanders in Cuba had tactical nuclear weapons, and at sea, the crew of a Soviet submarine, cornered by American ships, nearly launched a nuclear torpedo. According to later accounts, an officer named Vasily Arkhipov refused to agree to the launch. Historians still debate the details. But the story is a good reminder of how the history of the century could have turned on one man's decision.

Was Kennedy a brinkmanship gambler or a skilled statesman? The word brinkmanshipBRINK-mun-shipPushing a dangerous situation to the edge of disaster to make the other side back down. means pushing a dangerous situation to the edge of disaster in order to make the other side back down. Critics say that Kennedy's own earlier mistakes, the Bay of Pigs and the Vienna meeting, helped to cause the crisis, and that he risked the world over missiles that did not really change the balance of power. Defenders say that he refused to bomb, avoided a trap, left Khrushchev a way out, and ended the crisis without war. Virtues like prudence, which is good judgment about what to do in a hard case, were on display here. So was luck.

The crisis changed the Cold War. Both leaders had seen how close they came. A direct telephone line, the "hotline," was set up between Washington and Moscow in 1963. In August 1963, the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union signed the Limited Test Ban TreatyLIM-ih-tid test ban TREE-teeThe 1963 treaty among the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union that banned nuclear tests in the air, in space, and underwater., which banned nuclear tests in the air, in space, and underwater. Khrushchev was removed from power in 1964, in part because of the Cuban humiliation. Kennedy did not live to see what came next. On November 22, 1963, he was shot and killed in Dallas, Texas.
