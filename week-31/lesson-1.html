📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In November 1960, John F. Kennedyjon eff KEN-uh-deeThe thirty-fifth president, elected in 1960 and assassinated in November 1963., a young senator from Massachusetts, won one of the closest elections in American history. He beat Vice President Nixon by about 100,000 votes out of nearly 69 million. Television helped. In September, the two men had met in the first televised presidential debates. Kennedy looked calm and rested. Nixon, who had recently been ill, looked pale and tired, and many viewers judged Kennedy the winner. At forty-three, he was the youngest man ever elected president. His inaugural address, given on a bitterly cold day in January 1961, promised that the United States would "pay any price, bear any burden" to defend liberty, and it urged Americans to "ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country." Within two months, he had created the Peace Corps, which sent young volunteers to work in poor countries overseas. The test of those words came quickly, and it came in a place only ninety miles from Florida.

In 1959, a rebel leader named Fidel Castrofih-DEL KAS-trohThe communist leader who took power in Cuba in 1959 and ruled it for decades. had overthrown the Cuban dictator Fulgencio Batista. Within two years, Castro had taken American property, put his opponents in jail, and turned to the Soviet Union for money and arms. The Eisenhower administration had begun a secret plan to train Cuban exiles to invade the island. Kennedy inherited the plan and approved it. On April 17, 1961, about fourteen hundred exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs, on Cuba's southern coast. The plan had expected that the Cuban people would rise up, and that American air power would help. Neither happened.

The air power was a particular problem. Two days before the landing, planes flown by exiles and painted to look like Cuban planes had bombed Castro's airfields. On the night before the landing, Kennedy, worried that American fingerprints were showing, canceled a second round of strikes. Castro's surviving planes then sank ships carrying much of the invaders' ammunition and supplies. The invaders' other hope was to escape into the Escambray Mountains and fight on as guerrillas, but those mountains lay about eighty miles away across swamp. Kennedy refused to send in American planes, and Castro's forces captured most of the invaders. Nearly twelve hundred went to prison, and the next year Cuba released them in return for about fifty million dollars in food and medicine. The Bay of Pigsbay uv pigzThe failed April 1961 invasion of Cuba by about fourteen hundred American-trained exiles. was a humiliating failure. Kennedy took responsibility on television, and privately he admitted that he had been poorly advised. He also learned to be more careful about what the military and the CIA told him.

Two months later, in Vienna, Kennedy met the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchevnih-KEE-tuh KROOSH-chefThe Soviet leader from 1953 to 1964. for the first time. The meeting went badly. Khrushchev lectured and threatened, and Kennedy later told a reporter that it was the roughest experience of his life. Khrushchev concluded that the young president was weak and could be pushed. He renewed a demand that the Western powers leave Berlin. Kennedy told the nation that Berlin was a vital interest. The city was a strange place. It lay deep inside Communist East Germany, yet it was divided among the four powers that had won the Second World War, and its western half was a free island. An East German who reached West Berlin could walk out of the Communist world and fly on to the West. The real problem for Khrushchev was that thousands of East Germans were fleeing to the West through Berlin each month, including many of its most skilled workers. Since 1949, about two and a half million had gone.

In the early morning of Sunday, August 13, 1961, East German soldiers began to string barbed wire across the city. Families woke to find streets they had crossed all their lives cut in two. The wire soon became a concrete wall, and the Berlin Wallber-LIN wawlThe barrier built by East Germany in August 1961 to stop people from fleeing to West Berlin. It stood for twenty-eight years. stood for twenty-eight years. Kennedy did not try to knock it down. Privately, he said that a wall was better than a war. That October, American and Soviet tanks sat for most of a day with their guns pointed at each other at Checkpoint Charlie, a crossing point in the wall, in a dispute over whether American officials could enter East Berlin freely. Both sides backed away, and no one fired. Over the years that followed, at least a hundred people died trying to cross the wall.

In the summer of 1962, Khrushchev made a bold and risky move. He began to send medium-range nuclear missiles to Cuba, in secret. The missiles could reach Washington and much of the eastern United States. Soviet ships brought them under cover, along with about forty thousand Soviet troops to guard them, four times as many as American officials guessed. Soviet diplomats assured Kennedy that only defensive weapons were going to Cuba, and in September he warned publicly that offensive weapons there would raise the gravest issues. Khrushchev's reasons were mixed. He wanted to protect Cuba from an American invasion, and he wanted to even the balance of nuclear weapons, since the United States had missiles in Turkey and Italy that could reach the Soviet Union. He also hoped to gain an advantage in Berlin.

On October 16, 1962, Kennedy was shown photographs, taken by a U-2 spy plane, that showed missile sites being built in Cuba. The pictures had been taken two days earlier, and it had taken analysts another day to be sure what the long shapes on the ground were. His national security adviser, McGeorge Bundy, brought the news to the president that morning, while he was still in his bedroom. For thirteen days, the country came close to nuclear war. For the first week, almost no one outside the White House knew. Kennedy kept to his public schedule, and when he cut a campaign trip short on October 20, reporters were told that he had a cold.

Kennedy gathered a group of advisers, which later became known as ExCommEKS-komThe Executive Committee of the National Security Council, the group of advisers Kennedy gathered during the Cuban Missile Crisis., short for the Executive Committee of the National Security Council. Their discussions, which Kennedy secretly taped, show a group of serious people who disagreed. The Joint Chiefs wanted air strikes followed by an invasion. Others thought that a strike would lead to a Soviet attack on Berlin, or on the United States itself. Robert McNamara, the secretary of defense, favored a blockade first, which left room for something harsher later. Attorney General Robert Kennedy, the president's brother, passed him a note comparing a surprise attack on Cuba to Pearl Harbor. A few wanted to do nothing, and to accept that the Soviets had missiles too.

Kennedy chose something in between. He ordered a naval blockade to stop Soviet ships carrying weapons to Cuba. The word "blockade" is an act of war under international law, so he called it a quarantineKWOR-un-teenKennedy's name for the naval blockade of Cuba in 1962, chosen because a "blockade" is an act of war.. Navy ships would stop Soviet vessels at a line about five hundred miles from Cuba, and American nuclear forces went to the highest alert level they have ever reached. On October 22, he went on television and told the nation about the missiles. He warned that any missile launched from Cuba would be treated as an attack by the Soviet Union, requiring a full response. Millions of Americans listened in fear. Some families stocked up on food. The crisis reached its peak over the weekend. On October 24, Soviet ships bound for Cuba stopped short of the quarantine line. Secretary of State Dean Rusk said, "We're eyeball to eyeball, and I think the other fellow just blinked." But the missiles in Cuba were still being finished. At the United Nations, the American ambassador, Adlai Stevenson, set photographs on easels before the Security Council and told the Soviet ambassador not to wait for the translation: did the Soviet Union have missiles in Cuba, yes or no? A Soviet tanker carrying only oil was let through the line after it identified itself. On Friday, October 26, Khrushchev sent a long, emotional letter offering to remove the missiles if the United States promised not to invade Cuba. That same day, a Soviet embassy official had asked a reporter for ABC News, John Scali, over lunch whether the United States would make that promise in return for the missiles' removal, and Scali carried the message to the White House.

On Saturday, October 27, a second and tougher letter arrived, demanding that the United States remove its missiles from Turkey. The same day, a Soviet missile shot down an American U-2 over Cuba and killed its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson. Some advisers urged an immediate strike. Kennedy held back. On the same day, another U-2 lost its way in a storm and strayed into Soviet airspace over Siberia, where Soviet fighters chased it off. In such a tense hour, a mistake like that could have looked like the first step of an attack.

The solution was a mixture of firmness and compromise. Kennedy had recently read Barbara Tuchman's book The Guns of August, which tells how the leaders of Europe slid into war in 1914 through a chain of orders and mistakes, and he told his brother that he did not intend to be the president who let that happen again. Kennedy ignored the second letter and answered the first, accepting its offer: the missiles would come out of Cuba, and the United States would promise not to invade.

That evening, Robert Kennedy met privately with the Soviet ambassador, Anatoly Dobrynin, at the Justice Department. He told him that the American missiles in Turkey would be removed within a few months, though this could not be announced as part of a deal. The missiles in Turkey were old, and the president had already wanted them gone, but Turkey was an ally, and a public trade would have looked like giving in to a threat. On Sunday, October 28, Khrushchev agreed to take his missiles out of Cuba. He did not wait for a letter to cross the ocean. His answer was broadcast over Radio Moscow so that no one could delay it. The world let out its breath.

How close was it? Later evidence suggests that the danger was greater than those in the room knew. Soviet commanders in Cuba had tactical nuclear weapons, which American planners did not know were there, and at sea, the crew of a Soviet submarine, cornered by American ships, nearly launched a nuclear torpedo. American destroyers had been dropping small practice depth charges to force the submarine to the surface. Inside, the air was hot and foul, the crew had been out of touch with Moscow for days, and some aboard thought that war had already begun. According to later accounts, firing the torpedo required the agreement of three senior officers, and one of them, Vasily Arkhipov, refused to agree to the launch. Historians still debate the details. But the story is a good reminder of how the history of the century could have turned on one man's decision.

Was Kennedy a brinkmanship gambler or a skilled statesman? The word brinkmanshipBRINK-mun-shipPushing a dangerous situation to the edge of disaster to make the other side back down. means pushing a dangerous situation to the edge of disaster in order to make the other side back down. Critics say that Kennedy's own earlier mistakes, the Bay of Pigs and the Vienna meeting, helped to cause the crisis, and that he risked the world over missiles that did not really change the balance of power. Defenders say that he refused to bomb, avoided a trap, left Khrushchev a way out, and ended the crisis without war. Virtues like prudence, which is good judgment about what to do in a hard case, were on display here. So was luck.

The crisis changed the Cold War. Both leaders had seen how close they came. In June 1963, in a speech at American University, Kennedy urged Americans to look again at the Soviets and at peace, reminding them that "we all inhabit this small planet." A direct telephone line, the "hotline," was set up between Washington and Moscow in 1963. In August 1963, the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union signed the Limited Test Ban TreatyLIM-ih-tid test ban TREE-teeThe 1963 treaty among the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union that banned nuclear tests in the air, in space, and underwater., which banned nuclear tests in the air, in space, and underwater. Tests underground were still allowed. Khrushchev was removed from power in 1964, in part because of the Cuban humiliation.

Kennedy did not live to see what came next. On November 22, 1963, he was on a political trip to Texas, riding in an open car through the streets of Dallas, when he was shot and killed. A former Marine named Lee Harvey Oswald was arrested. Two days later, Oswald was himself shot dead by a nightclub owner, Jack Ruby, in front of television cameras. A commission headed by Chief Justice Earl Warren later concluded that Oswald had acted alone. The nation watched the funeral on television.
