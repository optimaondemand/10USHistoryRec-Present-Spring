📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On the afternoon of November 22, 1963, Vice President Lyndon B. JohnsonLIN-dun bee JON-sunThe thirty-sixth president, who took office after Kennedy's death and served until 1969. took the oath of office on an airplane in Dallas, with Kennedy's widow standing at his side. A federal judge, Sarah Hughes, gave him the oath, and Jacqueline Kennedy still wore the suit stained with her husband's blood. Then the plane flew to Washington with the president's coffin on board. Johnson asked Kennedy's cabinet and aides to stay on, so that the country would see that the government went on. Johnson was a very different man from Kennedy. He was a tall Texan from a poor part of the Hill Country, who had begun as a schoolteacher. In the small town of Cotulla, he taught poor Mexican American children who often came to school hungry, and he said later that the experience stayed with him. From there he had risen to become the most effective Senate majority leader of his time. He understood how Congress worked better than any president of the century. He knew whom to flatter, whom to pressure, and what each member needed. As vice president, he had felt like an outsider among Kennedy's polished circle of advisers, and he knew that many people doubted him. As Senate leader, he had been known for what people called the "Johnson treatment": standing close, leaning in, and talking and pleading until a senator gave way. When Kennedy died, his two biggest bills were stuck in committees. Within five days, he told Congress that the best memorial for Kennedy would be to pass the laws that Kennedy had proposed. His speech to Congress closed with the words "Let us continue."

He started with two. The first was a tax cut, which Kennedy had asked for and which passed in 1964. It cut the top income tax rate from 91 percent to 70 percent, so that families and businesses would have more to spend and invest. A powerful senator, Harry Byrd of Virginia, held the bill in his committee until Johnson promised to keep federal spending under one hundred billion dollars, and then it moved. It helped to power a long economic boom. The second was the Civil Rights Act, which you studied in the last module.

But Johnson wanted more than to finish Kennedy's work. In May 1964, speaking to a crowd of about eighty thousand in the football stadium at the University of Michigan, he described what he wanted to build. It would be a Great Societygrayt suh-SY-uh-teeJohnson's name for his plan to end poverty and racial injustice and to improve education, health care, and cities., one "that rests on abundance and liberty for all" and that "demands an end to poverty and racial injustice." Behind the speech, Johnson set up task forces of outside experts to draft bills in advance, so that when Congress met in 1965, the plans would be ready. You will read this speech in this week's source.

The first target was poverty. In 1964, nearly one in five Americans lived below the poverty linePOV-er-tee lyneThe income level the government sets as the least a family needs to meet its basic needs., the income level that the government sets as the least a family needs to meet its basic needs. That year, the line was about three thousand dollars a year for a family of four. A 1962 book, The Other America, by Michael Harrington, had shown many readers how many Americans lived in poverty in the middle of plenty, and many of the poor lived in places that prosperity had passed by. That spring, Johnson flew to Kentucky and sat on the porch of an out-of-work sawmill worker in the Appalachian mountains, with the cameras rolling, to show the nation what he meant. Johnson declared a War on Povertywor on POV-er-teeJohnson's 1964 campaign of programs meant to lift Americans out of poverty.. He asked Sargent Shriver, who had built the Peace Corps, to lead it.

The Economic Opportunity Act, passed that August, created a number of programs. Job Corps took young people from poor families to live at training centers, where they learned job skills. VISTA sent volunteers to work in poor communities, as a kind of domestic Peace Corps. Head Starthed startA program begun in 1965 that gives preschool classes to children from low-income families. gave preschool classes to children from low-income homes, to help them be ready for school. More than half a million children took part in its first summer. Community Action Programs let poor neighborhoods run local efforts. The law called for the "maximum feasible participation" of the poor in them. That sounded fair, but it led to quarrels. Some big-city mayors complained that federal money was paying groups that criticized them, and Congress later gave local officials more control. Some of these programs produced results. Head Start is still around today, and some studies find that children who attended did better in later life. Others had problems, and some were poorly managed. In 1964, Congress also made the food stamp program permanent, so that poor families could use stamps to buy groceries.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST
Five days after he took the oath, Johnson told Congress that the best memorial to Kennedy would be to pass Kennedy's own laws.
Why might that argument work, and what else would Johnson need? Decide first and jot down two ideas before you open this.
The argument may have worked because the nation was grieving. But a mood fades. Johnson also needed what the lesson says he had: a close knowledge of Congress, of whom to flatter, whom to pressure, and what each member needed. Had Kennedy lived, the same bills might have moved more slowly, or not at all. That is reasoning, not settled fact, since the lesson cannot tell us what would have happened. It does show that a good idea and a skilled legislator are two different things.

Johnson faced the voters in 1964 against Senator Barry GoldwaterBAIR-ee GOHL-waw-terThe Arizona senator and conservative who ran against Johnson in 1964. of Arizona. Johnson had chosen Senator Hubert Humphrey of Minnesota, a leader in the fight for the Civil Rights Act, as his running mate. Goldwater was an open conservative who believed that the federal government had grown too large and too powerful. He had voted against the Civil Rights Act, because he thought that parts of it went beyond what the Constitution allowed. His supporters liked that he spoke his mind, and his slogan was "In your heart you know he's right." His opponents tried to paint him as dangerous, and a famous Johnson television ad showed a little girl pulling the petals off a daisy while a voice counted down to a nuclear explosion. It never named Goldwater, and it aired only once, but people talked about it for weeks. It suggested that Goldwater might start a nuclear war.

In November, Johnson won with about 61 percent of the vote, the largest share of any president in the twentieth century. Goldwater carried only his home state and five states of the Deep South, where the Civil Rights Act was most bitterly opposed. Democrats also won huge majorities in Congress, and in 1965, Johnson had the votes to pass nearly anything he wanted.

What he wanted next was health care. Before 1965, many elderly Americans had no health insurance, and illness could wipe out a family's savings. Doctors' groups had fought national health insurance for years, calling it government medicine. In July 1965, Johnson signed MedicareMED-ih-kairA program, begun in 1965, that pays for hospital and medical care for Americans over sixty-five., which pays for hospital and medical care for Americans over sixty-five, and MedicaidMED-ih-kaydA program, begun in 1965, that helps the states pay for the health care of poor people., which helps the states pay for the health care of poor people. Medicare's hospital coverage was paid for by a tax on workers' paychecks, and older people could add doctor coverage for a small monthly fee. Each state ran its own Medicaid program with federal help. He signed the bill in Independence, Missouri, in front of Harry Truman, who had proposed national health insurance twenty years before. Johnson handed Truman the first Medicare card. In just a few months, millions of older Americans were enrolled. By the end of the first year, about nineteen million had signed up.

The 1965 Congress passed a great deal more. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act sent federal money to schools in poor districts, the first big federal role in local education. Johnson signed it in April, outside the one-room school in Stonewall, Texas, that he had attended as a boy, with his first teacher, Kate Deadrich Loney, at his side. Earlier education bills had died in a fight over aid to church schools, so this one directed the money at poor children rather than at any kind of school.

The Higher Education Act created scholarships and loans for college. Johnson signed it at Southwest Texas State, the small college where he had trained to be a teacher. Congress also created the National Endowments for the Arts and for the Humanities, a Department of Housing and Urban Development, and the Voting Rights Act, which you have already read about. New laws set standards for the safety of cars and for clean air and water. The car-safety law, passed in 1966, followed a book by a young lawyer, Ralph Nader, that charged automakers with ignoring safety. Lady Bird Johnson, the president's wife, led a campaign to plant flowers and to clear roadsides of billboards and junkyards, and it led to the Highway Beautification Act.

⚡ PUT IT IN ORDER
Put these events in order. Tap them one at a time.

    One of the most important laws was also one of the least noticed at the time. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965im-ih-GRAY-shun and nash-uh-NAL-ih-tee akt uv nyne-TEEN SIX-tee-fyveThe law that ended the quota system based on national origin and gave priority to family ties and skills. ended the quota system of the 1920s, which had set limits by national origin and favored Northern Europeans. Under the old quotas, most places went to people from Britain, Ireland, and Germany, while Italy, Greece, Poland, and other countries had small limits, and most of Asia was almost shut out. The new law gave priority to family ties and to skills. Supporters said that it was just a matter of fairness. They expected that few new immigrants would come from outside Europe. Johnson signed the law at the foot of the Statue of Liberty, and he said that it was not a revolutionary bill and would not reshape the daily lives of Americans.

    In fact, over the following decades, immigration shifted greatly toward Asia and Latin America. One reason was the family rule. Close relatives of citizens could come in without counting against the yearly limits, so each new arrival could open the door to others. The law also set a cap on immigration from the Western Hemisphere for the first time. Here, as in other cases, the results were very different from the expectations. It is one of the best examples in this course of an unintended consequence.

    How did the Great Society do? Supporters have a strong case. The poverty rate fell from about nineteen percent in 1964 to about twelve percent by 1969, and the elderly, who had been the poorest group in America, did far better once Medicare and Social Security grew. Medicare and Medicaid spread basic medical care. Medicare also gave hospitals a reason to end segregation, because hospitals that wanted its payments had to open their wards to all patients, and many in the South did so within months. Millions of students went to college with federal help.

    Critics have a case too. Some argue that welfare programs, as they grew, rewarded people for not working or not marrying, and led to long-term dependence. Conservatives made an argument that fit the Tocqueville tradition: that the real strength of America came from churches, families, and local groups, and that big federal programs pushed these aside. Others pointed out that the poverty line counted only money income, and so left out help such as medical care, which is one reason the figures are argued over. Still others said that the programs were too big and too hastily designed, that they promised more than they could deliver, and that federal spending on them added to the cost of government. A republic judges policies by whether they help solve the first-order economic problem, and this one is still contested. Another argument is about federalism: should the national government decide how to run local schools and health care, or should the states and cities?

Critics point to dependence, cost, and who should run local schools. How settled is the question of whether it worked?

    There was one more difficulty, and it was growing. Johnson wanted to spend on both guns and butter, which means that he wanted to fight a war abroad and build a Great Society at home. By 1965, the war in Vietnam was swallowing up money, attention, and the trust of the American people. Johnson himself feared that the war would crowd out his domestic dreams, and he later told a biographer that it had done just that. He said that giving up the Great Society for a war he did not want felt like abandoning the thing he loved most. He put off asking Americans to pay higher taxes for the war, because he feared that a tax fight would sink his domestic bills. He asked in 1967, and Congress did not pass an increase until 1968. By then spending was running ahead of income, and prices were beginning to rise faster. The next module turns to that war, the largest and most troubling event of his presidency.
