📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On the afternoon of November 22, 1963, Vice President Lyndon B. JohnsonLIN-dun bee JON-sunThe thirty-sixth president, who took office after Kennedy's death and served until 1969. took the oath of office on an airplane in Dallas, with Kennedy's widow standing at his side. Johnson was a very different man from Kennedy. He was a tall Texan from a poor part of the Hill Country, who had begun as a schoolteacher and risen to become the most effective Senate majority leader of his time. He understood how Congress worked better than any president of the century. He knew whom to flatter, whom to pressure, and what each member needed. Within five days, he told Congress that the best memorial for Kennedy would be to pass the laws that Kennedy had proposed.

He started with two. The first was a tax cut, which Kennedy had asked for and which passed in 1964. It helped to power a long economic boom. The second was the Civil Rights Act, which you studied in the last module. But Johnson wanted more than to finish Kennedy's work. In May 1964, speaking at the University of Michigan, he described what he wanted to build. It would be a Great Societygrayt suh-SY-uh-teeJohnson's name for his plan to end poverty and racial injustice and to improve education, health care, and cities., one "that rests on abundance and liberty for all" and that "demands an end to poverty and racial injustice." You will read this speech in this week's source.

The first target was poverty. In 1964, nearly one in five Americans lived below the poverty linePOV-er-tee lyneThe income level the government sets as the least a family needs to meet its basic needs., the income level that the government sets as the least a family needs to meet its basic needs. Johnson declared a War on Povertywor on POV-er-teeJohnson's 1964 campaign of programs meant to lift Americans out of poverty.. The Economic Opportunity Act, passed that August, created a number of programs. Job Corps trained young people from poor families. VISTA sent volunteers to work in poor communities, as a kind of domestic Peace Corps. Head Starthed startA program begun in 1965 that gives preschool classes to children from low-income families. gave preschool classes to children from low-income homes, to help them be ready for school. Community Action Programs let poor neighborhoods run local efforts. Some of these programs produced results. Head Start is still around today, and some studies find that children who attended did better in later life. Others had problems, and some were poorly managed.

Johnson faced the voters in 1964 against Senator Barry GoldwaterBAIR-ee GOHL-waw-terThe Arizona senator and conservative who ran against Johnson in 1964. of Arizona. Goldwater was an open conservative who believed that the federal government had grown too large and too powerful. He had voted against the Civil Rights Act, because he thought that parts of it went beyond what the Constitution allowed. His supporters liked that he spoke his mind. His opponents tried to paint him as dangerous, and a famous Johnson television ad suggested that Goldwater might start a nuclear war. In November, Johnson won with about 61 percent of the vote, the largest share of any president in the twentieth century. Democrats also won huge majorities in Congress, and in 1965, Johnson had the votes to pass nearly anything he wanted.

What he wanted next was health care. Before 1965, many elderly Americans had no health insurance, and illness could wipe out a family's savings. In July 1965, Johnson signed MedicareMED-ih-kairA program, begun in 1965, that pays for hospital and medical care for Americans over sixty-five., which pays for hospital and medical care for Americans over sixty-five, and MedicaidMED-ih-kaydA program, begun in 1965, that helps the states pay for the health care of poor people., which helps the states pay for the health care of poor people. He signed the bill in Independence, Missouri, in front of Harry Truman, who had proposed national health insurance twenty years before. In just a few months, millions of older Americans were enrolled.

The 1965 Congress passed a great deal more. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act sent federal money to schools in poor districts, the first big federal role in local education. The Higher Education Act created scholarships and loans for college. Congress also created the National Endowments for the Arts and for the Humanities, a Department of Housing and Urban Development, and the Voting Rights Act, which you have already read about. New laws set standards for the safety of cars and for clean air and water.

One of the most important laws was also one of the least noticed at the time. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965im-ih-GRAY-shun and nash-uh-NAL-ih-tee akt uv nyne-TEEN SIX-tee-fyveThe law that ended the quota system based on national origin and gave priority to family ties and skills. ended the quota system of the 1920s, which had set limits by national origin and favored Northern Europeans. The new law gave priority to family ties and to skills. Supporters said that it was just a matter of fairness. They expected that few new immigrants would come from outside Europe. In fact, over the following decades, immigration shifted greatly toward Asia and Latin America. Here, as in other cases, the results were very different from the expectations. It is one of the best examples in this course of an unintended consequence.

How did the Great Society do? Supporters have a strong case. The poverty rate fell from about nineteen percent in 1964 to about twelve percent by 1969, and the elderly, who had been the poorest group in America, did far better once Medicare and Social Security grew. Medicare and Medicaid spread basic medical care. Millions of students went to college with federal help.

Critics have a case too. Some argue that welfare programs, as they grew, rewarded people for not working or not marrying, and led to long-term dependence. Conservatives made an argument that fit the Tocqueville tradition: that the real strength of America came from churches, families, and local groups, and that big federal programs pushed these aside. Others said that the programs were too big and too hastily designed, that they promised more than they could deliver, and that federal spending on them added to the cost of government. A republic judges policies by whether they help solve the first-order economic problem, and this one is still contested. Another argument is about federalism: should the national government decide how to run local schools and health care, or should the states and cities?

There was one more difficulty, and it was growing. Johnson wanted to spend on both guns and butter, which means that he wanted to fight a war abroad and build a Great Society at home. By 1965, the war in Vietnam was swallowing up money, attention, and the trust of the American people. Johnson himself feared that the war would crowd out his domestic dreams, and he later told a biographer that it had done just that. The next module turns to that war, the largest and most troubling event of his presidency.
