Red Scare, Strikes, and the Closing Door

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Americans hoped the end of the war would bring peace at home. Instead, 1919 brought fear. Prices had jumped during the war, and wages had not kept up. Workers who had held back from striking during the war now wanted raises. In 1919, about four million workers walked off their jobs, a record. In February, some sixty-five thousand workers in Seattle shut down much of the city in a general strikeJEN-er-ul strykA strike in which workers in many different industries stop working at once. Seattle had one in February 1919.. The strikers kept some services running, including milk deliveries and kitchens that fed thousands of people, and there was almost no violence. Even so, Seattle's mayor, Ole Hanson, called it the start of a revolution and brought in soldiers. The strike ended after about five days. Hanson soon resigned and made money for a time giving speeches about the dangers of radicals. Many Americans looked at Seattle and saw revolution, though the strikers had mostly asked for better pay.

In September, most of Boston's police force went on strike. The police were poorly paid, and when the commissioner fired nineteen officers for joining a union, most of the force walked out. Looting broke out in the streets. Governor Calvin CoolidgeKAL-vin KOO-lijThe governor of Massachusetts who ended the Boston police strike in 1919. He became vice president in 1921 and president in 1923, and he won election in his own right in 1924. called out the state guard and ended the strike. "There is no right to strike against the public safety by anybody, anywhere, any time," he said. The line made him famous across the country. The striking officers were fired and replaced, and Coolidge won reelection as governor that fall by a wide margin. In 1920, Republicans chose him as Harding's running mate. That same month, 350,000 steelworkers struck, and their employers painted them as dangerous radicals. Many of the strikers worked twelve-hour days, and they wanted shorter hours and the right to bargain through a union. The head of U.S. Steel, Elbert Gary, refused even to meet with their leaders, and company spokesmen pointed to a few radicals among the strikers to turn the public against all of them. Steel companies also brought in Black workers from the South and Mexican workers to take the strikers' jobs, which turned some of the strikers' anger against those workers instead of the owners. The steel strike collapsed in early 1920.

The unrest was not only about labor and radicals. The summer of 1919 also brought racial violence, later called the Red Summer. The name came from the blood, not from communism. In more than two dozen cities, white mobs attacked Black neighborhoods, and Black residents, many of them returning soldiers, fought back. In Chicago, a riot that began at a beach in July left thirty-eight people dead and about a thousand families, most of them Black, without homes. It began on July 27, when a Black teenager named Eugene Williams drifted on a raft across an unmarked line in Lake Michigan into water that white swimmers thought of as theirs. Stones were thrown, and he drowned. When police refused to arrest the white man whom Black witnesses named, fighting broke out and went on for days. In October came another bloody outbreak, in Elaine, Arkansas, after Black sharecroppers met to organize for better pay from the landowners. Estimates of how many Black people were killed vary widely, but it was many. Black Americans had fought for their country overseas. Now they came home to a country that seemed to be at war with itself.

Many Americans tied these strikes to a larger fear. In 1917, the BolsheviksBOHL-shih-viksThe Communists who took power in Russia in 1917. Many Americans feared they wanted to spark revolutions in other countries. had seized power in Russia and built a Communist government. They called on workers everywhere to overthrow their own governments. Then, in 1919, anarchistsAN-er-kistsPeople who believe that governments should be abolished. A few anarchists used bombs in the years after the Great War. began mailing bombs to public figures. In April, about three dozen mail bombs were sent to judges, officials, and business leaders, though most were caught at the post office because the packages did not carry enough postage. On June 2, bombs went off in eight cities on the same night. One wrecked the front of the Washington home of Attorney General A. Mitchell PalmerAY MICH-ul PAHL-merThe attorney general who led the Palmer Raids against suspected radicals in 1919 and 1920.. Franklin D. Roosevelt, then an official in the Navy Department, lived across the street and ran over to help. Palmer and his family were unhurt, but the man who carried the bomb was killed. At each site, the bombers left flyers titled "Plain Words," warning that more would come. Many Americans were shocked. They feared a revolution was near. That fear is called the Red Scarered skairThe widespread fear of Communists and radicals in 1919 and 1920, named for the red flag of communism., named for the red flag of communism.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST
Imagine the bombs of June 1919 had never been mailed. The strikes, the Russian Revolution, and the fear of radicals would all still be there.
Would Americans still have backed large raids on suspected radicals? Decide first and jot down your answer and one reason before you open this.
The lesson shows several pressures at work. Strikes had already frightened many people, and the BolsheviksBOHL-shih-viksThe Communists who took power in Russia in 1917. Many Americans feared they wanted to spark revolutions in other countries. in Russia made a revolution at home seem possible. But the bombs, including one at the Attorney General's own home, turned worry into shock. Without them, the public might have been slower to accept raids, though nobody can say for sure. Other events might have supplied the same push. What you can judge is how much the bombs mattered compared with the strikes and the fear of revolution.

Palmer answered with force. His Justice Department set up a unit to track radicals, and a young lawyer named J. Edgar Hoover ran it. Hoover was twenty-four, and he filled index cards with the names of thousands of suspected radicals. In November 1919 and again in January 1920, federal agents carried out the Palmer RaidsPAH-mer raydzArrests of thousands of suspected radicals by federal agents in 1919 and 1920, often without warrants.. On one night in January, they arrested thousands of people in more than thirty cities. Many were seized without warrants. Some were held for days, beaten, or denied lawyers. Most were not dangerous. Many had done nothing more than join a union or a club for people from their old country. Hundreds of people who were not citizens faced deportationdee-por-TAY-shunForcing a person who is not a citizen to leave the country., which means being forced to leave the country. The Labor Department held the hearings, and immigrants often faced them without a lawyer. In December 1919, a ship called the Buford carried 249 of them to Russia, including the famous anarchist Emma Goldman. Newspapers called it the "Soviet Ark," and some crowds cheered as it left.

What stopped the raids? Law and common sense. Louis F. Post, an official in the Labor Department, reviewed the deportation cases. He found that agents had broken the rules, and he canceled many orders. He ruled that people were entitled to a lawyer and that evidence taken in illegal searches could not be used, and he threw out most of the cases that came to him. Judges criticized the raids. A group of prominent lawyers published a report calling them lawless. Twelve lawyers and law professors signed it, including Felix Frankfurter of Harvard, who would later sit on the Supreme Court. Then Palmer predicted a wave of violence on May 1, 1920. Police stood ready across the country, and nothing happened. Palmer looked foolish, and the Scare faded. A bomb on Wall Street in September 1920 killed dozens of people, but by then the public mood had changed. It exploded in a horse-drawn wagon at lunchtime, outside the offices of the banker J. P. Morgan and Company. Most of the dead were clerks, messengers, and passersby, and the crime was never solved.

The fear left one more famous case behind. In May 1920, Massachusetts police arrested two Italian immigrants and anarchists, Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, for a robbery and murder. A paymaster and a guard had been shot and killed while carrying a factory payroll in South Braintree. The two men were arrested on a streetcar, carrying guns and lying about their friends, because they feared being deported as radicals. They were convicted in 1921. Many people then and since have doubted that the trial was fair, and some felt the two men were judged for their beliefs and their birthplace as much as for the evidence. Others believed the verdict was right. Both men were executed in 1927, and protests against the executions were held in cities around the world.

⚡ PUT IT IN ORDER
Put these events of 1919 and 1920 in the order they happened. Tap them one at a time.

    The fear reached beyond bombers and foreign-born radicals. Eugene Debs, a leader of the Socialist Party who had already run for president four times, was arrested in 1918 for a speech against the war and sentenced to ten years in prison under a law that punished speech that hurt the war effort. In 1920, he ran for president again from his cell in the federal prison in Atlanta, and he won more than nine hundred thousand votes. Harding freed him at Christmas in 1921. Debs's case raised the same question as the raids. How far may a government go in punishing words, and when does it begin to punish beliefs? A republic that cannot tolerate dissent has given up something it was founded to protect.

    The Red Scare shows how a republic can stumble on its first duty, keeping order. Real violence had occurred, and a government must protect its people. But a republic also promises to rule by law that treats everyone the same way. In trying to reach the first goal, Palmer gave up the second. Palmer hoped to win the Democratic nomination for president in 1920. Historians still weigh how much ambition shaped his choices. What is clear is that the raids made him famous, and then they ruined him. The young man who kept the index cards did better. Hoover was made head of the Bureau of Investigation in 1924, and he led it, and the FBI it became, for nearly fifty years.

    Fear of radicals joined an older worry, fear of newcomers. Between 1900 and 1914, millions of immigrants had arrived, many from southern and eastern Europe. Many Americans looked at the crowded cities, full of people who spoke other languages, and worried about what kind of country they were becoming. Before 1921, there had been no overall limit on how many Europeans could come. A person who passed a health check and a legal check on arrival was let in, and since 1917 a person over sixteen also had to pass a simple reading test. After the war, many Americans expected another surge. NativismNAY-tiv-iz-umThe belief that people born in a country should be favored over immigrants., the belief that native-born residents should be favored over immigrants, grew strong. In 1921, Congress passed the Emergency Quota Actih-MER-jun-see KWOH-tuh aktA 1921 law that limited immigration by letting in only three percent of the number of people from each country already living in the United States in 1910.. A quotaKWOH-tuhA fixed limit on how many people or things may be allowed in. is a fixed limit. The law let in only three percent of the number of people from each country who had been living in the United States in 1910. That held total immigration to about 357,000 a year, down from more than 800,000 the year before.

    The Immigration Act of 1924im-ih-GRAY-shun akt uv nyn-TEEN TWEN-tee-forA law that cut immigration quotas to two percent of each nationality's 1890 population and shut out nearly all Asian immigrants. went further. It cut the share to two percent and moved the starting date back to 1890. That date was chosen on purpose. In 1890, far fewer southern and eastern Europeans were living in the country, so the new law favored people from northern and western Europe. The effect was sharp. Italy's yearly quota, about 42,000 under the 1921 law, fell to fewer than 4,000. Britain, Ireland, and Germany together received more than half of all the places. The act also shut out nearly all immigrants from Asia. Chinese workers had been barred since 1882, and Japan had agreed in 1907 to limit its own emigrants, so Japan took the new law as a public insult. Immigrants from Canada and Latin America were not under the quotas. From then on, an immigrant had to get a visa at an American consulate in the old country before sailing, so the long inspection lines at Ellis Island mattered less. The same year, Congress created the Border Patrol, whose jobs included stopping people who crossed the line without permission. President Coolidge signed the bill. The system stayed in place, with changes, for more than forty years.

    ⚡ HOW MUCH DID IT MATTER?
    The 1924 law moved its starting date back to 1890. How much did that choice depend on a wish to favor some newcomers over others?

    Supporters made serious arguments. A republic, they said, needs citizens who share loyalties, language, and habits, and a flood of strangers can strain schools, neighborhoods, and wages. Some labor leaders backed limits for that last reason, because a steady stream of new workers could pull wages down. Critics answered that earlier immigrants had also been feared, and they had still become Americans. They said it was wrong to judge people by their ancestors instead of their character. Both sides spoke in the name of the common good. They disagreed about who could be part of it.

    By 1921, the country was tired. Harding promised normalcyNOR-mul-seeWarren Harding's word for a calm return to ordinary life after the war and years of reform., and many hoped that meant calm. The country had just refused to join the League of Nationsleeg uv NAY-shunzThe international organization created by the Treaty of Versailles to keep the peace. The United States never joined it., turned away its radicals with raids, and then closed its door to many immigrants. Together, these choices show a nation pulling back from the world. Next week, you will see that the same years also saw the most dramatic economic change in the country's history. A man in Michigan was about to change how Americans worked, traveled, and spent.
