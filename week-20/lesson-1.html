Henry Ford and the Assembly Line

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In 1908, a new car cost far more than most families earned in a year. Cars were toys for the rich. Henry Ford wanted something different. Henry FordHEN-ree fordThe Michigan carmaker who built the Model T and introduced the moving assembly line. was a Michigan farm boy who had trained as a machinist and an engineer. He wanted to build a car that ordinary families could buy. In October 1908, his company released the Model TMOD-ul teeHenry Ford's tough, simple car, first sold in 1908. By 1927, fifteen million had been built.. It was tough, simple, and easy to repair. It sat high enough to bounce over rough dirt roads. It cost $850. That was a fair price for the time, but it was still out of reach for many families. Ford made it his mission to push the price down.

How did he do it? Part of the answer was interchangeable partsin-ter-CHAYN-juh-bul partsPieces made so exactly alike that any one of them will fit any product of the same kind.. These are pieces made so exactly alike that any one will fit any car. A mechanic no longer had to file and fit each piece by hand. Gunmakers in New England had used the idea in the early 1800s, but no one had ever applied it on Ford's scale. The little car soon earned a nickname, the "Tin Lizzie," and a flood of jokes. But the larger part of the answer came in 1913, at Ford's plant in Highland Park, Michigan. Before then, workers had walked from car to car, building each one in a single spot. Ford's engineers tried something new. They set the car's frame on a moving line and brought it to the workers. Each worker stayed in place and did one small job. The idea of a moving line had been used by Chicago meatpackers, and Ford's men borrowed it. The result was the assembly lineuh-SEM-blee lynA way of building something in which the product moves past workers who each stay in one place and do one small job.. The time needed to build a car's chassis, or frame, fell from more than twelve hours to about an hour and a half.

Ford also used ideas from an engineer named Frederick W. TaylorFRED-rik DUB-ul-yoo TAY-lerAn engineer who timed workers with a stopwatch to find the one best way to do each job. His ideas were called scientific management.. Taylor timed workers with a stopwatch and tried to find the "one best way" to do each job. His method was called scientific managementsy-un-TIF-ik MAN-ij-mentA system developed by Frederick Taylor that studied and timed each task to find the most efficient way to do it.. Put the three ideas together, and you have mass productionmas pruh-DUK-shunMaking huge numbers of identical goods cheaply, using standard parts, machines, and an assembly line., the making of huge numbers of identical goods at low cost. The Model T's price fell below $300 in the 1920s. By 1927, Ford had built fifteen million of them. He also built a giant plant at River Rouge, near Detroit, and owned the forests, mines, and ships that supplied it. This is called vertical integrationVER-tih-kul in-tuh-GRAY-shunOwning every stage of making a product, from raw materials to the finished good., which means controlling every stage of production from raw materials to the finished product.

The work was not pleasant. Jobs on the line were fast, dull, and tiring. Workers quit in huge numbers, and this turnoverTURN-oh-verHow often workers quit and must be replaced. High turnover is costly because each new worker has to be trained. cost Ford money, because every new worker had to be trained. In January 1914, Ford announced the Five-Dollar Dayfyv DOL-er dayHenry Ford's 1914 wage of five dollars a day, about double the usual factory pay. It cut worker turnover sharply.. It was about double the usual factory wage. Crowds of job seekers lined up outside his gates, and in the cold some of them rioted. Ford said that high pay would also create customers, since workers could now afford the cars they built. His motives were mixed, and historians still debate them. What is clear is that the plan worked. Quitting dropped sharply, and Ford's rivals had to raise wages too.

The high wage came with strings. Ford set up a Sociological Department. Its inspectors visited workers' homes to check on their habits. A worker who drank, kept a messy house, or lived in ways Ford disliked could lose part of the pay. Some workers accepted this as help. Many resented it as an employer reaching into their private lives. It raises an old question. How far should anyone, a company or a government, go in shaping the character of fellow citizens?

Ford is a good example of how one person's strengths and weaknesses can shape an era. His virtues were real. He had imagination, he kept trying after two earlier car companies failed, and he cared about producing something ordinary people could use. But he was also stubborn. He kept building the same car long after buyers wanted more. Other companies offered color, comfort, and new styles each year. Ford held on to the Model T until 1927, when he shut his factories for months to build a new model. By then, General Motors had passed him as the top seller. Ford also used his own newspaper in the early 1920s to print hateful lies about Jewish Americans, and he apologized in 1927. A fair account must record both his gifts and his faults.

The car changed American life. It needed steel, glass, rubber, and oil, so whole industries grew up around it. It needed paved roads, gas stations, repair shops, and roadside restaurants. By 1929, there were more than 23 million cars on American roads, about one for every five people. Farm families were no longer stuck miles from the nearest town. Women gained new freedom to travel and run errands on their own. Some Black families valued the car because it let them avoid segregated trains and buses, though finding a place to eat or sleep on the road could be hard in many parts of the country. A family could drive to church, to a market, or to a relative in the next county. Towns that sat on the new highways grew. Towns that did not were sometimes left behind. Blacksmiths, stable owners, and some railroads lost business.

Did the car make Americans freer? In some ways, clearly yes. People could move, shop, work, and visit as they chose. In other ways, the story is mixed. Cars made workers dependent on factory jobs, and the assembly line took away much of the pride of craft that earlier workers felt. Both sides of the story belong in an honest account, and you will return to the question in this week's discussion.

None of this was fixed in advance. If Ford's early companies had not failed, he might never have learned the lessons that produced the Model T. If the moving line had been tried first by a rival, a different company might have led the age. Ford was not simply carried along by large forces. He made choices, some wise and some foolish, and those choices changed the country.

One problem remained, and it was a new one. The factories could now make more cars than most families could pay for in cash. The next lesson explains how Americans solved that problem, and what the solution cost them.
