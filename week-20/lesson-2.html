📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Even at under $300, a Model T was a lot of money for a family whose whole income might be $1,500 a year. Many families would have had to save for years. Mass productionmas pruh-DUK-shunMaking huge numbers of identical goods cheaply, using standard parts, machines, and an assembly line. and the assembly lineuh-SEM-blee lynA way of building something in which the product moves past workers who each stay in one place and do one small job. had solved the problem of making goods cheaply. Now a new problem appeared. Factories could turn out more cars, radios, and washing machines than people could pay for in cash. If the buyers could not be found, the factories would sit idle. The answer was a new way to pay.

That answer was creditKRED-itAn arrangement that lets a buyer take a product now and pay for it later., which means buying something now and paying for it later. For most of American history, debt carried a stain. Parents taught children the old rule of thrift, which is to save first and buy later. Banks and civic groups still preached it. Every January, near Benjamin Franklin's birthday, the country held National Thrift Week, with speeches and bank drives that urged families to open savings accounts. Before 1919, many bankers refused to lend money for cars, which they saw as a risky luxury. But in 1919, General Motors created the General Motors Acceptance CorporationJEN-er-ul MOH-terz ak-SEP-tuns kor-puh-RAY-shunThe lending company General Motors created in 1919 to help customers buy its cars on credit. to lend money to people who wanted to buy its cars. The loan took the form of an installment planin-STAWL-ment planA way of buying in which the buyer pays a small amount down and the rest in monthly payments. If the buyer misses payments, the seller can take the product back.. A buyer paid a small amount down and the rest in monthly payments. If the buyer missed payments, the seller could take the product back. Ford resisted the idea for years. At first he offered a plan in which buyers saved a little each week toward a car, and later, in 1928, his company set up its own finance business. By the end of the decade, most new cars were sold on credit, about two of every three.

Here is how a typical loan worked. A buyer put down about a third of the price. The balance was divided into monthly payments, usually spread over a year. The car itself was the security for the loan, which means the lender could take it back if payments stopped. The interest was usually added up front, so a buyer paid interest on the whole amount even as the balance shrank, and the true cost of the loan was higher than it looked. A dealer could sell a car to a family that could not pay cash, and the dealer got his money at once, because the finance company bought the loan from him. That arrangement is why dealers, banks, and factories all liked it. Another loan sat further up the chain. The finance company also lent dealers the money to buy cars from the factory in the first place, so a showroom could fill its floor with models it had not yet paid for. Borrowed money moved each car from the factory to the dealer and then to the family.

Credit spread to other goods. People bought radios, refrigerators, vacuum cleaners, washing machines, and furniture this way. By the late 1920s, a majority of the radios and vacuum cleaners sold in the country were bought on installments. Economists call these consumer durableskun-SOO-mer DOOR-uh-bulzGoods meant to last for years, such as cars, radios, refrigerators, and washing machines., meaning goods meant to last for years. Electric power was reaching more and more city homes. In 1920, about a third of American homes were wired for electricity, and by 1929 it was about two-thirds. Families wanted the gadgets that went with it. Radio is the best example. Sales of radio sets rose from about $60 million in 1922 to more than $800 million in 1929. A new kind of advertising helped. Ads no longer just described a product. They sold a feeling, such as status, comfort, or the fear of being left behind. Spending on advertising roughly doubled during the decade, and much of it was aimed at wives, who ran most households. An ad for a washing machine or an electric iron promised a woman her afternoons back, and the small monthly payment looked tiny beside the promise. One famous campaign for a mouthwash gave bad breath a scary medical name, halitosis, and warned that it could cost a person friends and even a marriage.

Factories could make more than families could pay for in cash, so something had to change. The General Motors lending company showed one way, and Ford gave in later and offered his own installment planin-STAWL-ment planA way of buying in which the buyer pays a small amount down and the rest in monthly payments. If the buyer misses payments, the seller can take the product back.. Other firms or banks might have found a similar way, so the idea was not tied to one company. But the lesson also shows that old habits of thrift stood in the way, so the change was not certain, and nobody can say exactly when it would have come.

Buying a house worked differently, and it worked in a way that would matter later. A bank in those years usually lent for a short time, often five to ten years, and often for only half or so of the house's price. The monthly payments covered little more than the interest, so most of the loan was still owed when the term ended. Then the owner faced one large final payment, sometimes called a balloon payment, or had to talk the bank into a new loan. Few owners worried, because banks nearly always agreed. The whole arrangement held together only while banks kept lending and house prices held steady. If either failed, the owner had no way to pay, and the bank could take the house.

Credit even fed a boom in land. In the mid-1920s, thousands of buyers rushed to Florida, putting down small deposits on lots they had never seen. A buyer paid about a tenth of the price for a paper called a binder, which held the lot until the rest came due. Many planned not to live there at all but to sell the contract to someone else at a higher price, sometimes within days, before the next payment arrived. For a while, prices soared, and whole towns went up almost overnight. Swindlers followed the crowds. Charles Ponzi, a Boston promoter whose scheme to double investors' money had collapsed and sent him to prison, turned up in Florida in 1925 selling lots near Jacksonville, promising a huge profit in two months. Much of the land was swamp. He was convicted again, and his name later became a label for any swindle that pays old investors with new investors' money. Then buyers ran short, and in September 1926 a powerful hurricane struck Miami. It killed hundreds of people and wrecked thousands of buildings. The boom collapsed. Florida's bust came three years before the nation's, and it was an early warning that a rise in prices fed by borrowed money can fall as quickly as it rose.

The car business also shows how much one leader's choices can matter. Alfred P. He saw that buyers were changing. Once most families owned a car, they wanted a better one. Sloan built a ladder of brands, from the cheap Chevrolet at the bottom to the costly Cadillac at the top, so a family could move up as its income grew. Sloan's own phrase for the plan was a car for every purse and purpose. He offered bright colors and changed styles every year. A family with a two-year-old car could be made to feel a little behind, and the installment plan made it easy to trade up. Ford's Model TMOD-ul teeHenry Ford's tough, simple car, first sold in 1908. In May 1927, the last Model T, the fifteen millionth, rolled out of the plant. In 1927, General Motors passed Ford as the nation's top seller, and Ford had to close his plants for months to retool. The replacement, the Model A, arrived in December 1927 and came in a choice of colors. The man who had invented a new way to build a car had failed to see that customers wanted more than a cheap one.

What was Washington doing during these years? Warren Harding died suddenly in August 1923, and Vice President Calvin Coolidge took over. Coolidge was visiting his father's farmhouse in Vermont when the news came, in the middle of the night. His father, a local official who was also a notary public, gave him the oath of office by the light of a kerosene lamp in the family sitting room. Harding's administration had been stained by scandal, including a bribery case over the leasing of government oil land, known as Teapot Dome, in which a cabinet officer went to prison. Coolidge was quiet and honest, and he restored trust. Both men followed a policy of laissez-faireleh-say FAIRA French phrase meaning "let it be." The Treasury Secretary, Andrew MellonAN-droo MEL-unA very wealthy banker who served as Treasury Secretary in the 1920s and pushed for tax cuts., was one of the richest men in the country. He pushed for tax cuts and smaller government spending. The top tax rate on the highest incomes fell from 73 percent in 1920 to 25 percent by the middle of the decade. Mellon argued that lower taxes would encourage people to invest and build. His reasoning was that very high rates led rich people to hide their money in tax-free bonds, so lower rates would bring that money out and put it to work in factories. The spending cuts also let the government pay down its war debt. Many critics replied that the cuts mainly helped the wealthy. It is true that most families paid no federal income tax in those years, because the tax reached only people with fairly high incomes, so the cuts were felt mostly at the top.

The prosperity was real. Output per worker rose, because new machines and electric motors let each worker make more. Wages for factory workers rose, and unemployment stayed low for most of the decade. Millions of families lived better than their parents had. Yet not everyone shared in the good times. Farmers had grown rich during the war, but when European farms recovered, prices for crops fell hard. Many farmers were stuck with debts that they had taken on in the boom. Coal miners and textile workers faced low pay and weak demand. Many Black Southerners remained poor. Large parts of the countryside went without electricity. As late as 1930, only about one farm in ten had electric power. Even in the cities the gains were uneven. Near the end of the decade, the richest five percent of Americans received about a third of all the money earned in the country.

⚡ HOW WIDELY SHARED?
The prosperity of the 1920s was real. How widely was it shared?

Farm families felt the squeeze most. During the war, they had borrowed money to buy land and machines, because high prices made the loans look safe. When prices fell, the debts stayed the same. Wheat and corn sold for roughly half of their wartime prices, and many farms were lost to the bank. Even in the good years, hundreds of small banks failed each year, mostly in farm country. Farm leaders asked Congress to buy surplus crops and sell them abroad, in a plan known as the McNary-Haugen bill. Congress passed it twice, and Coolidge vetoed it both times, saying the government should not set farm prices.

Was credit a good thing? Its defenders said it let ordinary families enjoy comforts that only the rich had once known. They also said that making regular payments taught discipline. It kept factories busy and workers employed. Supporters pointed to what the goods did. A washing machine could save hours of hard labor each week, and a car could carry a worker to a better job. For such a family, paying over time was a way to get those benefits sooner.

Its critics saw a danger in it. Credit pulls future income into the present. A family that buys a car on installments has promised part of its future pay to someone else. A nation of such families may spend more than it would have, because the bill seems far away. The cost could surprise a buyer, since interest was often added up front, and a family that lost its job could lose the car too, along with every payment already made. Older writers on republican virtue worried about this very thing. A people that cannot restrain its appetites, they said, will find that its freedom is built on sand.

Both sides were right about something. Credit worked well as long as jobs and wages held steady. It worked badly when they did not. As household debt rose through the 1920s, so did the number of families whose spending depended on income that might vanish. One question hangs over this lesson. What happens to a country built on credit if the credit stops? Within a few years, Americans would learn the answer.
