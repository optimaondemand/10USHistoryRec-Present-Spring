Even at under $300, a Model T was a lot of money for a family whose whole income might be $1,500 a year. Now a new problem appeared. Factories could turn out more cars, radios, and washing machines than people could pay for in cash. If the buyers could not be found, the factories would sit idle. The answer was a new way to pay.

For most of American history, debt carried a stain. Parents taught children the old rule of thrift, which is to save first and buy later. If the buyer missed payments, the seller could take the product back. Ford resisted the idea for years, then gave in and offered his own plan. By the end of the decade, most new cars were sold on credit.

Credit spread to other goods. People bought radios, refrigerators, vacuum cleaners, washing machines, and furniture this way. Economists call these consumer durableskun-SOO-mer DOOR-uh-bulzGoods meant to last for years, such as cars, radios, refrigerators, and washing machines., meaning goods meant to last for years. Electric power was reaching more and more city homes, and families wanted the gadgets that went with it. A new kind of advertising helped. Ads no longer just described a product. They sold a feeling, such as status, comfort, or the fear of being left behind.

Credit even fed a boom in land. In the mid-1920s, thousands of buyers rushed to Florida, putting down small deposits on lots they had never seen. Many planned not to live there at all but to sell the contract to someone else at a higher price. For a while, prices soared, and whole towns went up almost overnight. Then buyers ran short, and in September 1926 a powerful hurricane struck Miami. The boom collapsed. Florida's bust came three years before the nation's, and it was an early warning that a rise in prices fed by borrowed money can fall as quickly as it rose.

The car business also shows how much one leader's choices can matter. Alfred P. He saw that buyers were changing. Once most families owned a car, they wanted a better one. Sloan built a ladder of brands, from the cheap Chevrolet at the bottom to the costly Cadillac at the top, so a family could move up as its income grew. In 1927, General Motors passed Ford as the nation's top seller, and Ford had to close his plants for months to retool. What was Washington doing during these years? Warren Harding died suddenly in August 1923, and Vice President Calvin Coolidge took over. Harding's administration had been stained by scandal, including a bribery case in which a cabinet officer went to prison. Coolidge was quiet and honest, and he restored trust. The Treasury Secretary, Andrew MellonAN-droo MEL-unA very wealthy banker who served as Treasury Secretary in the 1920s and pushed for tax cuts., was one of the richest men in the country. He pushed for tax cuts and smaller government spending. The top tax rate on the highest incomes fell from 73 percent in 1920 to 25 percent by the middle of the decade. Mellon argued that lower taxes would encourage people to invest and build. Many critics replied that the cuts mainly helped the wealthy.

The prosperity was real. Output per worker rose, wages for factory workers rose, and unemployment stayed low for most of the decade. Millions of families lived better than their parents had. Yet not everyone shared in the good times. Farmers had grown rich during the war, but when European farms recovered, prices for crops fell hard. Many farmers were stuck with debts that they had taken on in the boom. Coal miners and textile workers faced low pay and weak demand. Many Black Southerners remained poor. Large parts of the countryside went without electricity. As late as 1930, only about one farm in ten had electric power.

Was credit a good thing? Its defenders said it let ordinary families enjoy comforts that only the rich had once known. They also said that making regular payments taught discipline. It kept factories busy and workers employed. Its critics saw a danger in it. Credit pulls future income into the present. A family that buys a car on installments has promised part of its future pay to someone else. A nation of such families may spend more than it would have, because the bill seems far away. Older writers on republican virtue worried about this very thing. A people that cannot restrain its appetites, they said, will find that its freedom is built on sand.

Both sides were right about something. Credit worked well as long as jobs and wages held steady. It worked badly when they did not. As household debt rose through the 1920s, so did the number of families whose spending depended on income that might vanish. One question hangs over this lesson. What happens to a country built on credit if the credit stops? Within a few years, Americans would learn the answer.

Next week, you will see how the new wealth and the new machines changed American culture, and how those changes divided Americans as well as joined them.
