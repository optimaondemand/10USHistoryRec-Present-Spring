The Unipolar Moment: War, Trade, and Boom

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In 1991, the Cold War ended, and the United States was left as the strongest country in the world. No other nation could match its economy, its military, or its reach. A commentator named Charles Krauthammer called this a "unipolar moment." A unipolar worldyoo-nih-POH-ler WURLDA world with one great power, with no rival of equal strength. is one with a single great power, like a magnet with one pole. It is the opposite of the Cold War, when two superpowers each balanced the other. Some Americans hoped that the moment would last for a long time. Others warned that no country holds such a position forever. In this lesson, you will see how the United States used its power in the years after 1989, and what limits it met.

The first test came in the Middle East. In August 1990, Iraq's dictator, Saddam Husseinsah-DAHM hoo-SAYNThe dictator of Iraq from 1979 until 2003. His invasion of Kuwait led to the Persian Gulf War., sent his army into the small oil-rich country of Kuwait and took it in a few hours. Saddam had just finished a long and costly war with Iran, and he owed large debts to Kuwait. Now he controlled a large share of the world's oil, and his army threatened Saudi Arabia, the greatest oil producer of all. President George H. W. BushJORJ aych DUB-ul-yoo BUSHThe 41st president, from 1989 to 1993. He led the coalition in the Persian Gulf War. was a World War II navy pilot, a former head of the CIA, and an experienced diplomat. He said, "This will not stand."

Bush set out to build a coalitionkoh-uh-LISH-unA group of nations or groups that join together for a common purpose., a group of nations joined for a common purpose. He worked through the United Nations Security Council, where the Soviet Union, in its last year, voted with the United States instead of using its veto. More than thirty countries joined, including Britain, France, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia, and many shared the cost. Bush also asked Congress for approval, and in January 1991, Congress voted to authorize the use of force. The vote was close, 52 to 47 in the Senate. Nothing like this had been seen since Korea: a war fought under a UN resolution, with allies, and with Congress involved. In his address to Congress in September 1990, which you will read this week, Bush spoke of a "new world order," in which nations would work together to oppose aggression.

The Persian Gulf WarPER-zhun GULF WORThe 1991 war in which a coalition led by the United States drove Iraq's army out of Kuwait. began on January 17, 1991, with a huge air campaign. Cruise missiles and stealth planes hit Iraqi targets, and television audiences watched the first "live" war. On February 24, the ground attack began. In about a hundred hours, coalition forces drove Iraq's army out of Kuwait and destroyed a large part of it. American losses were light, fewer than 150 killed in combat, and Iraqi losses were many times higher. Bush then stopped. He did not march on Baghdad to remove Saddam, because he believed that this would break the coalition and leave America in charge of a hostile country. Saddam stayed in power, and he crushed uprisings by Kurds and Shiites that Americans had encouraged. Critics said the war was left unfinished. Defenders said the goal, to free Kuwait, had been reached, and that Bush had avoided a trap. It is a good example of a leader deciding what to leave undone.

Despite the victory, Bush lost the election of 1992. The war had made him popular, but an economic downturn made voters worry about jobs, and a third-party candidate, the Texas businessman Ross Perot, took many votes. The winner was the governor of Arkansas, Bill ClintonBIL KLIN-tunThe 42nd president, from 1993 to 2001. He was a Democrat and the former governor of Arkansas., a young Democrat who promised to focus "like a laser" on the economy. Clinton was a gifted speaker, and he had a quick mind for policy. He also proved to be careful about the political center. He declared that "the era of big government is over," and in 1996 he signed a law that reformed welfare by setting time limits on benefits and requiring work.

Clinton's most important economic decision was to follow Bush on free trade. The North American Free Trade Agreementnorth uh-MAIR-ih-kun FREE trayd uh-GREE-muntA treaty, called NAFTA, that removed most trade barriers among the United States, Canada, and Mexico. It took effect in 1994., or NAFTA, removed most trade barriers among the United States, Canada, and Mexico. Bush had signed it. After a bitter fight in Congress, in which many labor unions and many members of Clinton's own party opposed it, Clinton won its passage in 1993. Supporters said that free trade would lower prices and open new markets. Opponents said it would send factory jobs to Mexico. The truth is mixed. Trade grew sharply, and consumers gained from lower prices. But some towns that depended on manufacturing were hit hard, and the pain was real for the people who lived there. In 1995, the United States also helped create a new international body to set rules for trade among its members, and over the following years, trade with China grew rapidly.

At the same time, the economy took off. Much of the growth came from a new technology. The Internet had been a tool for scientists and the military. In the 1990s, it spread to homes, with the World Wide Web, web browsers, and online companies such as Amazon and Google. Investors poured money into any business with ".com" in its name. The dot-com boomDOT-kom BOOMThe rush of investment in internet companies in the late 1990s that drove the stock market to new heights before many of the companies failed. pushed the stock market to new heights. Unemployment fell to about four percent by 2000, and for the first time in decades, the federal government took in more money than it spent. Not all of this was lasting. In 2000 and 2001, many internet companies failed, and stock prices fell. An old lesson was learned again: speculation can run ahead of reality.

The decade was a test of how America would use its power without a rival. In some places, it acted, as when it helped end the war in Bosnia in 1995 and bombed Serbian forces in Kosovo in 1999. In others, it held back or failed. In 1993, eighteen American soldiers died in Somalia in a street battle in Mogadishu, and Clinton pulled out. In 1994, in Rwanda, about eight hundred thousand people were killed in a genocide, and the world did not act. American leaders drew a hard lesson from Somalia and were reluctant to send troops to places where no vital interest seemed to be at stake. They also wondered what role a country with no rival should play.

For many Americans, the 1990s felt like a time of peace and wealth, a holiday from history. That feeling is the subject of this week's discussion, which asks whether the peace was real or only a pause. The next lesson looks at three signs of trouble that were visible in these years: terrorism, immigration, and a presidential election that was decided in the courts.
