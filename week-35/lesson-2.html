📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The 1990s felt safe, but several dangers were growing in plain sight. This lesson looks at three of them: terrorism, the debate over immigration, and a shaky presidential election. They differ in every way, yet each tested the habits and institutions of the republic.

Terrorism first. Terrorism is the use of violence against civilians to frighten a population and to force a change in policy. Americans had met it before, but in the 1990s, it grew larger. In February 1993, a truck bomb exploded in the garage under the World Trade Center in New York, killing six people and injuring more than a thousand. The towers did not fall. The blast tore a crater several stories deep, and tens of thousands of people made their way down smoky stairwells in the dark. The men behind it were caught, tried in court, and sent to prison. One of them was caught because of a blunder: he went back to the rental company to ask for the deposit on the van that had carried the bomb, and investigators had already traced the van from a piece of it found in the wreckage. Many Americans treated the case as a crime to be punished rather than as a war.

In April 1995, an American-born man named Timothy McVeigh bombed a federal building in Oklahoma City and killed 168 people, showing that terrorists could be homegrown. McVeigh had served in the army in the Gulf War, and he hated the federal government. He was especially angry over the deadly end of a siege at Waco, Texas, exactly two years earlier. He parked a rental truck packed with explosives in front of the building, where a day-care center was on the second floor, and nineteen of the dead were children. Rescue workers dug through the rubble for days. In 1996, Congress passed a law that gave the government new tools against terrorism and limited some appeals in death-penalty cases. Police stopped him on a highway less than two hours later because his car had no license plate, and he was tried, convicted, and put to death in 2001. Neither event changed how most Americans thought about their safety.

A more dangerous enemy was taking shape abroad. Osama bin Ladenoh-SAH-mah bin LAH-denThe founder of al-Qaeda and the planner of the September 11 attacks. He was killed by American forces in 2011. was the son of a rich Saudi family who had joined the Afghan resistance against the Soviets in the 1980s. He founded al-Qaedaal-KY-duhAn Islamist terrorist network founded by Osama bin Laden. It carried out the attacks of September 11, 2001., an Islamist terrorist network, and turned against the United States. He objected above all to the presence of American troops in Saudi Arabia, home to Islam's holiest places, after the Gulf War. When Iraq invaded Kuwait, he had offered to defend Saudi Arabia with his own fighters, and he took it as an insult when the royal family chose American soldiers instead. Saudi Arabia later took away his citizenship, and in 1996, after years in Sudan, he found shelter in Afghanistan. There he paid for training camps with his own money and with gifts from supporters. In February 1998, he and several allies, among them an Egyptian doctor named Ayman al-Zawahiri, issued a fatwaFAHT-wahAn Arabic word for a religious ruling. Bin Laden issued one in 1998 that called for attacks on Americans., which is a religious ruling, declaring that Muslims should kill Americans anywhere. You will read an excerpt this week. Most Muslim scholars and leaders rejected such claims, and the great majority of Muslims, then and now, have nothing to do with terror. But his group acted on its words.

The lesson does not say, so any answer is reasoning rather than fact. One possibility is that he would have found other grievances, since the lesson says he objected 'above all' to the troops, which suggests there were others, and he had already turned against the United States. Another is that his message would have had less appeal. What the lesson does show is that most Muslim scholars and leaders rejected his claims, and that the great majority of Muslims have nothing to do with terror. A cause rarely has only one root, and historians weigh the roots against each other instead of choosing one.

Al-Qaeda attacked in August 1998, setting off truck bombs at the American embassies in Kenya and Tanzania. More than two hundred people died, most of them Africans. The two bombs went off within minutes of each other, in Nairobi and Dar es Salaam, and twelve of the dead were Americans. Thousands more were hurt, many by flying glass. Clinton ordered missile strikes on training camps in Afghanistan and on a factory in Sudan. Bin Laden escaped unharmed.

At the end of 1999, a customs inspector at a ferry port in Washington state grew suspicious of a nervous man arriving from Canada, and found bomb-making materials in the trunk of his car. Officials later learned that he had planned to attack the Los Angeles airport at the turn of the century. In October 2000, suicide bombers in a small boat hit the destroyer USS Cole in a harbor in Yemen, killing seventeen sailors. The boat pulled alongside while the ship was taking on fuel, and the blast tore a huge hole in its side. Officials warned of a bigger threat to come, and the White House's counterterrorism chief, Richard Clarke, pressed for stronger action against al-Qaeda. But there was no agreement on what to do about it, and Americans, tired of war, had little appetite for another one. The government also had trouble sharing information between agencies, such as the CIA and the FBI. Each held some pieces of the picture, and the habits and rules that kept their files apart made it hard to put the pieces together. As you will see in the next lesson, those warnings proved all too accurate.

The second theme was immigration. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, which you read about earlier, had ended the old quota system based on national origin. Under the new law, the main way to win a visa was to have a close relative who was already a citizen or a resident, so one arrival could lead to others. By the 1990s, most new immigrants came from Latin America and Asia, not Europe, and the foreign-born share of the population was rising again, from under five percent in 1970 to about eleven percent by 2000. Many came legally, to join family or to work. In 1990, Congress raised the yearly limit on legal immigration and added visas for skilled workers. Many others entered without permission, mostly across the long border with Mexico.

In 1986, Congress tried to settle the matter with the Immigration Reform and Control Actim-ih-GRAY-shun rih-FORM and kun-TROHL AKTA 1986 law that made it illegal to knowingly hire workers without legal status, and that gave amnesty to nearly three million people already living in the country., which President Reagan signed. It made it illegal for employers to knowingly hire people without legal status, and it required them to check each new worker's papers. It also offered amnestyAM-nes-teeA general pardon that a government gives to a group of people for breaking a law., a general pardon, to nearly three million people who had been in the country since 1982. Supporters expected that the law would stop the flow of illegal immigration. It did not. Employers found ways around the new rules, since fake papers were easy to buy and an employer needed only to see papers that looked genuine. Enforcement was weak, and the numbers grew. By 2000, researchers estimated that around eight million people were living in the country without legal status.

Put these events in order, from the earliest to the latest. Tap them one at a time.

    The debate was hard because both sides had a point. Those who wanted more immigration said that newcomers had always built the country, that they took jobs few others wanted, and that they brought energy and ambition. Those who wanted less said that a nation must control its own borders, that the rule of law should not reward those who break it, and that very large numbers made it harder to join newcomers into one people. A classical republican would see a genuine problem in both views: a republic needs citizens who share loyalty to its common good, and it also needs the talents of new people. The first part, becoming one people, has always been the work of schools, churches, and daily life, more than of laws. In 1994, California voters passed a measure to cut off public services to illegal immigrants, which a court later struck down. About three of every five voters had supported it. That same year, the Border Patrol began Operation Gatekeeper, which added fences, lights, and agents near San Diego. Crossings did not stop. They shifted east into the desert, where the trip was more dangerous. The argument continued into the next century, and it is still unsettled.

    The third theme was the election of 2000. Vice President Al Gore, a Democrat, ran against George W. BushJORJ DUB-ul-yoo BUSHThe 43rd president, from 2001 to 2009. He was the governor of Texas and the son of the 41st president., the governor of Texas and the son of the former president. The race was extremely close. On election night, networks called Florida for Gore, then for Bush, and then said it was too close to call. Gore telephoned Bush to concede, then called back later to take it back as the Florida count tightened. Whoever won Florida would win the Electoral Collegeih-lek-TOR-ul KOL-ijThe system in which each state casts electoral votes for president. A candidate needs a majority of the 538 votes to win., the system by which each state casts electoral votes, and the candidate with a majority of the 538 votes becomes president. In nearly every state, the winner of the state's popular vote takes all of its electors, and Florida had 25. Gore had won more votes nationwide, by about half a million, as had happened only a few times before, but Bush led in Florida by only a few hundred votes. A Green Party candidate, Ralph Nader, won about 97,000 votes in Florida, many times the gap between the two leaders.

    Florida law required a machine recountREE-kountA second counting of ballots to check the result of an election., which is a second counting of the ballots. Then Gore asked for hand recounts in several counties. Ballots there had been punched with a stylus, and some were only partly punched. The tiny bits of paper became known as "hanging chads" and "dimpled chads." Officials argued over how to decide the voters' intent. Florida's counties also used different kinds of voting machines, so the same mark on a ballot might be counted in one county and thrown out in another. In Palm Beach County, voters had also complained about the "butterfly ballot," on which the candidates' names ran down two facing pages with a single column of holes between them, and some said they had punched the wrong hole. Both sides sent lawyers, and the matter moved through the state courts for five weeks.

    In the end, it reached the Supreme Court. On December 8, the Florida Supreme Court ordered a hand count of disputed ballots across the state. The next day, the U.S. Supreme Court stopped that count while it heard the case. Federal law set December 12 as the date by which a state should settle any disputes over its electors, so the clock was running out. The electors were due to meet in their states on December 18 to cast the votes. In Bush v. GoreBUSH VER-sus GORThe 2000 Supreme Court case that stopped the Florida recount and decided the presidential election., on December 12, the Court ruled five to four that the recount, as it was being done, did not give equal treatment to all voters, and that there was no time to set up a fair one. Seven of the nine justices agreed that counting ballots by different standards in different counties raised a problem of equal treatment, but only five agreed that there was no time to fix it. The recount stopped, and Bush won Florida by 537 votes and the presidency by a margin of 271 electoral votes to 266. One elector from the District of Columbia left her ballot blank in protest, which is why the totals do not add up to 538.

    ⚡ HOW MUCH DID SMALL DETAILS MATTER?
    The way paper ballots were punched in Florida became a fight in court. How much did small details like this matter to the result of the election?

    Many people felt that the outcome was unfair. Gore's supporters said that the Court had stopped a count of the ballots, and Bush's supporters said that Florida officials had followed the law. Yet something notable happened next. Gore accepted the result, and said that he disagreed with the decision but would respect it. He spoke to the country on television on the evening of December 13, and Bush, speaking from the Texas capitol, promised to work with members of both parties. A few weeks later, when Congress met to count the electoral votes, it fell to Gore, as president of the Senate, to announce his own defeat. Some members of the House stood to object, but because no senator joined them, Gore ruled the objections out of order. On January 20, 2001, Bush took the oath of office. The transfer of power took place in peace, and no one called out the army. That fact says much about the health of the republic. The country had been through a very close election, in the hands of lawyers and judges, and it held together. Within a year, it would be tested in a much harsher way.
