📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The 1990s felt safe, but several dangers were growing in plain sight. This lesson looks at three of them: terrorism, the debate over immigration, and a shaky presidential election. They differ in every way, yet each tested the habits and institutions of the republic.

Terrorism first. Terrorism is the use of violence against civilians to frighten a population and to force a change in policy. Americans had met it before, but in the 1990s, it grew larger. In February 1993, a truck bomb exploded in the garage under the World Trade Center in New York, killing six people and injuring more than a thousand. The towers did not fall. The men behind it were caught, tried in court, and sent to prison, and many Americans treated the case as a crime to be punished rather than as a war. In April 1995, an American-born man named Timothy McVeigh bombed a federal building in Oklahoma City and killed 168 people, showing that terrorists could be homegrown. Neither event changed how most Americans thought about their safety.

A more dangerous enemy was taking shape abroad. Osama bin Ladenoh-SAH-mah bin LAH-denThe founder of al-Qaeda and the planner of the September 11 attacks. He was killed by American forces in 2011. was the son of a rich Saudi family who had joined the Afghan resistance against the Soviets in the 1980s. He founded al-Qaedaal-KY-duhAn Islamist terrorist network founded by Osama bin Laden. It carried out the attacks of September 11, 2001., an Islamist terrorist network, and turned against the United States. He objected above all to the presence of American troops in Saudi Arabia, home to Islam's holiest places, after the Gulf War. In February 1998, he and several allies issued a fatwaFAHT-wahAn Arabic word for a religious ruling. Bin Laden issued one in 1998 that called for attacks on Americans., which is a religious ruling, declaring that Muslims should kill Americans anywhere. You will read an excerpt this week. Most Muslim scholars and leaders rejected such claims, and the great majority of Muslims, then and now, have nothing to do with terror. But his group acted on its words.

Al-Qaeda attacked in August 1998, setting off truck bombs at the American embassies in Kenya and Tanzania. More than two hundred people died, most of them Africans. Clinton ordered missile strikes on training camps in Afghanistan and on a factory in Sudan. In October 2000, suicide bombers in a small boat hit the destroyer USS Cole in a harbor in Yemen, killing seventeen sailors. Officials warned of a bigger threat to come. But there was no agreement on what to do about it, and Americans, tired of war, had little appetite for another one. The government also had trouble sharing information between agencies, such as the CIA and the FBI. As you will see in the next lesson, those warnings proved all too accurate.

The second theme was immigration. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, which you read about earlier, had ended the old quota system based on national origin. By the 1990s, most new immigrants came from Latin America and Asia, not Europe, and the foreign-born share of the population was rising again. Many came legally, to join family or to work. Many others entered without permission, mostly across the long border with Mexico. In 1986, Congress tried to settle the matter with the Immigration Reform and Control Actim-ih-GRAY-shun rih-FORM and kun-TROHL AKTA 1986 law that made it illegal to knowingly hire workers without legal status, and that gave amnesty to nearly three million people already living in the country.. It made it illegal for employers to knowingly hire people without legal status. It also offered amnestyAM-nes-teeA general pardon that a government gives to a group of people for breaking a law., a general pardon, to nearly three million people who had been in the country since 1982. Supporters expected that the law would stop the flow of illegal immigration. It did not. Employers found ways around the new rules, enforcement was weak, and the numbers grew.

The debate was hard because both sides had a point. Those who wanted more immigration said that newcomers had always built the country, that they took jobs few others wanted, and that they brought energy and ambition. Those who wanted less said that a nation must control its own borders, that the rule of law should not reward those who break it, and that very large numbers made it harder to join newcomers into one people. A classical republican would see a genuine problem in both views: a republic needs citizens who share loyalty to its common good, and it also needs the talents of new people. The first part, becoming one people, has always been the work of schools, churches, and daily life, more than of laws. In 1994, California voters passed a measure to cut off public services to illegal immigrants, which a court later struck down. The argument continued into the next century, and it is still unsettled.

The third theme was the election of 2000. Vice President Al Gore, a Democrat, ran against George W. BushJORJ DUB-ul-yoo BUSHThe 43rd president, from 2001 to 2009. He was the governor of Texas and the son of the 41st president., the governor of Texas and the son of the former president. The race was extremely close. On election night, networks called Florida for Gore, then for Bush, and then said it was too close to call. Whoever won Florida would win the Electoral Collegeih-lek-TOR-ul KOL-ijThe system in which each state casts electoral votes for president. A candidate needs a majority of the 538 votes to win., the system by which each state casts electoral votes, and the candidate with a majority of the 538 votes becomes president. Gore had won more votes nationwide, as had happened only a few times before, but Bush led in Florida by only a few hundred votes.

Florida law required a machine recountREE-kountA second counting of ballots to check the result of an election., which is a second counting of the ballots. Then Gore asked for hand recounts in several counties. Ballots there had been punched with a stylus, and some were only partly punched. The tiny bits of paper became known as "hanging chads" and "dimpled chads." Officials argued over how to decide the voters' intent. Both sides sent lawyers, and the matter moved through the state courts for five weeks. In the end, it reached the Supreme Court. In Bush v. GoreBUSH VER-sus GORThe 2000 Supreme Court case that stopped the Florida recount and decided the presidential election., on December 12, the Court ruled five to four that the recount, as it was being done, did not give equal treatment to all voters, and that there was no time to set up a fair one. The recount stopped, and Bush won Florida by 537 votes and the presidency by a margin of 271 electoral votes to 266.

Many people felt that the outcome was unfair. Gore's supporters said that the Court had stopped a count of the ballots, and Bush's supporters said that Florida officials had followed the law. Yet something notable happened next. Gore accepted the result, and said that he disagreed with the decision but would respect it. The transfer of power took place in peace, and no one called out the army. That fact says much about the health of the republic. The country had been through a very close election, in the hands of lawyers and judges, and it held together. Within a year, it would be tested in a much harsher way.
