September 11 and the War on Terror

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On the morning of Tuesday, September 11, 2001, nineteen men from al-Qaeda hijacked four passenger airplanes. They flew two of the planes into the twin towers of the World Trade Center in New York, and both towers collapsed within two hours. A third plane struck the Pentagon, the headquarters of the American military, near Washington. The fourth was headed toward the Capitol or the White House, but its passengers, who had learned of the other attacks by telephone, fought back, and it crashed in a field in Pennsylvania. Nearly three thousand people died, the greatest loss of life in an attack on American soil in the nation's history. Hundreds of the dead were firefighters and police officers who had run into the buildings to save others.

President George W. Bush was reading to schoolchildren in Florida when he was told. That evening, he spoke to the nation and said that America would make no distinction between the terrorists and those who harbored them. Three days later, he stood in the rubble in New York with a bullhorn, and a rescue worker shouted that he could not hear him. "I can hear you," Bush answered. "The rest of the world hears you." The country was united as it had not been since Pearl Harbor. Flags appeared on houses and cars, and lines formed to donate blood.

Congress acted quickly. On September 14, it passed the Authorization for Use of Military Forceaw-thor-ih-ZAY-shun for YOOS uv MIL-ih-tair-ee FORSThe resolution Congress passed on September 14, 2001, that allowed the president to use force against those who planned or helped the attacks., a resolution that allowed the president to use "all necessary and appropriate force" against those who had planned or helped the attacks. It passed almost unanimously, 98 to 0 in the Senate and 420 to 1 in the House. It was broad and it had no end date. You may remember the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, which also gave a president wide powers in a short time. Some critics have argued that the new law, like the old one, was stretched far beyond what its authors expected, and that Congress gave up too much of its role.

The intelligence pointed to Osama bin Laden, who was sheltered in Afghanistan by the TalibanTAL-ih-banA strict Islamist group that ruled most of Afghanistan from the late 1990s until 2001 and sheltered al-Qaeda., a strict Islamist group that had taken control of most of the country in the 1990s. Bush demanded that the Taliban hand him over. They refused. In October 2001, American planes began to bomb Afghanistan, and American special forces joined Afghan fighters on the ground, with allies from Britain and other countries. The Taliban government fell within weeks. But bin Laden escaped into the mountains near Pakistan, and the Taliban was not destroyed. It regrouped, and a long fight began.

At home, the government moved to prevent another attack. In October 2001, Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Actyoo-ess-ay PAY-tree-ut AKTA 2001 law that gave police and intelligence agencies new powers to track suspected terrorists and share information., which gave law-enforcement and intelligence agencies new powers to track suspects, to share information between agencies, and to tap phones and watch communications. In 2002, Congress created the Department of Homeland Securitydih-PART-munt uv HOHM-land sih-KYOOR-ih-teeA department created in 2002 that brought together twenty-two agencies to protect the country from attack., which brought together twenty-two agencies, including the Coast Guard and the Secret Service, into one department. Airports installed new security, and a new agency took over screening of passengers. These measures were popular at first. Over time, however, a debate grew about whether the government had gone too far in gathering information on its own citizens. Supporters said that no further attack of this size occurred on American soil for many years, and that the powers were needed. Critics argued that they threatened liberty and the Fourth Amendment's protection against unreasonable searches. A classical republic has always had to balance order against freedom, and this period is a fresh case.

The second war was far more controversial. In 2002, the Bush administration argued that Iraq, under Saddam Hussein, had weapons of mass destructionWEP-unz uv MASS dih-STRUK-shunNuclear, chemical, or biological weapons that can kill large numbers of people., a term for nuclear, chemical, or biological weapons that can kill large numbers of people, and that he might give them to terrorists. Saddam had used chemical weapons in the past, and he had refused to let inspectors work freely. In October 2002, Congress authorized the use of force. In March 2003, American and British troops invaded Iraq. They took Baghdad in three weeks, and Saddam was captured that December. In May, Bush stood on an aircraft carrier beneath a banner that read "Mission Accomplished."

But the mission was not accomplished. No stockpiles of weapons of mass destruction were found, and the claim that had been made to justify the war proved to be mistaken. A major report later concluded that the intelligence had been faulty. More important, the American government had no plan for governing the country after Saddam's fall. Looting broke out, and the army was disbanded. An insurgencyin-SUR-jun-seeAn armed rebellion against an occupying power or a new government., an armed rebellion against an occupying power or a new government, spread, and for years, the war turned into a struggle against both Sunni and Shiite militias and foreign fighters. By 2006, Iraq was near civil war. In 2007, Bush ordered a "surge" of about thirty thousand more troops and a new strategy of protecting the population. Violence fell. The war in Iraq cost the lives of nearly 4,500 American service members and many times that number of Iraqis.

Barack Obamabuh-RAHK oh-BAH-muhThe 44th president, from 2009 to 2017, and the first African American president., an Illinois senator who had opposed the Iraq War from the beginning, won the election of 2008 and became the first African American president. He withdrew most American troops from Iraq in 2011, and he sent more to Afghanistan. On May 2, 2011, a team of Navy SEALs killed Osama bin Laden in a compound in Pakistan. Yet the Taliban remained in the field. The war in Afghanistan was now the longest in American history. In 2021, under President Joe Biden, the last American troops left in August, and the Taliban took Kabul within days. Some twenty-four hundred Americans had died there, along with many thousands of Afghans.

The war on terror left a mixed record. There was no second attack on the scale of September 11, and al-Qaeda's leadership was broken. But the wars were long, costly, and in the end, uncertain. Contingency is clear here: if the intelligence about Iraq had been correct, or if the occupation had been better planned, events might have gone differently. The great questions of this week reach beyond these wars. What should America's goals be in the world, and what should it not try to do? The next lesson takes up those larger questions.
