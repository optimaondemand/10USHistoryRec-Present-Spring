📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On the morning of Tuesday, September 11, 2001, nineteen men from al-Qaeda hijacked four passenger airplanes. The planes had left airports in Boston, Newark, and Washington, bound for California, and each carried a heavy load of fuel. The hijackers, armed with knives and box cutters, took control of the cockpits within an hour of takeoff. They flew two of the planes into the twin towers of the World Trade Center in New York, and both towers collapsed within two hours. A third plane struck the Pentagon, the headquarters of the American military, near Washington. Inside the building, 125 people died. The fourth was headed toward the Capitol or the White House, but its passengers, who had learned of the other attacks by telephone, fought back, and it crashed in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, and no one aboard survived. Nearly three thousand people died, the greatest loss of life in an attack on American soil in the nation's history. Hundreds of the dead were firefighters and police officers who had run into the buildings to save others. The New York Fire Department alone lost 343 firefighters. Later that morning, for the first time in history, the government ordered every airplane in the country to land, and thousands of planes came down within hours.

President George W. Bush was reading to schoolchildren in a Florida classroom when his chief of staff leaned in and whispered that a second plane had hit the second tower and that America was under attack. Bush stayed a few more minutes, then left. Security officers, fearing that Washington was a target, kept him moving on Air Force One, and he did not return to the capital until that evening. That evening, he spoke to the nation and said that America would make no distinction between the terrorists and those who harbored them. Three days later, he stood in the rubble in New York with a bullhorn, and a rescue worker shouted that he could not hear him. "I can hear you," Bush answered. "The rest of the world hears you." Fires in the rubble of the towers would burn for more than three months. The country was united as it had not been since Pearl Harbor. Flags appeared on houses and cars, and lines formed to donate blood. On the night of the attacks, members of both parties gathered on the steps of the Capitol and sang "God Bless America." Within weeks, Bush's approval rating climbed above ninety percent, the highest Gallup had ever recorded.

Congress acted quickly. On September 14, it passed the Authorization for Use of Military Forceaw-thor-ih-ZAY-shun for YOOS uv MIL-ih-tair-ee FORSThe resolution Congress passed on September 14, 2001, that allowed the president to use force against those who planned or helped the attacks., a resolution that allowed the president to use "all necessary and appropriate force" against those who had planned or helped the attacks. It passed almost unanimously, 98 to 0 in the Senate and 420 to 1 in the House. The one member who voted no, Representative Barbara Lee of California, warned that the resolution was too open-ended, and many people were angry with her for saying so. It was broad and it had no end date. It was also unusual. The Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war, but this law named no country. It named people, those who had planned or helped the attacks, and over the years, presidents of both parties cited it for operations in a number of places. You may remember the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, which also gave a president wide powers in a short time. Some critics have argued that the new law, like the old one, was stretched far beyond what its authors expected, and that Congress gave up too much of its role.

The intelligence pointed to Osama bin Laden, who was sheltered in Afghanistan by the TalibanTAL-ih-banA strict Islamist group that ruled most of Afghanistan from the late 1990s until 2001 and sheltered al-Qaeda., a strict Islamist group that had taken control of most of the country in the 1990s. The Taliban ruled harshly: girls were barred from school, and women from most work. Bush demanded that the Taliban hand him over. They refused. In October 2001, American planes began to bomb Afghanistan, and American special forces joined Afghan fighters on the ground, with allies from Britain and other countries. Some of the first American soldiers to fight there rode horses beside Afghan commanders, calling in air strikes by radio. Kabul fell in mid-November, and Kandahar, the Taliban's stronghold, in December. The Taliban government fell within weeks.

But bin Laden escaped into the mountains near Pakistan, slipping away during the fighting at Tora Bora in December, where Afghan fighters did most of the ground fighting. And the Taliban was not destroyed. Many of its fighters simply went home or crossed into Pakistan, where they found safe places to rest and rearm. That month, at a conference in Germany, Afghan leaders chose Hamid Karzai to head a new government, and the United States and its allies stayed to protect it. American and allied troops stayed to hunt the remaining fighters and to train a new Afghan army. NATO later took command of the international force. The Taliban regrouped, and a long fight began.

At home, the government moved to prevent another attack. In October 2001, Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Actyoo-ess-ay PAY-tree-ut AKTA 2001 law that gave police and intelligence agencies new powers to track suspected terrorists and share information., by 98 to 1 in the Senate and 357 to 66 in the House. It gave law-enforcement and intelligence agencies new powers to track suspects, to share information between agencies, and to tap phones and watch communications. The sharing rules aimed at a gap like the one described in the last lesson, where the CIA and the FBI each held pieces of a picture that no one put together. In 2002, Congress created the Department of Homeland Securitydih-PART-munt uv HOHM-land sih-KYOOR-ih-teeA department created in 2002 that brought together twenty-two agencies to protect the country from attack., which brought together twenty-two agencies, including the Coast Guard and the Secret Service, into one department. Airports installed new security, and a new agency, the Transportation Security Administration, took over screening of passengers. Before then, private companies had done the screening, and now the screeners became federal employees. These measures were popular at first. Over time, however, a debate grew about whether the government had gone too far in gathering information on its own citizens. Supporters said that no further attack of this size occurred on American soil for many years, and that the powers were needed. Critics argued that they threatened liberty and the Fourth Amendment's protection against unreasonable searches. A classical republic has always had to balance order against freedom, and this period is a fresh case.

At first, the measures were popular. Over time, a debate grew about whether the government had gone too far in gathering information on its own citizens.

Two parts of the debate drew special attention. In January 2002, the military opened a prison for men captured in the war at the American naval base at Guantanamo Bay, Cuba. About 780 men were held there over the years. Many were held for years without trial, and most were later released without ever being tried. The courts ruled that they had the right to challenge their detention. Supporters said that the men were dangerous enemies who could not be held in ordinary jails. Critics said that holding people without charges was not how a nation of laws should act. The other part was surveillance. In 2013, a former government contractor named Edward Snowden revealed that the National Security Agency had been collecting the records of Americans' telephone calls in bulk. Officials said the records helped them look for plots, and critics said the program gathered far more than it needed. Congress ended that program in 2015.

The second war was far more controversial. In 2002, the Bush administration argued that Iraq, under Saddam Hussein, had weapons of mass destructionWEP-unz uv MASS dih-STRUK-shunNuclear, chemical, or biological weapons that can kill large numbers of people., a term for nuclear, chemical, or biological weapons that can kill large numbers of people, and that he might give them to terrorists. Saddam had used chemical weapons in the past, and he had refused to let inspectors work freely. In October 2002, Congress authorized the use of force, by 77 to 23 in the Senate and 296 to 133 in the House, with members of both parties on each side of the vote. In February 2003, Secretary of State Colin Powell laid out the case before the United Nations Security Council, but the Council did not vote to approve a war, and France, Germany, and Russia asked for more time for the inspectors. In March 2003, American and British troops invaded Iraq. They took Baghdad in three weeks, and Saddam was captured that December, found hiding in a hole near his hometown. An Iraqi court later tried him, and he was hanged in December 2006. In May, Bush stood on an aircraft carrier beneath a banner that read "Mission Accomplished."

The lesson gives both sides. Officials said the records helped them look for plots, and critics said the program gathered far more than it needed. Congress ended it in 2015. Your questions might be whether the records really helped find plots, what was gathered beyond what was needed, and who checks that such a power is not misused. The lesson does not settle those questions, so any answer is reasoning rather than fact. A republic has always had to balance order against freedom, and thoughtful people weigh the two differently.

But the mission was not accomplished. No stockpiles of weapons of mass destruction were found, and the claim that had been made to justify the war proved to be mistaken. A major report later concluded that the intelligence had been faulty. More important, the American government had no plan for governing the country after Saddam's fall. Looting broke out, and the army was disbanded. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers who knew how to use weapons were suddenly out of work and out of a paycheck, and many held a grudge. An insurgencyin-SUR-jun-seeAn armed rebellion against an occupying power or a new government., an armed rebellion against an occupying power or a new government, spread, and for years, the war turned into a struggle against both Sunni and Shiite militias and foreign fighters. In 2004, photographs from Abu Ghraib prison near Baghdad showed American soldiers abusing Iraqi prisoners. They shocked Americans and damaged the country's standing in the world, and some of the soldiers were punished. By 2006, Iraq was near civil war. In February of that year, a bomb destroyed the golden dome of a Shiite shrine at Samarra, and a wave of revenge killings followed. In 2007, Bush ordered a "surge" of about thirty thousand more troops and a new strategy of protecting the population. At the same time, many Sunni tribal leaders in Anbar Province turned against the foreign fighters among them and joined the Americans. Violence fell. At the peak of the surge, about 170,000 American troops were in Iraq. The war in Iraq cost the lives of nearly 4,500 American service members and many times that number of Iraqis.

Barack Obamabuh-RAHK oh-BAH-muhThe 44th president, from 2009 to 2017, and the first African American president., an Illinois senator who had opposed the Iraq War from the beginning, won the election of 2008 and became the first African American president. He withdrew most American troops from Iraq in 2011, on the schedule that Bush had agreed to with Iraq's government in 2008, and he sent more to Afghanistan. In December 2009, he announced that thirty thousand more troops were on their way, and the American force there rose to about a hundred thousand. On May 2, 2011, a team of Navy SEALs killed Osama bin Laden in a compound in the town of Abbottabad, Pakistan. In 2014, a new terror group, the Islamic State, seized large parts of Iraq and Syria, and American forces returned to Iraq in small numbers to help fight it.

Yet the Taliban remained in the field. The war in Afghanistan was now the longest in American history. In February 2020, the administration of President Donald Trump signed an agreement with the Taliban that promised the withdrawal of American troops, and in 2021, President Joe Biden carried it out. The Taliban took Kabul on August 15, and the last American troops left on August 30. In those two weeks, American planes flew out more than 100,000 people, but a suicide bomber at the Kabul airport killed thirteen American service members and scores of Afghans. Some twenty-four hundred Americans had died in the war, along with many thousands of Afghans.

The war on terror left a mixed record. There was no second attack on the scale of September 11, and al-Qaeda's leadership was broken. But the wars were long, costly, and in the end, uncertain. Contingency is clear here: if the intelligence about Iraq had been correct, or if the occupation had been better planned, events might have gone differently. The great questions of this week reach beyond these wars. What should America's goals be in the world, and what should it not try to do? The next lesson takes up those larger questions.
