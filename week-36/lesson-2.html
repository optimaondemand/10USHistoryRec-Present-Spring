📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In the years after September 11, the world changed in ways that few people had expected. The unipolar moment faded. Other nations grew stronger, America's economy took a heavy blow, and the American people grew sharply divided about the country's place in the world. Because these events are recent, historians have less distance from them, and the facts are still disputed. Still, the main trends can be traced.

The first blow was economic. In the early 2000s, banks lent huge sums for home mortgages, many to buyers who could not afford them. Prices of homes rose quickly, and investors bought complicated financial products that depended on mortgages being repaid. Banks bundled thousands of mortgages into securities and sold shares of them around the world, and rating agencies stamped many of them as safe. Many buyers held loans whose payments jumped after the first few years. When the housing bubble burst in 2007 and 2008, those debts could not be paid, and large banks and investment firms faced collapse. Millions of homeowners found that they owed more on their houses than the houses were worth. On September 15, 2008, Lehman Brothers, a giant investment bank, failed. Credit froze, and stock prices fell by about half.

Congress and the Federal Reserve rescued banks and some other firms with hundreds of billions of dollars, which was bitterly unpopular. The Federal Reserve cut interest rates to nearly zero. The first rescue bill, for 700 billion dollars, failed in the House on September 29, and the stock market lost about 700 points in a single day. A revised bill passed within days, and President Bush signed it. The government also lent money to General Motors and Chrysler, first under Bush and then under Obama, and both automakers survived. Early in 2009, President Obama signed a stimulus law of about 787 billion dollars. Many Americans asked why firms that had taken reckless risks were saved while families lost their homes, and the anger fed movements as different as the Tea Party and Occupy Wall Street. In 2010, Congress tightened the rules for banks in a law called Dodd-Frank. Some said it did not go far enough, and others said it went too far. The result was the Great RecessionGRAYT rih-SESH-unThe severe economic downturn that began with the financial crisis of 2007 and 2008. It was the worst since the 1930s., the worst economic downturn since the 1930s. Millions of people lost their jobs and their homes, and unemployment rose to ten percent in 2009. The recession officially ended in June 2009, but recovery was slow, and many families did not feel it for years. The crisis damaged Americans' trust in banks, in the government, and in the experts who had said that such a thing could not happen.

While America fought in the Middle East and recovered from the crisis, China was rising. After Mao's death in 1976, its leaders, led by Deng Xiaoping, had begun to allow private farming and private business, while keeping the Communist Party in firm control. They opened special zones, such as Shenzhen, where foreign companies could build factories. China joined the World Trade OrganizationWURLD TRAYD or-guh-nih-ZAY-shunAn international body, founded in 1995, that sets rules for trade among its member nations., the body that sets rules for trade between nations, in 2001. Its factories grew rapidly, and its goods filled American stores. Within about two decades, China went from a poor country to the second-largest economy in the world, passing Japan in 2010, and hundreds of millions of its people rose out of extreme poverty. In 2008, Beijing hosted the Summer Olympics, a showcase of its new wealth. It also built a modern army and navy, and it became one of the largest holders of American government debt. China's leaders, especially Xi Jinping, who took power in 2012, claimed a larger role in Asia, built islands in the South China Sea, and expanded the country's reach with loans and projects across Africa, Asia, and Europe. In 2018, Xi removed the limit on how long he could serve. China also claimed the island of Taiwan, a self-governing democracy that the United States has long armed without formally recognizing it as a country, and it tightened its control of Hong Kong. Americans had expected that trade would make China freer and more like the West. That did not happen, and the debate over how to deal with the country, whether by cooperation, competition, or both, became one of the leading questions of American foreign policy. Presidents of both parties placed tariffs on Chinese goods or limited sales of advanced technology to China.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST
Many Americans expected that trade would make China freer and more like the West. That did not happen.
Suppose it had happened. How might the debate over China have looked? Decide first and jot down your guess before you open this.
The lesson does not say, so any answer is reasoning rather than fact. One possibility is that the debate would have been about cooperation, with less talk of rivalry. Another is that China would still have grown stronger and sought a larger role, and the argument would have been about how to respond. What the lesson does show is that Americans held an expectation, and when it failed, the question became one of cooperation, competition, or both. A forecast can shape policy, and a forecast can be wrong.

Russia also returned to the stage. After the Soviet collapse, the country had suffered years of poverty and disorder. In 2000, Vladimir Putin, a former KGB officer, became its leader. In the years that followed, he held power as president or as prime minister. Over time, he rebuilt the state and the army and set aside many of the freedoms that had come with the fall of communism. He spoke of the end of the Soviet Union as a catastrophe. In 2008, Russia fought a short war with Georgia, a small neighbor that had also been part of the Soviet Union, and it kept control of two breakaway regions there. Ukraine, another former Soviet republic, had given up the nuclear weapons left on its soil in 1994, in return for promises from Russia, Britain, and the United States to respect its borders. In 2014, after protesters in Kyiv drove out a Ukrainian president who favored Russia, Russian soldiers in uniforms with no badges took over Crimea. Russia then seized it from Ukraine in an annexationan-ek-SAY-shunThe formal taking of a territory by a stronger country., the formal taking of a territory by a stronger power, and backed a rebellion in eastern Ukraine. Most countries refused to accept the change.

In February 2022, Russia launched a full invasion of Ukraine, the largest war in Europe since 1945. Russian columns drove toward the capital, Kyiv, but Ukrainian forces stopped them. Ukraine's president, Volodymyr Zelensky, a former comedian, stayed in Kyiv and spoke to his people by video. The war settled into a long and bloody fight in the east and south. Millions of Ukrainians fled their homes. The United States and its NATO allies responded with sanctions and with arms for Ukraine, but they did not send their own troops. Americans argued over how much help to give and for how long. Finland and Sweden, neutral for decades, joined NATO. Many of the old ideas of the Cold War, such as deterrence and the danger of nuclear escalation, returned to debate.

Other countries also gained weight. India, Brazil, Turkey, and the oil-rich states of the Gulf pursued their own interests and refused to take sides. In 2023, India passed China as the most populous country. Terrorist groups and cyberattackers could do damage without armies. In 2021, hackers shut down the Colonial Pipeline, which carries fuel to much of the East Coast, and drivers lined up at gas stations that had run dry. Today people speak of a multipolar worldmul-tih-POH-ler WURLDA world in which several great and middle powers, not just one or two, shape events., one in which several great and middle powers, rather than one or two, shape events. In a multipolar world, no single country can set the rules. America remains very strong, with the world's largest economy by many measures and a military unmatched in its reach, but it can no longer expect to get its way simply by asking. History shows that this is the usual condition of nations. Some thinkers believe the moment of unrivaled power was the exception.

⚡ HOW UNUSUAL WAS IT?
Look across history. How unusual were the years when one country had no rival?

At home, Americans grew more divided. By many measures, the two parties were farther apart on basic questions than in decades, and fewer voters moved between them. Scholars call this polarizationpoh-ler-ih-ZAY-shunA division of a society into opposing camps that distrust each other., a division of a society into opposing camps that distrust each other. The causes are argued: changes in the news and in social media, where people tend to hear only those who agree with them; the sorting of voters by region, income, and education; and the long sense that institutions had failed. The smartphone, introduced in 2007, put news and arguments in every pocket, and Facebook, which began in 2004, reached billions of users. Several elections in this period were decided by narrow margins, and disputes over results and over the rules of voting grew sharper.

In 2016, Donald Trump won the Electoral College, while Hillary Clinton won about three million more votes nationwide, as Al Gore had in 2000. In 2020, a global pandemic of COVID-19 killed more than a million Americans, closed schools and businesses, and brought new fights over how to balance health and freedom. Vaccines were ready within a year of the outbreak. That November, Joe Biden defeated Trump, and many of Trump's supporters believed that the result was wrong. Election officials in every state certified it, and courts rejected dozens of challenges. On January 6, 2021, as Congress met to count the electoral votes, a crowd of Trump's supporters broke into the Capitol and stopped the count for hours. Congress finished its work early the next morning, and Biden took office on January 20. A republic can survive disagreement, but it needs enough shared affection among its citizens to settle quarrels peacefully. Many of the thinkers you have studied, from Aristotle to Tocqueville, would say that this habit is the true foundation of self-government.

⚡ PUT IT IN ORDER
Put these events in order. Tap them one at a time.

    What should America do in such a world? Here, Americans disagree, and thoughtful people hold different views. Some believe that the United States should lead and defend an open order of alliances and trade, because the price of withdrawal would be higher than the cost of leadership. Others believe that America has overreached, that it should concentrate on its own borders, economy, and people, and that it should avoid foreign wars that do not serve vital interests. Others seek a middle path: keep the alliances that matter most, use force rarely, and strengthen the country at home as the basis of strength abroad. Members of both parties have held each of these views. Supporters of the first point to NATO, whose members have kept the peace among themselves for more than seventy years, and supporters of the second point to the lives and money spent in Iraq and Afghanistan.

    This old argument stretches back to the beginning of the republic. In 1796, as he left office, George Washington wrote a Farewell Address to the American people. He had served two terms and could have won a third, but he chose to step down, which set a custom that lasted until the 1940s. He never spoke it aloud. It appeared in a Philadelphia newspaper in September, and Alexander Hamilton had helped him with the writing. Washington had watched Americans split over the wars between Britain and France, and in 1793 he had declared that the United States would stay neutral. In the address, he warned against "permanent alliances" with foreign nations, and he advised the country to extend its trade but to have "as little political connection as possible" with others. He also urged citizens to guard their unity, and to avoid party spirit, which he feared could be used by foreign powers and ambitious men. You will read excerpts this week. Washington did not say that America should never act abroad; he said it should do so for its own interests and its own peace, and he expected the United States to grow strong enough to choose its course. Notice that the question of how far to go in the world and the question of unity at home are bound together in his advice. The Senate still reads the address aloud every year near his birthday. Whether it is wise for the twenty-first century is for you to decide.

    ⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

    You have now followed the American story from the end of Reconstruction to the present day. You have seen a republic survive wars, depressions, and scandals, and you have seen it make serious mistakes. You have seen how leaders' choices, and sometimes chance, changed events. As you take up the final discussion, remember that you are now one of the people who will help to decide what comes next. The country you have studied is a trust, and it will pass to you.
