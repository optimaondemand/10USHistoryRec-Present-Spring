The Pacific: Midway to Iwo Jima

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor was only one part of a larger plan. In the same weeks, Japanese forces struck the Philippines, Malaya, Hong Kong, and the Dutch East Indies. They won victory after victory. By the spring of 1942, Japan ruled a huge empire that stretched from the borders of India to islands near Australia. Americans had agreed on a policy of Europe FirstYUR-up furstThe Allied plan to defeat Germany before Japan., so the forces in the Pacific had to make do with less. They would need skill, courage, and some luck to survive.

The first great defeat came in the Philippines. General Douglas MacArthur commanded the American and Filipino troops there. In March 1942, he was ordered to leave for Australia, and he told the Filipinos, "I shall return." On the Bataan Peninsula, about 75,000 American and Filipino soldiers, sick and starving, surrendered in April. The Japanese forced them to march about sixty-five miles to a prison camp, in what is known as the Bataan Death Marchbuh-TAN deth marchThe forced march, in April 1942, of about 75,000 American and Filipino prisoners in the Philippines. Thousands died.. Thousands died from beatings, hunger, and disease, or were killed when they could not go on. Americans learned of it in 1944, and it hardened the nation's will to fight.

The war turned in June 1942 at the Battle of MidwayBAT-ul uv MID-wayThe June 1942 naval battle in which American carrier planes sank four Japanese carriers and turned the tide in the Pacific.. The Pacific Fleet was led by Admiral Chester NimitzCHES-ter NIM-itsThe admiral who commanded the American Pacific Fleet., a Texan with a calm temper. The main weapon in that fleet was the aircraft carrierAIR-kraft KAIR-ee-erA warship with a long flat deck from which airplanes take off and land., a ship with a long flat deck from which planes can take off and land. Pearl Harbor had destroyed American battleships but had missed the carriers, and now they were the heart of the American fleet. Nimitz had another advantage. A team of navy codebreakers in Hawaii had broken part of the Japanese naval code. They learned that Japan planned an attack on a target called "AF." To prove that AF was Midway, a small island in the middle of the Pacific, they had the island send out a false message that its water supply was low. Soon after, the Japanese reported that "AF" was short of water.

Nimitz sent his three carriers to wait. On the morning of June 4, the Japanese fleet, led by Admiral Yamamoto's four large carriers, began to attack Midway. The American planes found the Japanese carriers at a moment when their decks were crowded with planes being refueled and rearmed. Within minutes, American dive bombers destroyed three of the four. The fourth carrier was sunk later that day. The Americans lost one carrier. Japan lost four, along with many of its most skilled pilots, and could not replace them. Midway was a victory of intelligence, of courage, and of luck. A small delay one way or the other might have changed it all. Historians who like to study contingency often start here.

After Midway, the Americans went on the offense. In August 1942, Marines landed on Guadalcanalgwah-dul-kuh-NALThe island in the Solomons where American Marines landed in August 1942 and won in February 1943, Japan's first big defeat on land., an island in the Solomon chain where the Japanese were building an airfield. For six months, soldiers, sailors, and airmen fought on land, at sea, and in the air. The fighting was brutal, and the jungle added disease and misery. The Americans held the airfield and won in February 1943. It was Japan's first big defeat on land.

From there, the Americans developed a plan known as island hoppingEYE-lund HOP-ingThe American strategy of taking some Pacific islands and skipping others, which left Japanese troops on the skipped islands cut off.. There were too many Japanese-held islands to capture them all. Instead, the Americans seized some, built airfields on them, and skipped others, leaving the Japanese troops there cut off and useless. Meanwhile, American submarines sank much of Japan's merchant fleet, cutting off the oil and supplies that its war depended on. The factories at home, which you will read about next week, poured out ships, planes, and tanks. American forces moved west across the Pacific, one step at a time. In November 1943, at Tarawa, more than a thousand Marines died in a few days taking a tiny island. The lessons from that battle helped the Navy and Marines plan better.

Many Americans served in this war who did not usually appear in the story. Navajo men served in the Marine Corps as code talkers. Using their own language as the base of a code that the Japanese could not break, they sent important messages quickly and accurately. Florida also played its part. The Naval Air Station at Pensacola trained thousands of the pilots who flew from the carriers, and bases across the state trained soldiers and sailors for both oceans.

By the summer of 1944, American forces were close enough to Japan to attack it directly. After they took the island of Saipan in June, giant B-29 bombers could fly from there to Tokyo. In October, MacArthur kept his promise. He waded ashore in the Philippines on the island of Leyte. A few days later, the largest naval battle in history, the Battle of Leyte GulfBAT-ul uv LAY-tee gulfThe October 1944 naval battle near the Philippines, the largest in history, which destroyed most of Japan's navy., destroyed most of what was left of Japan's navy. It also saw the first organized attacks by pilots who flew their planes into American ships on purpose.

The last stop before Japan itself was Iwo JimaEE-woh JEE-muhA small volcanic island, about 750 miles from Tokyo, that the Marines captured in February and March of 1945., a small volcanic island only about 750 miles from Tokyo. American leaders wanted its airfields so that fighter planes could protect the bombers and damaged planes could land safely. The Marines landed on February 19, 1945. The Japanese defenders had dug deep into the rock. They had built hundreds of hidden tunnels and bunkers, and they planned to make the Americans pay for every yard. They did. In five weeks of fighting, about 6,800 Americans died, and almost all of the roughly 21,000 Japanese defenders were killed. On the fifth day, Marines raised a flag on Mount Suribachi. A photographer, Joe Rosenthal, took a picture of a second, larger flag-raising that day. It became one of the most famous images in American history. Three of the six men in it were killed in the fighting that followed.

By the spring of 1945, the American position was strong, but the Japanese showed no sign of giving up. The Allies had demanded unconditional surrenderun-kun-DISH-un-ul suh-REN-derA surrender in which the losing side gives up completely, with no bargaining over terms., and Japan's leaders were not ready to accept it. The fighting was getting closer to the Japanese home islands, and with each step, the fighting grew more deadly. Next week, you will first see the home front that made this fighting possible. Then you will see how the war ended, and why the leaders of the United States chose the path that they did.
