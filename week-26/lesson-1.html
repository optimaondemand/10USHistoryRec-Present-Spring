📖 TODAY'S LESSON

After Pearl Harbor, Americans wanted to strike back at Japan. But Roosevelt and Winston ChurchillWIN-stun CHUR-chilThe prime minister of Britain from 1940, who led his country through most of the war. met in Washington in December 1941 and made a hard decision. Germany was the stronger enemy. If it conquered the Soviet Union and Britain, it would control all of Europe and its industry. The two leaders agreed to defeat Germany first, while holding Japan back in the Pacific. This plan is known as Europe FirstYUR-up furstThe Allied plan to defeat Germany before Japan.. It was unpopular with many Americans, but it shaped the whole war. They also agreed to accept nothing less than unconditional surrenderun-kun-DISH-un-ul suh-REN-derA surrender in which the losing side gives up completely, with no bargaining over terms., which means that the enemy must give up completely, with no bargaining over terms. The two nations were now fighting together, along with the Soviet Union and others, as the Allied PowersAL-yd POW-urzThe nations that fought the Axis in World War II, led by Britain, the Soviet Union, and the United States.. Their enemies were the Axis PowersAK-sis POW-urzGermany, Italy, and Japan, the nations that fought the Allies in World War II..

The first American battles against Germany took place in North Africa. In November 1942, American and British troops landed in Morocco and Algeria. In their first big fight, at the Kasserine Pass in Tunisia in February 1943, American soldiers were badly beaten by experienced German troops. They learned fast. By May 1943, the Axis forces in Africa had surrendered. In July, the Allies invaded Sicily, and Italy soon changed sides, though German troops fought on in Italy for almost two more years.

The largest land battles, however, were on the Eastern Front. At Stalingrad, in the winter of 1942 to 1943, the Soviet army trapped and destroyed a German army. American help was important here. Under the Lend-Leaselend LEESA 1941 law that let the president lend or lease weapons and supplies to any country whose defense he thought vital to the United States. program, the United States sent the Soviet Union hundreds of thousands of trucks, as well as food and fuel. Stalin kept asking for a second front in Western Europe. The Western Allies could not yet provide one, and the Soviets bore the weight of the war on land for most of three years.

In the meantime, Americans were turning out weapons by the thousands, and the air war against Germany began. American bombers flew in the daytime from bases in England, and British bombers flew at night. The air raids damaged German industry and killed many civilians. They also cost the Allies terrible losses, since bomber crews suffered some of the highest death rates of the war.

The invasion of France came on June 6, 1944, known as D-DayDEE-dayJune 6, 1944, the day the Allies landed on the beaches of Normandy, France.. General Dwight D. Eisenhowerdwyt dee EYE-zun-how-erThe American general who commanded the Allied armies in Europe and later became president., the Supreme Allied Commander, led the operation. The night before, he wrote a short note taking responsibility in case it failed. More than 150,000 troops crossed the English Channel in the largest invasion from the sea in history. They landed on five beaches in Normandy. At a beach codenamed Omaha, the American troops met fierce fire from cliffs above and suffered about two thousand casualties on the first day. Yet by nightfall, the Allies had a hold on the coast. Many things could have gone wrong. Bad weather had forced a one-day delay, and Eisenhower had to decide in a short break in the clouds. Had he waited longer, the next good weather might have been weeks away.

Paris was freed in August. By September, the Allies were at Germany's border. Then, in December 1944, the Germans launched a surprise attack through the forests of the Ardennes, creating a bulge in the Allied line. The Battle of the BulgeBAT-ul uv thuh BULJThe last great German attack in the West, begun in December 1944. It failed by late January 1945. was the largest battle the United States Army ever fought. American soldiers held the town of Bastogne, and by late January the German attack had failed. In the spring, the Allies crossed the Rhine. Roosevelt died on April 12, 1945, and the vice president took the oath of office. On April 30, Hitler killed himself in Berlin. Germany surrendered on May 8, 1945.

Now we turn to the worst crime of the war. Beginning in 1933, the Nazi government persecuted the Jews of Germany. After the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, it began a plan to murder all the Jews of Europe. In January 1942, officials met at a villa near Berlin and discussed how to carry out the plan. The result is called the HolocaustHOL-uh-kawstThe murder of about six million Jews, and millions of other people, by the Nazi government and its helpers.. German forces and their helpers shot hundreds of thousands of Jews in towns and ravines in the East. Millions more were sent by train to killing centers, such as Auschwitz, Treblinka, and Sobibor, where most were killed at once in gas chambers. Others died of hunger, disease, and forced labor in concentration campkon-sun-TRAY-shun kampA prison where people are held without trial, often in terrible conditions. The Nazis used such camps to imprison and kill their victims. prisons, which were set up to hold people without trial. About six million Jews were killed. The Nazis also murdered millions of other people, including Roma, Poles, Soviet prisoners, and disabled people.

What did Americans know? Reports began to arrive in 1942. In August of that year, a representative of the World Jewish Congress in Switzerland sent a cable saying he had heard of a German plan to kill all of the Jews of Europe. Officials at the State Department doubted the report and at first held back the message. By the end of 1942, the news had been confirmed, and in December, the Allied governments, including the United States, publicly condemned the killing. In 1943, American and British officials met in Bermuda to discuss refugees, and little came of it. Immigration laws were strict, and those laws and the State Department's rules kept the number of refugees low. In early 1944, a report from officials in the Treasury Department accused the State Department of failing to act. Soon after, Roosevelt created the War Refugee Boardwor REF-yoo-jee bordA United States agency, created in January 1944, to help rescue people in danger from Nazi persecution., which helped to save tens of thousands of lives by working with neutral countries and by supporting rescue efforts, including the work of the Swedish diplomat Raoul Wallenberg. Historians still argue about how much more might have been done, and how soon. That question is the subject of this week's discussion.

The American army also met the horror directly. In April 1945, its soldiers reached camps such as Buchenwald and Dachau. Eisenhower visited one camp and ordered that the local German townspeople be brought through it. He asked photographers and reporters to record what he saw, so that no one could say later that it had not happened. Many of the men who liberated the camps never forgot what they had found.

The defeat of Germany showed the republic at its best and at its limits. It showed the power of free workers and free industry to make the weapons of victory. It showed the courage of ordinary soldiers. The Holocaust showed what happens when a modern state has no limits on its power and no respect for human dignity. It was, in the end, the strongest possible argument against the totalitarian idea.

The war was far from over, however. In the Pacific, Americans were fighting a different enemy, across thousands of miles of ocean.
