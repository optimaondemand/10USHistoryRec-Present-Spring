📖 TODAY'S LESSON

After Pearl Harbor, Americans wanted to strike back at Japan. But Roosevelt and Winston ChurchillWIN-stun CHUR-chilThe prime minister of Britain from 1940, who led his country through most of the war. met in Washington in December 1941 and made a hard decision. Churchill stayed at the White House for most of three weeks, and on Christmas Eve the two men stood together at the lighting of the national Christmas tree. Germany was the stronger enemy. If it conquered the Soviet Union and Britain, it would control all of Europe and its industry. The two leaders agreed to defeat Germany first, while holding Japan back in the Pacific. This plan is known as Europe FirstYUR-up furstThe Allied plan to defeat Germany before Japan.. It was unpopular with many Americans, but it shaped the whole war. At a later meeting in Casablanca, in January 1943, they announced that they would accept nothing less than unconditional surrenderun-kun-DISH-un-ul suh-REN-derA surrender in which the losing side gives up completely, with no bargaining over terms., which means that the enemy must give up completely, with no bargaining over terms. The two nations were now fighting together, along with the Soviet Union and others, as the Allied PowersAL-yd POW-urzThe nations that fought the Axis in World War II, led by Britain, the Soviet Union, and the United States.. Their enemies were the Axis PowersAK-sis POW-urzGermany, Italy, and Japan, the nations that fought the Allies in World War II..

The first American battles against Germany took place in North Africa. In November 1942, American and British troops landed in Morocco and Algeria, the first time the two armies fought side by side in the West. In their first big fight, at the Kasserine Pass in Tunisia in February 1943, American soldiers were badly beaten by experienced German troops, led in the field by Erwin Rommel, the famous Desert Fox. A tougher American general, George Patton, took over the troops after that defeat. They learned fast. By May 1943, the Axis forces in Africa, about a quarter of a million men, had surrendered. In July, the Allies invaded Sicily. Mussolini was removed from power that month, and Italy soon changed sides, though German troops fought on in Italy for almost two more years. Rome fell to the Allies on June 4, 1944, two days before the invasion of France.

The largest land battles, however, were on the Eastern Front. At Stalingrad, in the winter of 1942 to 1943, the Soviet army trapped and destroyed a German army. The fighting went on house by house in a ruined city. Of a force that had once numbered some three hundred thousand, about ninety thousand survivors surrendered in February. In July 1943, near the city of Kursk, the two armies fought what is often called the largest tank battle in history, and the Germans never again attacked in strength in the East. American help was important here. Under the Lend-Leaselend LEESA 1941 law that let the president lend or lease weapons and supplies to any country whose defense he thought vital to the United States. program, the United States sent the Soviet Union hundreds of thousands of trucks, many of them built by Studebaker, as well as food and fuel. The siege of Leningrad lasted nearly nine hundred days, and hundreds of thousands of its people died of hunger. Stalin kept asking for a second front in Western Europe. The Western Allies could not yet provide one, and the Soviets bore the weight of the war on land for most of three years.

In the meantime, Americans were turning out weapons by the thousands, and the air war against Germany began. American bombers flew in the daytime from bases in England, and British bombers flew at night. The Americans aimed at particular factories, but clouds, smoke, and enemy fire meant that many bombs fell wide. The air raids damaged German industry and killed many civilians. They also cost the Allies terrible losses, since bomber crews suffered some of the highest death rates of the war.

A bomber carried a crew of ten, and the rule at first was that a crew was finished after twenty-five missions. Later the number rose to thirty-five. In 1943, few crews lived to finish their tours. On one day in October, sixty bombers failed to return from a raid on the factories at Schweinfurt, out of about three hundred sent. The bombers had no fighters able to fly with them all the way to Germany and back, until the long-range P-51 Mustang arrived in the winter of 1943 and 1944. In February 1945, British and American bombers burned much of the city of Dresden, and historians estimate that about twenty-five thousand people died. Whether that raid was necessary is still argued.

The invasion of France came on June 6, 1944, known as D-DayDEE-dayJune 6, 1944, the day the Allies landed on the beaches of Normandy, France.. General Dwight D. Eisenhowerdwyt dee EYE-zun-how-erThe American general who commanded the Allied armies in Europe and later became president., the Supreme Allied Commander, led the operation. A staff officer who had never led troops in battle before the landings in North Africa, he had earned the job by keeping a coalition of proud generals working together. The night before, he wrote a short note taking responsibility in case it failed. More than 150,000 troops crossed the English Channel in the largest invasion from the sea in history. The Allies had worked hard to fool the Germans. They built a make-believe army of rubber tanks and false radio messages in southeast England, with Patton as its supposed commander, so that the Germans would expect the blow farther north at Calais. On the evening before, Eisenhower walked among paratroopers who would drop behind the beaches that night. The troops landed on five beaches in Normandy. At a beach codenamed Omaha, heavy seas swamped many of the landing craft and tanks, and bombs meant to clear the beach had fallen too far inland. The American troops met fierce fire from cliffs above and suffered about two thousand casualties on the first day. Yet by nightfall, the Allies had a hold on the coast. Many things could have gone wrong. Bad weather had forced a one-day delay, and Eisenhower had to decide in a short break in the clouds. His weather officer, a Scottish officer named James Stagg, had told him that the storm would ease for a short time on June 6. Had he waited longer, the next good weather might have been weeks away.

Paris was freed in August, and French troops led the way into the city on August 25. By September, the Allies were at Germany's border. Then, in December 1944, the Germans launched a surprise attack through the forests of the Ardennes, creating a bulge in the Allied line. Hitler's aim was to drive to the port of Antwerp and split the British from the Americans. The Battle of the BulgeBAT-ul uv thuh BULJThe last great German attack in the West, begun in December 1944. It failed by late January 1945. was the largest battle the United States Army ever fought. The Germans struck in fog and snow that kept Allied planes on the ground. American soldiers held the town of Bastogne. When German officers demanded that the town surrender, the American general, Anthony McAuliffe, sent back a one-word reply, "Nuts." Patton turned his army north and broke through to the town in about a week. By late January the German attack had failed, and about nineteen thousand Americans had died. In the spring, the Allies crossed the Rhine. On March 7, American troops captured a bridge over the river at Remagen before the Germans could blow it up. Roosevelt died on April 12, 1945, of a stroke, at Warm Springs, Georgia, the resort he had made a home for polio patients. The vice president, Harry Truman, took the oath of office. He had held the job for only eighty-two days. On April 30, as Soviet troops fought their way into Berlin, Hitler killed himself. Mussolini had been killed by Italian partisans two days earlier. Germany signed its surrender on May 7, and it took effect on May 8, 1945.

Now we turn to the worst crime of the war. Beginning in 1933, the Nazi government persecuted the Jews of Germany. After the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, it began a plan to murder all the Jews of Europe. You read last week about Kristallnacht. On January 20, 1942, officials met at a lakeside villa near Berlin and discussed how to carry out the plan. The result is called the HolocaustHOL-uh-kawstThe murder of about six million Jews, and millions of other people, by the Nazi government and its helpers.. German forces and their helpers shot hundreds of thousands of Jews in towns and ravines in the East. At a ravine called Babi Yar, near Kyiv, German units shot more than thirty thousand Jews in two days in September 1941. Millions more were sent by train to killing centers, such as Auschwitz, Treblinka, and Sobibor, where most were killed at once in gas chambers. More than a million people died at Auschwitz alone, most of them Jews. Others died of hunger, disease, and forced labor in concentration campkon-sun-TRAY-shun kampA prison where people are held without trial, often in terrible conditions. The Nazis used such camps to imprison and kill their victims. prisons, which were set up to hold people without trial. About six million Jews were killed. The Nazis also murdered millions of other people, including Roma, Poles, Soviet prisoners, and disabled people. Some Jews fought back. In April 1943, a few hundred young fighters in the Warsaw ghetto, with a handful of pistols and homemade bombs, rose against the German troops sent to empty it, and they held out for about a month. And some people risked their lives to hide their Jewish neighbors. In October 1943, Danes carried about seven thousand Danish Jews in fishing boats across the water to neutral Sweden in a few weeks.

What did Americans know? Reports began to arrive in 1942. In August of that year, a representative of the World Jewish Congress in Switzerland sent a cable saying he had heard of a German plan to kill all of the Jews of Europe. Officials at the State Department doubted the report and at first held back the message. By the end of 1942, the news had been confirmed. In November, Undersecretary of State Sumner Welles confirmed the reports to Rabbi Stephen Wise of the World Jewish Congress, and Wise told the press. In December, the Allied governments, including the United States, publicly condemned the killing. In 1943, American and British officials met in Bermuda to discuss refugees, and little came of it. That summer, a Polish courier named Jan Karski, who had seen the Warsaw ghetto with his own eyes, told Roosevelt in person what was happening. Immigration laws were strict, and those laws and the State Department's rules kept the number of refugees low. In early 1944, a report from officials in the Treasury Department accused the State Department of failing to act. Soon after, Roosevelt created the War Refugee Boardwor REF-yoo-jee bordA United States agency, created in January 1944, to help rescue people in danger from Nazi persecution., which helped to save tens of thousands of lives by working with neutral countries and by supporting rescue efforts, including the work of the Swedish diplomat Raoul Wallenberg. In Budapest in 1944, Wallenberg handed out protective papers that saved thousands of Jews from deportation.

One argument concerns bombing. In 1944, Jewish leaders asked the Allies to bomb the railroad lines leading to Auschwitz, or the camp itself. The War Department replied that its planes were needed for the military effort and that the quickest way to help was to win the war. Allied planes had already photographed the area during raids on nearby factories. Whether bombing would have saved lives is disputed. Historians still argue about how much more might have been done, and how soon. That question is the subject of this week's discussion.

The American army also met the horror directly. In April 1945, its soldiers reached camps such as Buchenwald and Dachau. Eisenhower visited one camp, Ohrdruf, and ordered that the local German townspeople be brought through it. He asked photographers and reporters to record what he saw, so that no one could say later that it had not happened. He urged Washington to send members of Congress and newspaper editors to see the camps for themselves. Many of the men who liberated the camps never forgot what they had found.

The defeat of Germany showed the republic at its best and at its limits. It showed the power of free workers and free industry to make the weapons of victory. It showed the courage of ordinary soldiers. The Holocaust showed what happens when a modern state has no limits on its power and no respect for human dignity. It was, in the end, the strongest possible argument against the totalitarian idea.

The war was far from over, however. In the Pacific, Americans were fighting a different enemy, across thousands of miles of ocean.
