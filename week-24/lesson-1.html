📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By the start of 1935, the New Deal had helped the country, but the Depression was far from over. About one worker in five still had no job. Roosevelt also faced pressure from two directions. Critics such as Huey LongHYOO-ee lawngA powerful Louisiana governor and senator who proposed the Share Our Wealth plan. He was shot and killed in 1935. and the supporters of the Townsend PlanTOWN-zend planDr. Francis Townsend's proposal to pay every American over sixty two hundred dollars a month, on the condition that they spend it within the month. said he had not gone far enough, and millions of voters listened to them. The elections of 1934 had given him larger Democratic majorities in Congress, and many of the new members wanted bolder action. At the same time, the Supreme Court was getting ready to strike down some of his laws. Roosevelt's answer, in the spring and summer of 1935, was a new burst of lawmaking. Historians call it the Second New DealSEK-und noo deelThe group of laws passed in 1935, including Social Security and the Wagner Act, that tried to make lasting changes rather than only meet the emergency.. The first New Deal had tried to rescue the country in an emergency. The second tried to build things that would last. That summer Congress also raised taxes on the largest incomes and inheritances, which critics called a "soak the rich" tax.

The first step was more relief. In April, Congress created the Works Progress Administrationwurks PRAH-gres ad-min-ih-STRAY-shunA New Deal agency, begun in 1935, that paid unemployed Americans to build roads, schools, and parks and to work as artists and writers., or WPA, and Roosevelt put his friend Harry Hopkins in charge. Roosevelt believed that handing out money to people who did nothing was bad for them and bad for the country. He wanted the government to pay people to work instead. Congress gave him nearly five billion dollars for the purpose, the largest sum it had ever approved for one program in peacetime. Over the next eight years, the WPA gave jobs to roughly eight million people. They built roads, bridges, schools, airports, and parks. Artists painted murals in post offices. Writers collected the stories of Americans who had lived through slavery. In Florida, the writer Zora Neale Hurston took a job with the writers' project and collected folk songs and stories. The pay was set above a relief check but below what private jobs paid, so that no one would prefer the program to a real job. Opponents sneered that the workers only leaned on their shovels, and the word boondoggle, meaning wasteful busywork, came into common use. Even so, the WPA never reached everyone who needed work.

The most lasting law of that summer was the Social Security ActSOH-shul sih-KYUR-ih-tee aktA 1935 law that created old-age insurance and unemployment insurance and gave aid to poor children and blind people., signed on August 14, 1935. Frances Perkins stood behind the president's chair as he signed it. In 1934, roughly half of the old people in the country lacked enough income to live on, and many ended their lives in county poorhouses. Perkins, the Secretary of Labor, led the team that designed the law. When Roosevelt had offered her the job in 1933, she had handed him a list of her goals, among them unemployment insurance, old-age pensions, shorter hours, and an end to child labor, and she asked whether he would back them. He said he would try, and over the next five years she held him to it. The law did several things. It set up unemployment insurance, run through the states. To get the states moving, the law taxed employers but let them subtract whatever they paid into a state fund, so every state soon set one up. Benefits were small and lasted only a limited time, and each state set its own amounts, so a laid-off worker in one state might receive far less than one in another. It gave money to the states to help poor children in homes without a father, and to help blind people. The aid for children grew out of mothers' pensions that some states already paid. Its largest part was old-age insurance. Workers and their employers would each pay a payroll taxPAY-rohl taksA tax taken out of a worker's paycheck, and matched by the employer, to pay for programs such as Social Security., a tax taken out of every paycheck. The rate started at one percent each, on the first three thousand dollars a worker earned. Workers who retired at sixty-five would then receive a monthly check for the rest of their lives. The House passed the bill by 372 votes to 33, and the Senate by 77 to 6.

Notice how the law was built. It was not a gift. It was insurance, paid for by the people who would use it. Insurance works by pooling risk. Few workers lose a job in any given year, so small payments from everyone can pay large benefits to the few who do. Roosevelt chose this plan on purpose. He knew that workers who had paid into the system would feel they had earned their benefits, and no future Congress would find it easy to take them away. The first monthly check went to a retired legal secretary from Vermont, Ida May Fuller, in January 1940. She had paid only about twenty-five dollars in taxes. She had retired in 1939 at the age of sixty-five, after only three years of paying in, and her first check was for $22.54. She lived to be one hundred and collected far more, more than twenty thousand dollars in all.

The law also had gaps. It left out farm workers and household servants, and in the 1930s that meant many Black Americans, especially in the South. Southern members of Congress had insisted on this, and Roosevelt needed their votes. The law also began collecting taxes in 1937 but did not pay monthly benefits until 1940. Critics noted that the tax pulled money out of the economy just when it was still weak. It was a trade. The government gained a steady source of money for the system, but at a cost.

The second great law of 1935 helped workers in a different way. Senator Robert Wagner of New York wrote a bill to protect the right of workers to form unions. Wagner had come to New York from Germany as a boy of eight, grown up poor in the tenements, and risen to the Senate. Roosevelt was cool to the idea until it was clear that it would pass. He signed the Wagner ActWAG-ner aktA 1935 law that protected the right of workers to form unions and bargain with their employers. It is also called the National Labor Relations Act. on July 5. The law said that workers could join a union of their choice. It required employers to take part in collective bargainingkoh-LEK-tiv BAR-gun-ingTalks between a group of workers, usually in a union, and their employer about pay, hours, and working conditions., which means negotiating as a group over pay and working conditions. It also banned certain unfair practices, such as firing a worker for joining a union. It also banned company unions, which were groups run by the employer that workers could not truly control. A new board would enforce the rules. Like the other laws of 1935, it left out farm workers and household servants. It could hold secret-ballot elections to find out whether the workers in a plant wanted a union. Lawyers for the wealthy business group you read about last time declared the law unconstitutional and urged employers to ignore it.

Many employers hated the law and refused to obey it, but it gave workers a strong new tool. Until then, most unions had been made up of skilled craftsmen, such as carpenters and machinists. John L. Lewisjon el LOO-issThe head of the United Mine Workers, who helped start the CIO and led the drive to organize workers in big industries., the head of the coal miners' union, believed that the time had come to organize everyone who worked in a big industry, from the skilled to the unskilled. Lewis was a burly man with thick eyebrows and a booming voice, the son of Welsh immigrants, who had worked in the mines himself. His new group became the Congress of Industrial OrganizationsKONG-gres uv in-DUS-tree-ul or-gun-ih-ZAY-shunzA group of unions, begun by John L. Lewis, that organized all the workers in a big industry, skilled or unskilled. It was also called the CIO., or CIO. It began in 1935 as a committee inside the older American Federation of Labor, whose leaders disagreed with it, and the two groups soon split. The CIO's founders argued that organizing by craft left autoworkers and steelworkers with no union at all. Lewis used money from the miners' union treasury to pay organizers. The Wagner Act gave it a chance to grow. Within a few years, union membership in the country had about tripled.

The great test came at General Motors. In December 1936, workers at a plant in Flint, Michigan, began a sit-down strikeSIT-down strikeA strike in which workers stay inside the workplace and refuse to work or to leave.. Instead of walking off the job, they sat down at their machines and refused to leave. Since the strikers were inside, the company could not bring in other workers to replace them. The strike began at the Fisher Body plants, which made car bodies, and the workers belonged to a new union, the United Auto Workers. It started when they heard that the company planned to move the huge dies that shaped car bodies to plants where it had no union. The strike lasted forty-four days in the winter cold. Their families brought food to the windows. Wives and daughters formed a Women's Emergency Brigade, wore red berets, and stood ready to march if the police came. The strikers organized themselves like a small town. Committees ran the kitchen and kept the plant clean, and the men were forbidden liquor, so that no one could call them rioters. On January 11, the company shut off the heat in one plant, and police tried to stop the food from passing through the windows. Outside, strikers and their families answered tear gas with bottles, car-door hinges, and water from fire hoses, and the police fell back. The strikers named it the Battle of the Running Bulls. Several strikers were shot, but none died. The governor of Michigan, Frank Murphy, refused to send troops to remove them by force. A judge ordered the strikers out, but it turned out that he owned a large block of General Motors stock, and the order lost its force. Murphy did send the National Guard, but only to keep the peace. In February 1937, General Motors agreed to bargain with the union. Lewis and the governor had both pushed the company to the table.

Other companies soon followed. Chrysler signed in April, after a sit-down strike of its own. In March, U.S. Steel, the nation's biggest steelmaker, signed an agreement with the CIO without a strike. But not every fight ended well for workers. On Memorial Day, 1937, police in Chicago fired on strikers marching toward a Republic Steel plant and killed ten. Not everyone cheered. Sit-down strikes meant taking over property that belonged to someone else, and many Americans, including some who liked unions, thought that was wrong. The Supreme Court would later rule that such strikes were illegal.

Here is what the lesson tells us. Murphy refused to use force, a judge's order lost its power, and in February 1937 General Motors agreed to bargain. Nobody can know what troops would have done. The Republic Steel march in Chicago, where police fired on strikers and killed ten, shows that a clash could end very differently. A careful historian says what was possible, not what was certain.

One more law finished the work. In 1938, Congress passed the Fair Labor Standards Actfair LAY-bur STAN-durdz aktA 1938 law that set the first national minimum wage, limited the workweek, and banned most child labor.. It set a minimum wageMIN-ih-mum wayjThe lowest hourly pay that an employer may legally offer a worker., the lowest hourly pay that an employer may legally offer, at twenty-five cents an hour, a rate that was to rise to forty cents over seven years. It limited the workweek and banned most work by children. The workweek limit started at forty-four hours and was to drop to forty within three years, and the law required extra pay, time and a half, for overtime. Again, important groups, including farm workers and household servants, were left out, and again the law passed only after a bargain with Southern Democrats.

What should we make of all this? In the classical republican tradition, a healthy republic depends on citizens who can support themselves and take part in public life. Supporters of these laws argued that no one can do that when the loss of a job or old age can bring ruin. They said security would help citizens be free. Critics worried about the opposite. They feared that a people who counted on the national government might lose the habits of self-reliance and local cooperation, which Tocqueville had thought were the strength of American life. Before 1935, many workers had relied on churches, mutual aid societies, and lodges, which paid for sickness and burial. Supporters said that these groups could not carry a whole country through a depression, and critics feared that a national program would crowd them out. Roosevelt himself shared some of that worry, which is why he preferred insurance and work to plain relief.

The debate has never ended. By 1940, though, one thing had clearly changed. Americans now expected Washington to help protect their families against old age, unemployment, and hard bargains. Whether the Constitution allowed all of this was still an open question.
