📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By the start of 1935, the New Deal had helped the country, but the Depression was far from over. About one worker in five still had no job. Roosevelt also faced pressure from two directions. Critics such as Huey LongHYOO-ee lawngA powerful Louisiana governor and senator who proposed the Share Our Wealth plan. He was shot and killed in 1935. and the supporters of the Townsend PlanTOWN-zend planDr. Francis Townsend's proposal to pay every American over sixty two hundred dollars a month, on the condition that they spend it within the month. said he had not gone far enough, and millions of voters listened to them. At the same time, the Supreme Court was getting ready to strike down some of his laws. Roosevelt's answer, in the spring and summer of 1935, was a new burst of lawmaking. Historians call it the Second New DealSEK-und noo deelThe group of laws passed in 1935, including Social Security and the Wagner Act, that tried to make lasting changes rather than only meet the emergency.. The first New Deal had tried to rescue the country in an emergency. The second tried to build things that would last.

The first step was more relief. In April, Congress created the Works Progress Administrationwurks PRAH-gres ad-min-ih-STRAY-shunA New Deal agency, begun in 1935, that paid unemployed Americans to build roads, schools, and parks and to work as artists and writers., or WPA, and Roosevelt put his friend Harry Hopkins in charge. Roosevelt believed that handing out money to people who did nothing was bad for them and bad for the country. He wanted the government to pay people to work instead. Over the next eight years, the WPA gave jobs to roughly eight million people. They built roads, bridges, schools, airports, and parks. Artists painted murals in post offices. Writers collected the stories of Americans who had lived through slavery. Even so, the WPA never reached everyone who needed work.

The most lasting law of that summer was the Social Security ActSOH-shul sih-KYUR-ih-tee aktA 1935 law that created old-age insurance and unemployment insurance and gave aid to poor children and blind people., signed on August 14, 1935. Frances Perkins, the Secretary of Labor, led the team that designed it. The law did several things. It set up unemployment insurance, run through the states. It gave money to the states to help poor children in homes without a father, and to help blind people. Its largest part was old-age insurance. Workers and their employers would each pay a payroll taxPAY-rohl taksA tax taken out of a worker's paycheck, and matched by the employer, to pay for programs such as Social Security., a tax taken out of every paycheck. Workers who retired at sixty-five would then receive a monthly check for the rest of their lives.

Notice how the law was built. It was not a gift. It was insurance, paid for by the people who would use it. Roosevelt chose this plan on purpose. He knew that workers who had paid into the system would feel they had earned their benefits, and no future Congress would find it easy to take them away. The first monthly check went to a retired legal secretary from Vermont, Ida May Fuller, in January 1940. She had paid only about twenty-five dollars in taxes. She lived to be one hundred and collected far more.

The law also had gaps. It left out farm workers and household servants, and in the 1930s that meant many Black Americans, especially in the South. Southern members of Congress had insisted on this, and Roosevelt needed their votes. The law also began collecting taxes in 1937 but did not pay monthly benefits until 1940. Critics noted that the tax pulled money out of the economy just when it was still weak. It was a trade. The government gained a steady source of money for the system, but at a cost.

The second great law of 1935 helped workers in a different way. Senator Robert Wagner of New York wrote a bill to protect the right of workers to form unions. Roosevelt was cool to the idea until it was clear that it would pass. He signed the Wagner ActWAG-ner aktA 1935 law that protected the right of workers to form unions and bargain with their employers. It is also called the National Labor Relations Act. on July 5. The law said that workers could join a union of their choice. It required employers to take part in collective bargainingkoh-LEK-tiv BAR-gun-ingTalks between a group of workers, usually in a union, and their employer about pay, hours, and working conditions., which means negotiating as a group over pay and working conditions. It also banned certain unfair practices, such as firing a worker for joining a union. A new board would enforce the rules.

Many employers hated the law and refused to obey it, but it gave workers a strong new tool. Until then, most unions had been made up of skilled craftsmen, such as carpenters and machinists. John L. Lewisjon el LOO-issThe head of the United Mine Workers, who helped start the CIO and led the drive to organize workers in big industries., the head of the coal miners' union, believed that the time had come to organize everyone who worked in a big industry, from the skilled to the unskilled. His new group became the Congress of Industrial OrganizationsKONG-gres uv in-DUS-tree-ul or-gun-ih-ZAY-shunzA group of unions, begun by John L. Lewis, that organized all the workers in a big industry, skilled or unskilled. It was also called the CIO., or CIO. The Wagner Act gave it a chance to grow. Within a few years, union membership in the country had about tripled.

The great test came at General Motors. In December 1936, workers at a plant in Flint, Michigan, began a sit-down strikeSIT-down strikeA strike in which workers stay inside the workplace and refuse to work or to leave.. Instead of walking off the job, they sat down at their machines and refused to leave. Since the strikers were inside, the company could not bring in other workers to replace them. The strike lasted forty-four days in the winter cold. Their families brought food to the windows. The governor of Michigan, Frank Murphy, refused to send troops to remove them by force. In February 1937, General Motors agreed to bargain with the union. Other companies soon followed. Not everyone cheered. Sit-down strikes meant taking over property that belonged to someone else, and many Americans, including some who liked unions, thought that was wrong. The Supreme Court would later rule that such strikes were illegal.

One more law finished the work. In 1938, Congress passed the Fair Labor Standards Actfair LAY-bur STAN-durdz aktA 1938 law that set the first national minimum wage, limited the workweek, and banned most child labor.. It set a minimum wageMIN-ih-mum wayjThe lowest hourly pay that an employer may legally offer a worker., the lowest hourly pay that an employer may legally offer, at twenty-five cents an hour. It limited the workweek and banned most work by children. Again, important groups were left out, and again the law passed only after a bargain with Southern Democrats.

What should we make of all this? In the classical republican tradition, a healthy republic depends on citizens who can support themselves and take part in public life. Supporters of these laws argued that no one can do that when the loss of a job or old age can bring ruin. They said security would help citizens be free. Critics worried about the opposite. They feared that a people who counted on the national government might lose the habits of self-reliance and local cooperation, which Tocqueville had thought were the strength of American life. Roosevelt himself shared some of that worry, which is why he preferred insurance and work to plain relief.

The debate has never ended. By 1940, though, one thing had clearly changed. Americans now expected Washington to help protect their families against old age, unemployment, and hard bargains. Whether the Constitution allowed all of this was still an open question. In the next lesson, you will see how the Supreme Court answered, and why that fight almost broke the balance of power in Washington.
