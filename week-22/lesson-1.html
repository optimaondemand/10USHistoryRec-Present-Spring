📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In November 1928, Americans elected Herbert HooverHER-bert HOO-verThe mining engineer and relief organizer who was elected president in 1928. He led the nation as the Great Depression began. president in a landslide. Hoover was an orphan from Iowa who had become a rich mining engineer. He lost both parents before he was ten and was raised by relatives, mostly an uncle in Oregon. He was part of the first class to attend Stanford University, where he studied geology, and then he worked in mines in Australia and China. During the Great War, he organized food relief for starving Belgium and then ran the nation's wartime food program. The Belgian relief effort, paid for by donations and run largely by volunteers, fed millions of people. As Secretary of Commerce in the 1920s, he was known as the great manager of American prosperity. In 1927, when the Mississippi River flooded and left hundreds of thousands of people homeless, he was sent to lead the rescue, and his name as the man who could fix anything grew. In his speech accepting the nomination, he said, "We in America today are nearer to the final triumph over poverty than ever before in the history of any land." Few Americans doubted him.

The stock market was one reason. The stock marketstok MAR-kitThe place where people buy and sell shares, which are small pieces of ownership in companies. is where people buy and sell shares, or small pieces of ownership, in companies. Through the 1920s, stock prices climbed. Between 1925 and September 1929, the average price of leading stocks more than tripled. For the first time, many middle-class families bought stocks, not just the rich. Stories of easy fortunes brought in even more buyers. Many of them practiced buying on marginBY-ing on MAR-jinBuying stocks by paying only a small part of the price and borrowing the rest from a broker. It brings big profits if prices rise and big losses if they fall.. That means paying only a small part of a stock's price, often ten to twenty percent, and borrowing the rest from a broker. If the stock's price rose, the buyer made a large profit on a small amount of money. If it fell, the broker demanded repayment right away. Suppose a buyer put down $1,000 and borrowed $9,000 to buy $10,000 worth of stock. A rise of ten percent doubled his money. A fall of ten percent wiped out his $1,000, and the broker, who had lent against the stock, wanted more cash at once. By 1929, brokers had lent about eight billion dollars to people buying stocks, more than all the paper money then in circulation in the country. This kind of speculationspek-yuh-LAY-shunBetting that prices will rise in order to make a quick profit, instead of investing for the long term., betting on prices to rise instead of investing for the long term, can turn a boom into a bubble. Warnings came, but so did reassurance. In September 1929, a business forecaster named Roger Babson told a luncheon that a crash was coming, and stocks dropped sharply that afternoon, a dip people called the Babson Break. A few weeks later, Irving Fisher, a Yale economist whom many trusted, said that stocks had reached what looked like a permanently high plateau.

The bubble burst in October 1929. On Thursday, October 24, prices plunged. Nearly thirteen million shares were traded that day, a record at the time, and crowds gathered in the street outside the Stock Exchange on Wall Street to watch. Leading bankers pooled money to buy shares and calm the panic, and prices steadied for a few days. They met at the office of J. P. Morgan and Company, and an officer of the Exchange then walked onto the floor and bid for shares of U.S. Steel at a price well above the market. The bid worked for a moment, and the selling stopped. The next day, Hoover said that the country's business was sound, and many people, including many experts, hoped that was true.

Then the selling began again. On Monday, October 28, the market fell almost 13 percent in a single day. On Tuesday, October 29, a day known as Black Tuesdayblak TOOZ-dayOctober 29, 1929, the day of the worst stock market crash, when more than sixteen million shares were traded., more than sixteen million shares changed hands, a record, and prices fell almost 12 percent more. The next day, the show-business paper Variety summed it up in a headline: "Wall St. Lays an Egg." The ticker, which printed prices on a paper tape, ran hours behind, so people in brokers' offices across the country did not learn what their shares were worth until long after the prices had dropped further. Buyers on margin were wiped out, because their brokers demanded money they did not have. Fortunes disappeared in days. Prices kept sliding for almost three years. By the summer of 1932, stocks had lost close to ninety percent of their value.

A stock crash by itself does not explain a decade of misery. Most Americans did not own stocks. The crash was the match, but the fuel had been piling up. Many banks had made poor loans. In those days, there was no government insurance on deposits. A bank keeps only a small part of its deposits in cash. The rest is out on loans to farmers, factories, and homebuyers, and a bank cannot call those loans back in a day. When people heard rumors that a bank was in trouble, they rushed to take out their money. Frightened families also began hoarding cash, keeping their bills at home in mattresses and jars, which pulled still more money out of the banks. Such a bank runbangk runA sudden rush by many depositors to withdraw their money because they fear their bank will fail. Banks lend out most of their deposits, so a run can ruin even a healthy bank. could ruin even a healthy bank, because banks lend out most of what they take in. Between 1930 and 1933, roughly nine thousand banks failed, and their customers lost their savings. In December 1930, the Bank of United States in New York, with some four hundred thousand depositors, closed its doors, the biggest bank failure the country had ever seen. The Federal ReserveFED-er-ul rih-ZURVThe nation's central bank, created in 1913. It controls the supply of money and credit., the nation's central bank, could have flooded the system with money to calm the panic. Instead, many economists now believe, it let the supply of money shrink, which made lending dry up and prices fall. Between 1929 and 1933, the nation's money supply shrank by about a third.

Falling prices made matters worse. When money is scarce, prices drop, and a drop in prices hurts anyone in debt. A farmer who borrowed in 1925 still owed the same number of dollars, but each bushel of wheat now brought far fewer of them. A factory with a loan had to repay it out of sales that were shrinking. Businesses cut wages and let workers go to survive, and workers with less pay bought less, which hurt businesses again. Round and round it went. Factories felt it first. Car makers had built more than five million cars in 1929, and by 1932 their output had fallen by about three fourths. Each idle car plant also idled the steel mills, glassmakers, and tire makers that had sold to it.

Then came a mistake in trade policy. A tariffTAIR-ifA tax on imported goods. is a tax on imported goods. Farmers and factory owners had long asked for high tariffs to protect them from foreign competition. Hoover had promised farmers help in the campaign, and in 1929 he called Congress into special session to raise tariffs on farm goods. Once the bill was open, industry after industry asked for protection too, and it grew to cover about twenty thousand products. In June 1930, Congress passed the Smoot-Hawley TariffSMOOT-HAW-lee TAIR-ifA 1930 law that raised tariffs on thousands of imported goods. Other countries struck back and world trade collapsed., which raised rates on thousands of goods. More than a thousand economists signed a petition asking Hoover to veto it. He signed it anyway. Other countries, Canada among them, struck back with tariffs of their own, and world trade collapsed. Between 1929 and 1933, it fell by more than half. American farmers and factories lost foreign customers they could not afford to lose.

The crisis was also worldwide. European nations owed large war debts to the United States, and many had borrowed American money to rebuild. When American lending dried up, the shock spread across the ocean. In May 1931, a major Austrian bank failed, and panic ran through the banks of Central Europe. Hoover proposed a one-year pause in war debt payments, but it was too little to stop the fall. The trouble also fed politics. In Germany, a small extremist party, the Nazis, went from 12 seats in the national legislature in 1928 to 107 in 1930, as jobless voters looked for anyone who promised change. Most nations were also tied to the gold standardgohld STAN-derdA system in which a nation's money is worth a fixed amount of gold. It made it hard for governments to add money to the economy in a crisis., a system that fixed the value of money to a set amount of gold. It made it hard for governments to put more money into their economies when trouble came. Britain left the gold standard in 1931, and the United States left it in 1933. After Britain's departure, foreign holders of dollars began trading them in for American gold. In October 1931, the Federal Reserve raised its lending rate sharply to stop the flow, and the higher rate made loans harder to get just when the country needed them. Countries that left it sooner generally began to recover sooner.

By 1933, about one worker in four had no job, and the nation's output had fallen by roughly a third. Americans began calling the disaster the Great Depressiongrayt dih-PRESH-unThe worst economic crisis in American history. It began with the 1929 crash and lasted through the 1930s..

How did Hoover respond? He is often pictured as a man who did nothing, and that picture is unfair. Hoover believed in action, but of a certain kind. He thought the government should encourage cooperation but not take over. In his view, trade groups, business owners, and bankers, left to work together, could steady the economy without orders from Washington. He believed that relief for the poor should come from families, churches, local charities, and state governments. A direct federal handout, he feared, would weaken the independence and character of citizens. So he urged business leaders to keep wages up and avoid layoffs. In October 1930, he named a committee to help local groups find work for the jobless, but it had no money of its own to spend. He created a Federal Farm Board, which tried to buy up surplus crops to hold up prices, and he expanded public works, including a huge dam on the Colorado River, now called Hoover Dam. In January 1932, he asked Congress to create the Reconstruction Finance Corporationrek-un-STRUK-shun fih-NANS kor-puh-RAY-shunA government agency created in 1932 to lend money to banks, railroads, and other large businesses so they would not collapse., which lent money to banks, railroads, and insurance companies so they would not collapse. In July 1932, he signed a law that let the Corporation lend money to the states for relief of the poor. It was a small step toward federal help, and it came late. In 1932, he also signed a large tax increase to balance the budget. It raised the top rate on the highest incomes from 25 percent to 63 percent. Many economists now think that move deepened the slump.

Evaluate Hoover as you would any leader, by his habits of character and their results. His virtues were real. He was honest, hardworking, and deeply generous in his own way. He gave away his presidential salary. His rescue of Belgian civilians had saved millions. His trust in voluntary effort had a source. In Belgium, donations and volunteers had saved millions of lives, and he had seen it work. But the United States, with more than a hundred million people and a quarter of its workers jobless, was a far larger task than a small country with a single need. He held to his principles even as the evidence mounted against them. He found it hard to speak warmly to a frightened public, and many Americans came to feel that he did not understand their fear. Meanwhile, the voluntary system he trusted was overwhelmed. Private charities and local governments ran out of money well before the need ran out.

It is tempting to say the Depression was bound to happen, or that Hoover alone caused it. Neither claim is sound. The crash was shaped by decisions, and different decisions were possible. If the Federal Reserve had acted differently, the bank failures might have been smaller. If Hoover had vetoed the tariff, world trade might have fallen less. If the banking crisis had been faced early and boldly, the panic might have been contained. No one can say for sure what would have happened. What we know is that a collection of human choices, made in a hurry and under pressure, helped turn a financial shock into the worst economic crisis in American history.
