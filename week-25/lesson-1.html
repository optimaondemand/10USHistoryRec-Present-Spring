Isolation and the Road to War

The Dictators and an Isolated Republic

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In 1919, Americans had hoped that the Great War would be the war to end all wars. By the middle of the 1930s, many of them felt fooled. The Depression made things worse. When so many people were out of work at home, a quarrel across the ocean seemed like someone else's trouble. While Americans looked inward, a new kind of government was spreading in Europe and Asia. It would test whether a free republic could see danger coming from far away.

The new governments had a name, totalitarianismtoh-tal-ih-TAIR-ee-un-iz-umA system in which one party and one leader control every part of life, using propaganda and secret police.. In a totalitarian state, one party and one leader try to control not just the government but every part of life, including the schools, the newspapers, the churches, and even the family. A secret police enforces obedience. Propaganda, which is one-sided messages meant to shape what people believe, fills the air. Two kinds of totalitarian state grew up between the wars. In the Soviet Union, the Communist Party under Joseph Stalin seized the farms and the factories, and millions of people died in famines and prisons. In the late 1930s, Stalin even had many of his own generals and officials arrested and shot. The other kind was called fascismFASH-iz-umA system of government in which a dictator rules, the nation and the state count for more than the individual, opposition is forbidden, and war is praised., a system that praises the nation and the state above the individual, demands total loyalty to a single leader, and glorifies war.

Fascism began in Italy. Benito Mussolinibuh-NEE-toh moo-suh-LEE-neeThe fascist dictator of Italy from 1922 until 1943., a newspaper editor and war veteran who had once been a Socialist, took power in 1922 after his followers marched on Rome. The marchers were poorly armed, and Mussolini himself waited in Milan. The king could have ordered the army to stop them, but he feared a civil war and invited Mussolini to form a government instead, and Mussolini came to Rome by train. Within a few years he ruled as a dictator, and the Italians called him Il Duce, "the leader." In October 1935, he sent his army to conquer Ethiopia, one of the few free countries in Africa. Italian tanks, planes, and poison gas met soldiers armed largely with rifles and spears. The League of Nations protested but did little. In 1936, Emperor Haile Selassie of Ethiopia went to the League's meeting in Geneva and warned that its failure to act would be a lesson to other aggressors. Its weakness was plain to see, and the United States was not even a member.

In Germany, the road was different but the result was the same. After losing the war, Germany suffered inflation, then unemployment during the Depression. By the end of 1923, prices rose so fast that a loaf of bread cost billions of marks, and by 1932 about six million Germans were out of work. Many Germans were angry and afraid. The Nazi PartyNAHT-see PAR-teeThe German political party led by Hitler. It taught extreme nationalism, hatred of Jews, and total obedience to the leader. promised to give them work, restore their pride, and punish their enemies. Its leader, Adolf HitlerAY-dolf HIT-lerThe leader of the Nazi Party, who became dictator of Germany in the 1930s and started the war in Europe in 1939., had spent time in prison in the 1920s and wrote a book in which he described his hatred of the Jews and his plans for conquest. In January 1933, he became chancellor. He did not seize the office. President Hindenburg, an old general, appointed him, after conservative politicians persuaded the president that they could control him. They were wrong. After a fire burned the parliament building in February, Hitler pressed the legislature in March to give him the power to make laws on his own. Within a year he had made himself dictator. In May 1933, students in German cities burned thousands of books by Jewish and other disliked authors in public bonfires. In 1935, new laws took away the rights of German Jews. In 1936, Hitler broke the Treaty of Versailles by sending troops into the Rhineland, an area next to France that was supposed to stay free of soldiers. France and Britain did nothing. Hitler had told his generals to pull back if the French army moved, and he later said that those days were the most nerve-racking of his life. That summer, Berlin hosted the Olympic Games. Americans listened as Jesse Owens, a Black sprinter from Ohio, won four gold medals in front of Hitler's guests, after a hard argument at home over whether the United States should stay away. In March 1938, he absorbed Austria, the country where he had been born, and rode into Vienna past cheering crowds.

In the Pacific, Japan was ruled by a government in which army officers had great power. In 1932, young naval officers had even murdered the prime minister, and no civilian leader dared stand up to the military after that. In 1931, the army seized Manchuria, a region of China. Japan walked out of the League of Nations in 1933 after the League criticized the seizure. The United States answered with words only. Secretary of State Henry Stimson said that his government would not recognize land taken by force. In 1937, Japan launched a full war against China. In December 1937, Japanese troops took the Chinese capital, Nanjing, and killed tens of thousands of civilians and prisoners. When Japanese planes sank an American gunboat, the USS Panay, on the Yangtze River in December 1937, Japan apologized and paid for the damage, and the American public wanted to forget it. Newsreel cameramen aboard the gunboat had filmed the attack, and the pictures were shown in American theaters, but they stirred more worry than anger. Few people yet imagined that the same country would someday attack Hawaii.

    Why did Americans not act? One reason was memory. Over 100,000 Americans had died in the Great War, and the peace had brought disappointment. In a poll in 1937, about seventy percent of Americans said that it had been a mistake to go to war in 1917. Between 1934 and 1936, a Senate group called the Nye Committeenye kuh-MIT-eeA Senate group, active from 1934 to 1936, that argued that bankers and arms makers had pushed the United States into the Great War., led by Senator Gerald Nye of North Dakota, held hearings that made headlines in which it argued that bankers and arms makers had pushed America into the war to make money. Historians now think the case was weak, but many Americans believed it. A popular book called Merchants of Death, and articles in magazines, added to the feeling. The result was isolationismeye-suh-LAY-shun-iz-umThe belief that a country should stay out of the quarrels and wars of other nations., the belief that the United States should stay out of the quarrels of other nations. Isolationists came from both parties and all walks of life. Some were pacifists. Some distrusted Britain. Some feared that war would give the president too much power. The Constitution, they pointed out, gives only Congress the power to declare war.

    In 1935, 1936, and 1937, Congress passed the Neutrality Actsnoo-TRAL-ih-tee aktsLaws passed in the 1930s that banned arms sales to countries at war and kept Americans out of war zones.. The first came in August 1935, as Italy prepared to invade Ethiopia, and Roosevelt signed it with reluctance. These laws banned the sale of weapons to countries at war and kept American ships and citizens out of war zones. Their aim was to avoid the mistakes that had led to war in 1917. But the laws made no difference between a bully and a victim. A country that was attacked could not buy weapons from the United States any more easily than the attacker could. When civil war broke out in Spain in 1936, the United States also stayed out, even though Germany and Italy sent planes and soldiers to help General Franco. In 1938, a proposal for a constitutional amendment that required a national vote before any declaration of war came close to reaching a vote in the House. Roosevelt worked hard to stop it, and the House kept it off the floor by a margin of 209 votes to 188.

    Roosevelt saw the danger more clearly than most, but he was also weaker than he had been. In 1936, he had told a crowd in western New York that he had seen war and hated it, and that he would work to keep the country out of it. The fight over court-packingKORT-pak-ingThe name critics gave to Roosevelt's 1937 plan to add justices to the Supreme Court so that it would favor his laws. had cost him allies in Congress. In October 1937, he gave a speech in Chicago in which he said that the world's lawless nations should be "quarantined," as a community cuts off the carrier of a disease. The public reaction was cold. Roosevelt took the hint and backed away. He later told an aide that it was a terrible thing to look over your shoulder when you are trying to lead, and to find that no one is there. The country's army at this time had fewer than 200,000 soldiers, and no one in Washington expected them to be used.

    Meanwhile, the British and French tried to avoid war by giving in to Hitler's demands. This policy is called appeasementuh-PEEZ-mentThe policy of giving in to an aggressor's demands in the hope of keeping the peace.. In September 1938, their leaders met Hitler in Munich, along with Mussolini. About three million ethnic Germans lived in the Sudetenland, and Hitler claimed that he was protecting them. In the Munich AgreementMYOO-nik uh-GREE-mentThe 1938 deal in which Britain and France let Germany take part of Czechoslovakia, hoping to avoid war., they agreed that Germany could take the Sudetenland, a border region of Czechoslovakia, in return for his promise to ask for nothing more. The Czechs were not even invited. Roosevelt, who had urged both sides to talk, sent Chamberlain a cable of two words, "Good man." Britain's prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, returned home, waved a paper signed by Hitler, and said that he had brought "peace for our time." The Czechs had mountain forts along that border, and giving them up left the rest of the country open. Winston Churchill, who was then out of office, said that Britain had been given a choice between shame and war, had chosen shame, and would get war anyway. Six months later, German troops took the rest of Czechoslovakia. Britain then promised to defend Poland.

    The cruelty of the Nazi government was also becoming harder to ignore. On the night of November 9, 1938, mobs across Germany, led by Nazi officials, smashed the shops of Jewish families and burned synagogues. The event is known as KristallnachtKRIS-tahl-nahktThe "night of broken glass," November 9 and 10, 1938, when Nazi mobs attacked Jewish homes, shops, and synagogues across Germany., the "night of broken glass." The excuse was the shooting of a German diplomat in Paris by a seventeen-year-old Jewish refugee. About seven thousand Jewish shops were smashed, and fire crews were told to protect only the buildings next door. Nearly one hundred Jews were killed, and about thirty thousand Jewish men were sent to camps. Roosevelt recalled the American ambassador from Berlin in protest. But most Americans, including many who were horrified, were not ready to let in large numbers of refugees. A poll that winter found that most disapproved of what the Nazis were doing and still did not want more refugees admitted. The immigration law of 1924, which you read about earlier, let in only about twenty-six thousand Germans a year, and in the early 1930s officials had admitted far fewer than that. In 1939, a ship carrying more than nine hundred Jewish refugees, the St. Louis, was turned away from Cuba and then was not allowed to land in the United States. It came close enough to Florida for passengers to see the lights of Miami. It sailed back to Europe. Several European countries took in the passengers, but Germany conquered most of those countries within a year, and many of the passengers were later murdered. That same year, a bill to admit twenty thousand Jewish refugee children from Germany died in Congress without ever coming to a vote.

    On September 1, 1939, German armies invaded Poland. Britain and France declared war two days later. Days earlier, Hitler had made a secret deal with Stalin, so that the two dictators could divide Poland between them. On September 17, Soviet troops marched into Poland from the east. Europe was at war again.

    Could a free people have seen this coming? Historians still argue about it. The classical republican tradition holds that a state must be able to defend itself and to see threats clearly. A free republic has a special difficulty. Its leaders must persuade a public that does not want to listen. In the 1930s, many Americans were wrong about Hitler, but they were not foolish. They remembered a real war and had real doubts about a new one. The question for the next two years was whether events, or leaders, would change their minds.
