Okinawa, the Bomb, and the Surrender

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By the spring of 1945, Germany was nearly beaten, and Japan was losing. But the closer the Americans came to Japan, the harder the Japanese fought. In March, American bombers had already burned much of Tokyo in a single night. The firebombing killed about 100,000 people and destroyed a quarter of the city. The raid used a new plan. General Curtis LeMay sent the B-29s in low, at night, loaded with fire bombs, over a city built largely of wood and paper. Dozens of other Japanese cities were burned in the same way over the following months. Yet Japan's government did not give in. The next stage of the war would show why.

On April 1, 1945, American soldiers and Marines landed on Okinawa, an island only about 350 miles from the Japanese mainland. It was Easter Sunday. The invasion force was the largest the Americans ever used in the Pacific. The Japanese commander, Lieutenant General Mitsuru Ushijima, did not try to stop the landing at the beaches. Instead he pulled his men back into the hills and caves and let the Americans come to him. The Americans took the beaches almost without a fight and then ran into ridges and caves where hidden guns covered every approach. The fighting ground on through rain and mud. The Battle of OkinawaBAT-ul uv oh-kih-NAH-wuhThe 82-day battle in 1945 for an island about 350 miles from Japan, the last and bloodiest battle of the Pacific War. lasted eighty-two days. More than twelve thousand Americans died on land and sea. More than a hundred thousand Japanese soldiers were killed. Tens of thousands of Okinawan civilians died as well, caught between the armies. Okinawan students, including teenage girls drafted as nurses, were pulled into the fighting, and many of them died. The American commander, General Simon Bolivar Buckner, was killed by a shell while watching the front on June 18, the highest-ranking American killed by enemy fire in the war. The Japanese commander took his own life a few days later.

At sea, the Americans met a new weapon. Japanese pilots began to fly their planes loaded with explosives straight into ships. In April, hundreds of planes came in waves. Destroyers stationed far out from the landing beaches, to warn the fleet by radar, took the first hits, and many were crippled or sunk. Japan's navy made one last effort as well. On April 7, it sent the Yamato, the largest battleship ever built, on what was in effect a suicide mission. American planes sank it within hours. They were called kamikazekah-mih-KAH-zeeA Japanese word meaning "divine wind." It names the pilots who flew explosive-filled planes into ships., a Japanese word meaning "divine wind." Many of the pilots were young and had only a few weeks of training, but a plane that struck its target did more damage than one that dropped a bomb and flew away. Over the course of the battle, these attacks sank dozens of ships and killed nearly five thousand sailors. It was the deadliest battle in the history of the United States Navy. To Americans in the fleet, the message was clear. If this was what it cost to take a small island, what would it cost to take Japan itself?

On April 12, 1945, in the middle of the Okinawa fighting, Roosevelt died of a stroke at his vacation home in Georgia. His successor was Harry S. TrumanHAIR-ee es TROO-munThe thirty-third president, who took office when Roosevelt died in April 1945., a former senator from Missouri who had been vice president for only eighty-two days. Roosevelt had told him almost nothing about the war plans. He was called to the White House that afternoon, where Eleanor Roosevelt told him that her husband was dead. He took the oath that evening in the Cabinet Room of the White House, with a few officials and his wife and daughter looking on. Only after he took the oath did Truman learn about the Manhattan Projectman-HAT-un PROJ-ektThe secret American program, begun in 1942, to build an atomic bomb.. On April 25, Secretary of War Henry Stimson sat down with him and described a new weapon of unheard-of power that would be ready within months. The new president, who had no experience with foreign affairs, now had to make the most serious decision any American leader had faced.

⚡ DECIDE FIRST
In April 1945, Truman became president knowing almost nothing about the biggest secrets of the war.
What might have been different if TrumanHAIR-ee es TROO-munThe thirty-third president, who took office when Roosevelt died in April 1945. had been fully briefed during his eighty-two days as vice president? Decide first and jot down two possible differences before you open this.
He would still have faced the same war, the same invasion plan, and the same secret weapon. But he might have had time to ask questions, hear from more advisers, and weigh his choices before facing them alone. The lesson does not tell us what he would have decided. This is reasoning from what we know, not a certain result.

The planning for an invasion of Japan was already under way. It was known as Operation Downfallop-er-AY-shun DOWN-fawlThe American plan to invade Japan, set to begin in November 1945.. The first stage, an attack on the southern island of Kyushu, was set for November 1945. A second landing, near Tokyo, would follow in the spring. Japan had many soldiers at home, and it was gathering thousands of planes for suicide attacks. Civilians were being trained to fight, some of them with bamboo spears. In the United States, the army ordered Purple Heart medals for the wounded by the hundreds of thousands. Experts guessed at how many Americans might die in the invasion, and their guesses varied widely. Historians still argue about the numbers. What is clear is that the leaders in Washington expected a battle that would be far worse than Okinawa. Many Japanese civilians would die too.

At the same time, the Americans tried to end the war by other means. A tight naval blockade, together with bombing, was choking off Japan's food and fuel. American submarines had sunk most of Japan's merchant ships, and B-29s had dropped mines in its harbors and waterways. Japan imported much of its food and almost all of its oil, and by summer the rice ration was shrinking. In July 1945, Truman met with Churchill and Stalin at Potsdam, a city near Berlin. Halfway through the meeting, British voters ended Churchill's time as prime minister, and Clement Attlee took his seat. Stalin repeated his promise to join the war against Japan in August. On July 26, the United States, Britain, and China issued the Potsdam DeclarationPOTS-dam dek-luh-RAY-shunThe July 26, 1945, demand by the United States, Britain, and China that Japan surrender or face "prompt and utter destruction.". It called on Japan to surrender at once, or face "prompt and utter destruction." The declaration did not say what would happen to the emperor, and that was a key question for Japan's leaders. Japan's government answered that it would "ignore" the declaration. The word could be read in different ways, but the Americans took it as a refusal.

Meanwhile, the work in the desert had succeeded. On July 16, before dawn, scientists exploded the first atomic bombuh-TOM-ik bomA weapon whose great force comes from splitting atoms. at a site in New Mexico called Trinity. The device sat on top of a steel tower in the desert near Alamogordo. When it went off, the flash lit the sky and was seen for many miles, and the tower was gone. An atomic bomb is a weapon that gets its force from splitting atoms, and its power far exceeded that of any earlier weapon. Not everyone who worked on the weapon wanted it used without warning, and some scientists urged a demonstration. But a committee of advisers led by Stimson, meeting in June, had recommended that it be used without warning on a military target surrounded by workers' homes. Truman received the news at Potsdam. He told Stalin only in a vague way that the United States had a new weapon. Stalin barely reacted. His spies had already told him.

On Monday, August 6, a B-29 named the Enola Gay, after its pilot's mother, dropped a bomb on the city of Hiroshima at about quarter past eight in the morning. Hiroshima was an army headquarters and a port. The firebombing raids had mostly spared it, which left an untouched city on which to measure the weapon's effect. Kyoto, once near the top of the list of targets, had been struck off by Stimson, who knew its importance to the Japanese. The Hiroshima bomb used uranium, while the Trinity device had used plutonium. About 70,000 people died almost at once, and the total by the end of the year was perhaps twice that. Truman, who got the word at lunch with sailors aboard the cruiser USS Augusta on his way home from Potsdam, announced the news to the American people and warned that more would follow if Japan did not give up. On August 8, the Soviet Union, which had promised to join the war, declared war on Japan and attacked Japanese forces in Manchuria. The attack was huge, with well over a million Soviet troops, and it quickly overwhelmed the Japanese army there. On August 9, a second bomb, a plutonium bomb like the one tested at Trinity, fell on Nagasaki. The plane had been sent to another city, Kokura, but clouds covered it, so the crew went on to its second target. Perhaps 40,000 died at once at Nagasaki, and by the end of the year, about 70,000.

⚡ PUT IT IN ORDER
Put these events of 1945 in order, from earliest to latest. Tap them one at a time.

    Even then, Japan's leaders were split. Some members of the war council wanted to surrender if the emperor kept his position. Others wanted to fight on. The council's six members were split evenly, three and three. The army's leaders wanted three more conditions: no occupation, Japanese handling of disarmament, and Japanese trials of any war criminals. The council met in an underground shelter at the palace in the middle of the night of August 9 to 10, and still could not agree. The prime minister, Kantaro Suzuki, then did something unusual and asked the emperor to decide. The emperor, Hirohitoheer-oh-HEE-tohThe emperor of Japan from 1926 to 1989, whose radio speech announced Japan's surrender., broke the tie and called for surrender. Japan asked if it could keep him, and the Allies replied in a way that allowed it. The reply put the emperor's authority under the Allied commander but did not remove him, and even that wording set off another argument in Tokyo that lasted for days.

    Some officers refused to accept the decision. On August 14, the emperor ruled again in favor of accepting the Allied reply, and he recorded his speech that night. Later that night, a group of army officers tried to stop the surrender. They seized part of the palace and searched for the recording of the emperor's speech, which had been made earlier and hidden. They failed, and the plot collapsed by dawn. On August 15, for the first time, the Japanese people heard the voice of their emperor, in a radio speech announcing the end of the war. He spoke in the formal language of the court, which many listeners found hard to follow, and he did not use the word surrender. Americans celebrated what they called V-J DayVEE-jay day"Victory over Japan Day," the celebration of the end of the war with Japan, in August 1945., for Victory over Japan. Crowds filled Times Square in New York. The formal surrender took place on September 2, aboard the battleship USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, with General MacArthur presiding. A flag that Commodore Perry had flown in Tokyo Bay in 1853 hung on the ship for the ceremony.

    Was the use of the bomb right? Historians have argued about it for decades, and you should look at both sides. Those who defended it said that it ended the war quickly and saved many lives, American and Japanese, that would have been lost in an invasion or in a longer blockade. They pointed out that Japan had not surrendered after the firebombing, after the loss of its navy, or after the Potsdam Declaration. Critics said that Japan was already close to defeat and that a demonstration of the weapon, a clearer promise about the emperor, or a short wait for the Soviet attack might have worked. Some also argued that deliberately killing so many civilians broke the old rule that soldiers must protect those who are not fighting. Even some officers who served in the war doubted that the bombs had been needed.

    ⚡ WHERE WOULD YOU PLACE IT?
    How settled is the debate over whether the bomb was needed to end the war?

    Notice that every choice in 1945 was made with limited knowledge, in the middle of a war that had already killed tens of millions. The leaders could not see how things would turn out, and the next day might have gone in different directions. A clouded sky saved Kokura. A tie vote in a Japanese council was broken by one man. As you read Truman's own statement, ask what he said, what he did not say, and what questions it leaves open.

    When the war ended, the United States stood as the strongest country on earth. It held a weapon that no other nation had. It faced a new rival, the Soviet Union, that had also grown mighty. American troops that had expected to storm Japan's beaches arrived as an occupying force, under General MacArthur. The story of the new struggle begins next quarter.
