📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In a speech in December 1940, Roosevelt called the United States "the great arsenal of democracy." By 1942, the phrase was no longer just a hope. The country was now the largest factory in the world. Wars of the twentieth century were won by those who could make more tanks, ships, and planes than the enemy, and the United States did this better than anyone. The home front, as Americans called it, was where much of the war was decided.

The change started in early 1942. The government told car companies to stop making cars. Within months, the same factories were making tanks, jeeps, and aircraft engines. Roosevelt set a goal of sixty thousand planes in a single year, which seemed impossible, and the country met it. Henry Ford's new plant at Willow Run, Michigan, was one of the largest factory buildings in the world and was built to make B-24 bombers. At its peak, it turned out one bomber an hour. In the shipyards of Henry Kaiser, workers built the Liberty shipLIB-ur-tee shipA cheap cargo ship, built from standard parts with welded seams, that American yards produced by the thousands in the war., a cheap cargo vessel made from standard parts, with welded seams in place of rivets. One Liberty ship, the Robert E. Peary, was finished in a little under five days. Over the course of the war, American yards built more than two thousand of them. These ships could carry food and weapons faster than German submarines could sink them.

The Great Depression ended because of the war. Unemployment, which had been above twenty percent in 1933, fell below two percent by 1944. Wages rose. But money could not buy everything, because factories were making weapons instead of goods for families. To make sure there was enough to go around, the government began rationingRASH-un-ingA system that limits how much of a scarce good each person may buy., which limits how much of something each person may buy. Families received books of stamps for sugar, meat, butter, gasoline, and rubber tires. Citizens also bought war bondswor bondzBonds sold to citizens to lend money to the government in wartime, to be paid back later with interest., which let them lend money to the government and be paid back later with interest. Americans planted victory gardens and collected scrap metal. This kind of cooperation is what the classical republican tradition calls civic virtue, which means that ordinary people accepted limits on themselves for the common good. The American people did not do it perfectly, and a black market in rationed goods grew. But they did it on a scale that surprised even their leaders.

Women were central to the change. About six million women entered the workforce during the war, many taking jobs in factories that had been closed to them. Posters and songs celebrated "Rosie the Riveter." Women welded ships, built planes, and ran machines. Others served in the military in nonfighting roles, as nurses, pilots who delivered planes, and radio operators. When the war ended, most women left their jobs, either because they chose to or because they were told to. Yet the experience had changed what many women expected from life.

The war also changed things for Black Americans, though not by itself. The Great Migration of Black families from the South to the cities of the North and West continued, since the best war jobs were there. But those jobs were not given freely. In 1941, the labor leader A. Philip Randolphay FIL-ip RAN-dolfA Black labor leader whose threat of a march on Washington in 1941 helped win a ban on discrimination in defense jobs. threatened to bring a hundred thousand Black marchers to Washington to demand equal hiring. Roosevelt did not want the embarrassment, so he issued an order that banned discrimination in defense industries and set up the Fair Employment Practices Committeefair em-PLOY-ment PRAK-tis-iz kuh-MIT-eeA federal committee set up in 1941 to enforce the ban on job discrimination in defense industries. to enforce it. The committee had little power, but it was an important first step. The armed forces stayed segregated. Even so, Black soldiers, sailors, and airmen served with courage. The Tuskegee Airmen, Black pilots trained in Alabama, escorted bombers over Europe and earned an excellent record. Tension at home also ran high. In June 1943, a riot in Detroit killed thirty-four people. Many Black Americans spoke of a "double victory," over fascism abroad and over racism at home.

The darkest chapter on the home front involved Japanese Americans. After Pearl Harbor, fear spread on the West Coast that Japan might invade, and that people of Japanese descent could help it. There was no proof that any Japanese American was a spy. Still, on February 19, 1942, Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066ig-ZEK-yuh-tiv OR-der nyn-oh-siks-siksThe February 1942 order that let the army remove people of Japanese descent from the West Coast.. It gave the army power to remove people from areas it judged important for defense. In practice, this meant that about 120,000 people of Japanese descent, most of whom lived in California, Oregon, and Washington, were forced from their homes. About two-thirds of them were American citizens. They were sent to camps in remote places, such as Manzanar in California and Heart Mountain in Wyoming, and held behind barbed wire. Many lost their homes, farms, and businesses. No Japanese American was ever convicted of spying.

A young man named Fred Korematsufred kor-eh-MAHT-sooA Japanese American who refused to go to the camps and took his case to the Supreme Court. refused to go. He was arrested and convicted. His case reached the Supreme Court. In December 1944, in Korematsu v. United States, the Court ruled six to three that the order was allowed because of military necessity. Justice Frank Murphy, in a dissent, said that the order went over "the very brink of constitutional power" and fell into "the ugly abyss of racism." The decision has not been supported by later generations. Congress passed a law in 1988 that apologized and paid twenty thousand dollars to each survivor of the camps. In 2018, the Court itself said that Korematsu was "gravely wrong the day it was decided." You will read parts of the opinion in this week's source. Meanwhile, young Japanese American men in the army, the 442nd Regimental Combat Team, fought in Italy and France. For its size and length of service, it became the most decorated unit in American history.

Back at home, Congress also planned for the future. In 1944, it passed the GI BillJEE eye bilThe 1944 law that gave veterans help with college, home loans, and finding work., a law that offered veterans money for college, loans to buy homes and start businesses, and help in finding work. Millions of veterans used it, and the law shaped the next generation of American life.

One part of the home front was kept secret from almost everybody. In 1939, scientists had warned Roosevelt that Germany might be able to build a bomb of tremendous power. By 1942, the United States had launched the Manhattan Projectman-HAT-un PROJ-ektThe secret American program, begun in 1942, to build an atomic bomb., a huge secret program to build such a weapon first. It was led by General Leslie Groves and the physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer. Workers at Oak Ridge in Tennessee, at Hanford in Washington, and at Los Alamos in New Mexico built different parts of it. More than a hundred thousand people worked on the project. Most of them did not know what they were making. It cost about two billion dollars.

The home front shows a republic at its best and at its worst. It gave an example of freely chosen sacrifice and fast cooperation. It also showed that a nation can give up the rights of a group of its own citizens out of fear. The next lesson turns back to the fighting and to the use of the weapon that came out of the secret project.
