📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Vietnam was a small country in Southeast Asia that most Americans in 1960 could not have found on a map. For about a century, it had been part of the French empire. After the Second World War, a communist nationalist named Ho Chi Minhhoh chee minThe communist nationalist who led North Vietnam. led a rebellion to drive the French out. Ho had spent about thirty years abroad, in places as different as Paris and Moscow, before he returned to lead the fight. In September 1945, when he announced his country's independence in Hanoi, he began with words borrowed from the American Declaration of Independence, that all men are created equal. The French fought for eight years and lost. The last great battle was at Dien Bien Phu, a French base in a mountain valley. Ho's soldiers dragged heavy guns by hand up the hills around it, and the French garrison surrendered after a siege of about two months. In 1954, after that defeat, an international meeting at Geneva divided the country at the 17th parallel. The north was run by Ho's communists, and the south by an anti-communist government. Elections to reunite the country were promised for 1956, but they were never held.

The United States soon took on the defense of South Vietnam. Eisenhower explained why with the domino theoryDOM-ih-noh THEER-eeThe belief that if one country fell to communism, its neighbors would fall one after another., the belief that if one country in a region fell to communism, its neighbors would fall one after another, like a row of dominoes. He had first used that picture at a news conference in 1954. Americans had just seen China fall, and Korea was a fresh memory. The idea had force, and it was also a guess about the future that no one could prove. The Americans backed a South Vietnamese leader, Ngo Dinh Diem, and sent money, weapons, and military advisers. Diem, with American approval, refused to hold the 1956 elections, because American officials feared that Ho would win them. Under Kennedy, the number of advisers grew from about nine hundred to roughly sixteen thousand. Some advisers pushed a plan to move villagers into fortified settlements called strategic hamlets, in the hope of cutting the rebels off from the people. Many farmers resented being moved away from their fields and their family graves.

In the south, a communist-led rebel movement, which Americans called the Viet Congvee-ET kongThe communist-led rebels who fought against the South Vietnamese government., fought a war of ambushes and terror against the government, with help from the north. Diem was an unpopular ruler, who favored his own Catholic minority in a country that was mostly Buddhist. In June 1963, an elderly Buddhist monk burned himself to death at a Saigon street corner to protest the government's treatment of Buddhists, and a photograph of the scene went around the world. In November 1963, a group of South Vietnamese generals overthrew Diem and killed him. The United States had signaled that it would not stand in their way. Three weeks later, Kennedy was dead, and Johnson inherited a war that was already going badly.

The event that opened the door to a bigger war happened in August 1964. On August 2, North Vietnamese boats fired on an American destroyer, the USS Maddox, in the Gulf of Tonkin. The Maddox had been collecting radio and radar information near the coast at a time when South Vietnamese raiders, helped by the United States, were attacking the North's shoreline. Two days later, the Maddox and another ship reported a second attack. Later studies showed that the second attack almost certainly never happened, and that bad weather and nervous radar operators had been the cause. Within hours, the captain of the Maddox cabled that freak weather and overeager operators might explain many of the reports, and he asked for a review. But the next day, American planes bombed North Vietnamese torpedo-boat bases.

At the time, Johnson called it an unprovoked attack. He asked Congress for authority to respond, and on August 7, it passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolutiongulf uv TON-kin rez-uh-LOO-shunThe August 1964 vote of Congress that let the president take "all necessary measures" in Vietnam., by a vote of 88 to 2 in the Senate and 416 to 0 in the House. The two senators who voted no were Wayne Morse of Oregon and Ernest Gruening of Alaska, and the House debated the resolution for only about forty minutes. It allowed the president to take "all necessary measures" to repel attacks and prevent further aggression. You will read the text this week. Notice what it does not say. It is not a declaration of war, and it contains no limit on the length or size of any fighting. Johnson is said to have joked that it was like grandma's nightshirt, because it covered everything.

After winning the election of 1964, in part by promising not to send American boys to fight an Asian war, Johnson began to do exactly that. In February 1965, after the Viet Cong attacked an American base at Pleiku and killed eight Americans, he ordered bombing of the north. The campaign, Operation Rolling Thunderop-er-AY-shun ROH-ling THUN-derThe American bombing campaign against North Vietnam that lasted from 1965 to 1968., lasted for three years. It was meant to push Hanoi to negotiate, but it did not. The plan was to turn up the pressure slowly, so that Hanoi would feel it and give way. Instead, North Vietnam dug in, rebuilt its roads and bridges, and put civilians to work on repairs. In Washington, Johnson and his advisers chose targets at weekly lunches in the White House, and he reportedly boasted that the military could not bomb so much as an outhouse without his permission. In March, Marines landed at Da Nang to guard an air base. Their job was defense, but within weeks the Marines were allowed to go out on patrol and fight, and more units followed.

In July, Johnson announced that he was sending 125,000 men, and that more would follow. He made the decision after weeks of debate with advisers. One of them, Undersecretary of State George Ball, warned in a long memo that the United States could not win a long war in the jungle against a determined enemy, and that it would be drawn in deeper and deeper. Johnson read the memo, and then went ahead. He announced the decision at a midday news conference, not in a speech to the nation at night, and he called up no reserve units, which would have alarmed the public. Instead, the monthly draft calls roughly doubled. He did not put it to Congress or ask the nation to make sacrifices. He did not want to endanger the Great Society, and he did not want to scare China into entering the war. Contingency matters here: at several points, a different decision by Johnson might have led to a very different war, or to no war at all.

The American commander, General William WestmorelandWIL-yum west-MOR-lundThe American general who commanded U.S. forces in Vietnam from 1964 to 1968., fought a war of attritionuh-TRISH-unA strategy of wearing the enemy down by killing more of his soldiers than he can replace.. That is a strategy of wearing the enemy down by killing more of his soldiers than he can replace. American troops went out on "search and destroy" missions, and success was measured by a "body count." Robert McNamara, the secretary of defense, believed that a war could be measured in numbers, and the body count gave him one. But the enemy did not need to win battles. It only had to avoid losing and outlast American patience. American helicopters could lift soldiers into a battle within minutes, and American bombs and artillery were far heavier than anything the enemy had. But the enemy chose when and where to fight, and often broke off after a few days, before the Americans could bring that firepower to bear. The first big test came in November 1965, in the Ia Drang valley in the Central Highlands. Helicopters dropped American soldiers into a fight with North Vietnamese regulars, and after days of bitter combat, the Americans held the field. They drew the lesson that their firepower worked. The North Vietnamese drew another: to fight close, hugging the American troops so that American planes and artillery could not strike them without hitting their own men.

North Vietnam sent men and supplies down the Ho Chi Minh Trail through Laos and Cambodia. The Trail was not one road but a web of paths, bridges, and river crossings, and the Americans could bomb it for years without closing it. The Americans also had to fight a war among villages, where a farmer by day might be a guerrilla at night. Mines and booby traps wounded many soldiers who never saw an enemy. Each soldier served a one-year tour, so a unit's experienced men were always leaving as new men arrived, and many counted the days until their year was up. The government in Saigon, which had changed hands several times through coups after Diem's fall, struggled to win the loyalty of its own villagers. By the end of 1967, there were about 485,000 American troops in Vietnam, and the number kept rising to over 540,000 in 1969. Many of them were drafted. Young men who were in college, or who could find a doctor's note, could often avoid service, so the burden fell heavily on the poor and working class.

By the end of 1967, Westmoreland was telling Americans that the enemy was losing and the end was in sight. On January 30, 1968, during the holiday of Tet, North Vietnam and the Viet Cong launched surprise attacks on more than one hundred cities and towns in South Vietnam, including an assault on the American embassy in Saigon. At the embassy, nineteen Viet Cong fighters blew a hole in the wall and held part of the grounds for about six hours before they were killed or captured, and none of them reached the main building. In the old imperial city of Hue, communist forces held much of the city for about a month, and American Marines and South Vietnamese troops had to retake it house by house. At the same time, the Marine base at Khe Sanh, in the northwest, was surrounded for weeks, and many Americans feared another Dien Bien Phu.

The Tet Offensivetet uh-FEN-sivThe surprise attacks on cities and towns in South Vietnam that began on January 30, 1968, during the Tet holiday. was a military defeat for the communists. They lost tens of thousands of fighters, and they did not spark the uprising that they had hoped for. But it was a political disaster for Johnson. Americans had been told the war was being won. Now they saw the fighting at the embassy on television. A news photograph of a South Vietnamese police chief shooting a captured Viet Cong fighter in a Saigon street shocked many who saw it. The difference between what the government said and what people saw was called the credibility gapkred-uh-BIL-ih-tee gapThe difference between what the government said about the Vietnam War and what people could see for themselves.. The famous television anchor Walter Cronkite, after a trip to Vietnam, told his audience that the war looked like a stalemate. Johnson is said to have remarked that if he had lost Cronkite, he had lost the middle of the country. Westmoreland also asked for about two hundred thousand more troops, and when the request leaked into the newspapers, it only added to the doubts.

On March 31, 1968, Johnson went on television. He announced a partial halt to the bombing, offered to talk, and then stunned the country by saying that he would not run for another term. He said that he wanted to give all of his time in his last months to the search for peace. The war that he had expanded had cost him his presidency. Peace talks opened in Paris, but the fighting went on for five more years.

The Vietnam War asks a hard question of any republic. The Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war, but Congress gave away its role in this case with one vote, in a few days, on partly false information. The president fought the war without a clear definition of victory. Johnson had set limits on what he would do, and the enemy had set none. A classical republican would ask whether a people who are not asked to sacrifice for a war can be expected to sustain it. Johnson's critics say no, and that is one reason the public mood turned. You will weigh these questions in this week's discussion, which asks whether America could have won. The next lesson looks at what was happening at home while the war went on.
