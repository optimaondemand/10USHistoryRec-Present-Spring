Nixon's Grand Design and Its Costs

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Richard NixonRICH-erd NIK-sunThe 37th president, from 1969 to 1974. He is the only president to have resigned from office. won the presidency in 1968 by a narrow margin. He had spent twenty years in politics, first as a congressman, then as Eisenhower's vice president, and then as a man who had lost two elections in a row. The first loss was the race for president in 1960, which he lost to John F. Kennedy by about one hundred thousand votes out of nearly seventy million. The second was a race for governor of California in 1962. After that defeat he told reporters, bitterly, that they would not have Nixon to kick around anymore. Six years later he was in the White House. He took office with a hard problem and a plan. The problem was Vietnam. The plan was to end the war without making America look weak, a goal he called "peace with honor." He chose as his main adviser a Harvard professor of foreign policy named Henry KissingerHEN-ree KISS-in-jerNixon's national security adviser and later secretary of state. He planned the opening to China and the arms talks with the Soviets.. Kissinger had come to America as a teenager in 1938, one of many Jewish refugees from Nazi Germany, and he never lost his accent. He believed that a nation's power, more than its ideals, decided what happened in the world. He and Nixon ran American foreign policy from the White House with little help from anyone else, and the secretary of state, William Rogers, was often the last to hear what they had decided.

Nixon's first step was a policy known as Vietnamizationvee-et-nah-mih-ZAY-shunNixon's plan to hand the ground fighting to the South Vietnamese army while American troops went home a little at a time.. The idea was to hand the ground fighting back to the South Vietnamese army, while American troops went home a little at a time. Nixon announced the first cut, 25,000 men, in June 1969, after meeting South Vietnam's president, Nguyen Van Thieu, on the island of Midway. More cuts followed every few months, each announced on television. As the Americans left, the South Vietnamese army grew to about a million men and received American rifles, trucks, and aircraft. What it could not copy quickly was American air power, which still did much of the fighting. American forces in Vietnam fell from about 540,000 in 1969 to under 30,000 by the end of 1972. At the same time, the United States gave South Vietnam weapons and money, and kept up heavy bombing. Nixon explained the thinking in a wider form, which became known as the Nixon DoctrineNIK-sun DOK-trinNixon's policy that America would keep its treaty promises and help its allies, but those allies would supply most of the soldiers for their own defense.. America would keep its treaty promises and would help its allies, but those allies would have to provide most of the soldiers needed for their own defense.

The war did not end quickly. In the spring of 1970, Nixon sent troops into Cambodia to destroy enemy bases there. The move set off protests across the country, and it led to the shootings at Kent State that you read about in the last lesson. Nixon also kept the secret bombing of Cambodia hidden from the public, and he kept the details from most of Congress. That bombing had begun in March 1969, more than a year before the ground troops crossed the border. North Vietnamese soldiers used the Cambodian side of the line to rest and store supplies, and Nixon wanted them driven out. But he feared the protests the news would cause, so the air force kept two sets of records. The official reports placed the bombs inside South Vietnam, and a separate set of secret files showed where they really fell. The full story did not come out until 1973.

Meanwhile, peace talks in Paris dragged on for years. In the spring of 1972, North Vietnam struck hard. Its tanks and soldiers poured into South Vietnam in what became known as the Easter Offensive, and the South Vietnamese army held only with the help of American bombers. Nixon answered by ordering mines laid in the harbor at Haiphong, the main port of North Vietnam. Then, just before the election, came a sudden hope. On October 26, 1972, Kissinger told reporters that peace was at hand. Thieu was furious. The draft terms would let North Vietnamese soldiers stay in his country, and he refused to sign. By December the talks had collapsed, and Nixon ordered a fierce new bombing campaign against Hanoi and Haiphong that lasted about eleven days around Christmas. Then the North returned to the table. The terms that were finally signed were close to the ones Thieu had refused. Nixon sent him secret letters promising that America would strike back if the North broke the agreement. Finally, in January 1973, the two sides signed the Paris Peace AccordsPAIR-iss PEES uh-KORDZThe January 1973 agreement that ended direct American fighting in Vietnam. American troops left and prisoners of war came home.. The United States agreed to withdraw its remaining troops, and North Vietnam agreed to return American prisoners of war. Nearly six hundred of them came home, the first in February. But North Vietnamese soldiers stayed in the south, and nothing in the agreement stopped the fighting between the two Vietnamese governments. Many observers saw the agreement as a "decent interval," a pause meant to let America leave before South Vietnam collapsed.

Congress reacted to the way the war had been fought. Many members felt shut out. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution of 1964 had let a president widen the war without a declaration of war, and Nixon had bombed a neutral country in secret. In November 1973, Congress passed the War Powers ResolutionWOR POW-erz rez-uh-LOO-shunA 1973 law that requires a president to tell Congress soon after sending troops into combat, and to bring them home within sixty days unless Congress approves. over Nixon's veto. Overriding a veto takes two thirds of both the House and the Senate, which shows how widely the feeling ran. The law said the president must tell Congress within forty-eight hours of sending troops into combat, and must withdraw them within sixty days unless Congress approves. Presidents since then have argued that the law limits their powers more than the Constitution allows, and they have often worked around it. The question of who decides when America goes to war, which you met in the last lesson, remains unsettled. The draft also ended in 1973, and America turned to an all-volunteer army. Nixon had promised in 1968 to end it. For several years, young men had lived with a lottery, in which birthdays were drawn one by one in a live broadcast and the date that came up decided how soon a man would be called. That fear was gone.

While the war wound down, Nixon and Kissinger made a pair of surprising moves in the wider world. For twenty years, the United States had refused to recognize the communist government of China. Americans had even been forbidden to travel there. But the two Cold War rivals, China and the Soviet Union, had grown to distrust each other, and they had fought a border clash in 1969. Kissinger saw an opening. The first sign was small. In April 1971, an American table tennis team playing in Japan was invited to visit China, and the players became the first Americans in years to be welcomed there. In July, Kissinger made a secret trip to Beijing. He was visiting Pakistan, whose president had agreed to help, and he told reporters that he had a stomach ailment and needed to rest. The next morning he was on a plane to Beijing to meet China's premier, Zhou Enlai. A few days after he returned, Nixon surprised the nation by announcing on television that he would visit China.

In February 1972, Nixon himself landed in China. Cameras followed him down the steps of the plane as he walked toward Zhou with his hand held out. The gesture was planned. In 1954, at a conference in Geneva, the American secretary of state, John Foster Dulles, had refused to shake Zhou's hand. Later that day Nixon met Mao Zedong, the aging founder of communist China. The leader who had built his career on fighting communism was now the first president to visit a communist capital. Few other leaders could have done it, because few others had Nixon's record of anticommunism to protect them from the charge of being "soft." The two governments ended the trip with a statement called the Shanghai Communiqué. On Taiwan, the United States said that it knew Chinese on both sides of the Taiwan Strait held that there is one China, and that it did not challenge that view. It also said that the question should be settled peacefully. The visit did not bring full diplomatic ties right away, but it changed the balance of the Cold War. The Soviets now had to worry about a friendship between their two largest rivals.

Three months later, Nixon traveled to Moscow. The timing was remarkable. Only two weeks before, Nixon had ordered the mining of the North Vietnamese harbor, a move that threatened Soviet supply ships among others, and many expected the Soviet leader, Leonid Brezhnev, to cancel the meeting. He did not. Both men wanted an arms agreement, and neither would let a war in a third country stand in the way. The Soviet Union had reached rough equality with the United States in nuclear weapons, and both sides were spending huge sums to stay even. In 1972, they signed the first agreement of the Strategic Arms Limitation Talksstruh-TEE-jik ARMZ lim-ih-TAY-shun TAWKSNegotiations, called SALT, in which the United States and the Soviet Union agreed in 1972 to limit the number of certain nuclear missiles each could build., called SALT I, which set limits on the number of missiles each side could build. The limits were a freeze, not a cut, and they ran for five years. They said nothing about how many warheads each missile could carry, and as missiles were fitted with several warheads apiece, the number of warheads kept climbing. The two sides also signed a treaty that sharply limited anti-ballistic missile systems, which were designed to shoot down incoming warheads. The thinking was that if neither side could defend itself, neither would dare to strike first. This easing of tensions became known as détenteday-TAHNTA French word meaning a loosening or relaxing. It names the easing of Cold War tensions between the United States and the Soviet Union in the 1970s., a French word meaning a loosening or relaxing. Soviet and American leaders met, trade grew, and the danger of war seemed to shrink.

Nixon's critics attacked détente from both sides. Some on the left said that he was too willing to prop up harsh governments in the name of stability, as in Chile and Pakistan. Some on the right said that détente let the Soviets catch up while America pretended all was well. Defenders answer that it kept the peace between two armed superpowers, and that Kissinger's patient bargaining was exactly what a republic needs from its diplomats. As always, it is worth asking what might have happened otherwise. Without the opening to China, the Soviets might have faced no pressure at all. But it is also true that Moscow kept building missiles, and it kept making trouble around the world, during the years of détente.

At home, the early 1970s brought an economic shock. Trouble had started even before the oil crisis. Prices were climbing faster than they had in years. Foreign governments that held dollars were allowed to trade them for gold at a fixed price, and they were beginning to ask for the gold. On August 15, 1971, Nixon went on television and ended that promise. In the same speech he froze wages and prices for ninety days, a step no president had ever taken in peacetime. It helped for a while. But controls only hold down prices that would otherwise be rising, and when they were loosened the prices began to climb again.

Then, in October 1973, came a shock from far away. Egypt and Syria attacked Israel on Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the Jewish year, and the United States helped to resupply the Israelis. In response, the Arab members of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, or OPECOH-pekThe Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, a group of oil-producing nations that tries to control the supply and price of oil., stopped selling oil to the United States. America had come to depend on imported oil, and the price of a barrel roughly quadrupled within a few months. Gas prices soared, and drivers waited in long lines at stations. In some states, drivers could buy gas only on days that matched the last number of their license plates. Congress set a national speed limit of fifty-five miles per hour to save fuel. Even after the embargo ended in March 1974, the price of oil stayed high. Now the economy faced an odd combination. Prices were rising fast, and the economy was also stuck and unemployment was high. Economists called it stagflationstag-FLAY-shunA time when prices rise quickly (inflation) while the economy is stuck and many people are out of work. The word blends "stagnation" and "inflation.", a blend of "stagnation" and "inflation." Earlier tools, which worked against one problem at a time, seemed useless against both at once. To fight rising prices, a government usually slows the economy by making loans costly, and that puts people out of work. To fight unemployment, it does the opposite, and that pushes prices up. Each cure made the other sickness worse. By 1975 unemployment reached nearly nine percent, the highest since the 1930s, and prices had risen by more than ten percent in 1974 alone. Neither Nixon nor the presidents after him found an easy answer.

When Nixon won reelection in November 1972, in one of the biggest landslides in history, his plan seemed to be working. He carried forty-nine of the fifty states against Senator George McGovern of South Dakota, an antiwar Democrat. America was leaving Vietnam, tensions were lower, and China and the Soviets were talking. Yet the war had cost the lives of more than 58,000 Americans, and it had cost the country its trust in its leaders. The Vietnamese had paid far more. Estimates of the dead, soldiers and civilians together, run well over a million. In early 1975, North Vietnam launched its last offensive. American combat forces were long gone, Congress had cut off money for American fighting in the region in 1973, and it would not vote large new sums to rescue South Vietnam. The army that Vietnamization had built collapsed in less than two months. On April 30, 1975, helicopters lifted Americans, and thousands of Vietnamese who had worked with them, from the American embassy and other sites in Saigon, while North Vietnamese tanks rolled into the city and the South Vietnamese government surrendered. Nixon was by then out of office. The cause of his fall was not the war in Asia but a scandal at home, which is the subject of the next lesson.
