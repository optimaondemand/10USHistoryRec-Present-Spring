📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Richard NixonRICH-erd NIK-sunThe 37th president, from 1969 to 1974. He is the only president to have resigned from office. won the presidency in 1968 by a narrow margin. He had spent twenty years in politics, first as a congressman, then as Eisenhower's vice president, and then as a man who had lost two elections in a row. He took office with a hard problem and a plan. The problem was Vietnam. The plan was to end the war without making America look weak, a goal he called "peace with honor." He chose as his main adviser a Harvard professor of foreign policy named Henry KissingerHEN-ree KISS-in-jerNixon's national security adviser and later secretary of state. He planned the opening to China and the arms talks with the Soviets., and the two of them ran American foreign policy from the White House with little help from anyone else.

Nixon's first step was a policy known as Vietnamizationvee-et-nah-mih-ZAY-shunNixon's plan to hand the ground fighting to the South Vietnamese army while American troops went home a little at a time.. The idea was to hand the ground fighting back to the South Vietnamese army, while American troops went home a little at a time. American forces in Vietnam fell from about 540,000 in 1969 to under 30,000 by the end of 1972. At the same time, the United States gave South Vietnam weapons and money, and kept up heavy bombing. Nixon explained the thinking in a wider form, which became known as the Nixon DoctrineNIK-sun DOK-trinNixon's policy that America would keep its treaty promises and help its allies, but those allies would supply most of the soldiers for their own defense.. America would keep its treaty promises and would help its allies, but those allies would have to provide most of the soldiers needed for their own defense.

The war did not end quickly. In the spring of 1970, Nixon sent troops into Cambodia to destroy enemy bases there. The move set off protests across the country, and it led to the shootings at Kent State that you read about in the last lesson. Nixon also kept the secret bombing of Cambodia hidden from the public, and he kept the details from most of Congress. Meanwhile, peace talks in Paris dragged on. Finally, in January 1973, the two sides signed the Paris Peace AccordsPAIR-iss PEES uh-KORDZThe January 1973 agreement that ended direct American fighting in Vietnam. American troops left and prisoners of war came home.. The United States agreed to withdraw its remaining troops, and North Vietnam agreed to return American prisoners of war. But North Vietnamese soldiers stayed in the south, and nothing in the agreement stopped the fighting between the two Vietnamese governments. Many observers saw the agreement as a "decent interval," a pause meant to let America leave before South Vietnam collapsed.

Congress reacted to the way the war had been fought. In November 1973, it passed the War Powers ResolutionWOR POW-erz rez-uh-LOO-shunA 1973 law that requires a president to tell Congress soon after sending troops into combat, and to bring them home within sixty days unless Congress approves. over Nixon's veto. The law said the president must tell Congress within forty-eight hours of sending troops into combat, and must withdraw them within sixty days unless Congress approves. Presidents since then have argued that the law limits their powers more than the Constitution allows, and they have often worked around it. The question of who decides when America goes to war, which you met in the last lesson, remains unsettled. The draft also ended in 1973, and America turned to an all-volunteer army.

While the war wound down, Nixon and Kissinger made a pair of surprising moves in the wider world. For twenty years, the United States had refused to recognize the communist government of China. Americans had even been forbidden to travel there. But the two Cold War rivals, China and the Soviet Union, had grown to distrust each other, and they had fought a border clash in 1969. Kissinger saw an opening. In July 1971, he made a secret trip to Beijing. In February 1972, Nixon himself landed in China and shook hands with Mao Zedong. The leader who had built his career on fighting communism was now the first president to visit a communist capital. Few other leaders could have done it, because few others had Nixon's record of anticommunism to protect them from the charge of being "soft." The visit did not bring full diplomatic ties right away, but it changed the balance of the Cold War. The Soviets now had to worry about a friendship between their two largest rivals.

Three months later, Nixon traveled to Moscow. The Soviet Union had reached rough equality with the United States in nuclear weapons, and both sides were spending huge sums to stay even. In 1972, they signed the first agreement of the Strategic Arms Limitation Talksstruh-TEE-jik ARMZ lim-ih-TAY-shun TAWKSNegotiations, called SALT, in which the United States and the Soviet Union agreed in 1972 to limit the number of certain nuclear missiles each could build., called SALT I, which set limits on the number of missiles each side could build. They also signed a treaty that sharply limited anti-ballistic missile systems, which were designed to shoot down incoming warheads. The thinking was that if neither side could defend itself, neither would dare to strike first. This easing of tensions became known as détenteday-TAHNTA French word meaning a loosening or relaxing. It names the easing of Cold War tensions between the United States and the Soviet Union in the 1970s., a French word meaning a loosening or relaxing. Soviet and American leaders met, trade grew, and the danger of war seemed to shrink.

Nixon's critics attacked détente from both sides. Some on the left said that he was too willing to prop up harsh governments in the name of stability, as in Chile and Pakistan. Some on the right said that détente let the Soviets catch up while America pretended all was well. Defenders answer that it kept the peace between two armed superpowers, and that Kissinger's patient bargaining was exactly what a republic needs from its diplomats. As always, it is worth asking what might have happened otherwise. Without the opening to China, the Soviets might have faced no pressure at all. But it is also true that Moscow kept building missiles, and it kept making trouble around the world, during the years of détente.

At home, the early 1970s brought an economic shock. In October 1973, Egypt and Syria attacked Israel, and the United States helped to resupply the Israelis. In response, the Arab members of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, or OPECOH-pekThe Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, a group of oil-producing nations that tries to control the supply and price of oil., stopped selling oil to the United States. Gas prices soared, and drivers waited in long lines at stations. Even after the embargo ended, the price of oil stayed high. Now the economy faced an odd combination. Prices were rising fast, and the economy was also stuck and unemployment was high. Economists called it stagflationstag-FLAY-shunA time when prices rise quickly (inflation) while the economy is stuck and many people are out of work. The word blends "stagnation" and "inflation.", a blend of "stagnation" and "inflation." Earlier tools, which worked against one problem at a time, seemed useless against both at once. Neither Nixon nor the presidents after him found an easy answer.

When Nixon won reelection in November 1972, in one of the biggest landslides in history, his plan seemed to be working. America was leaving Vietnam, tensions were lower, and China and the Soviets were talking. Yet the war had cost the lives of more than 58,000 Americans, and it had cost the country its trust in its leaders. In April 1975, North Vietnamese tanks rolled into Saigon, and the South Vietnamese government surrendered. Nixon was by then out of office. The cause of his fall was not the war in Asia but a scandal at home, which is the subject of the next lesson.
