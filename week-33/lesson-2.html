📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On the night of June 17, 1972, a security guard noticed a piece of tape holding a door lock open at the WatergateWAW-ter-gaytThe scandal that began with a 1972 break-in at Democratic Party offices in the Watergate complex and ended with Nixon's resignation., an office and apartment complex in Washington. He called the police. They arrested five men inside the offices of the Democratic National Committee. The burglars carried cameras and listening equipment. Nixon's press secretary called the event "a third-rate burglary attempt." In the months that followed, it became clear that it was something far larger.

The men were linked to the Committee to Re-elect the President, Nixon's campaign group. Money from the campaign had paid for the burglary, and the burglary was part of a wider set of illegal acts, often called "dirty tricks," against the president's political opponents. Nixon denied any part in the break-in, and he won reelection in a landslide a few months later. But two young reporters at the Washington Post, Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, kept digging, helped by a secret source inside the government. Meanwhile, Judge John Sirica, who presided over the burglars' trial, pressed the defendants to talk. One of them wrote a letter admitting that political pressure had been put on them to keep quiet.

The question was now whether the president had known. The Senate set up a special committee, led by Senator Sam Ervin of North Carolina, a student of the Constitution, and the hearings were shown on television during the summer of 1973. Nixon's former counsel, John Dean, testified that the president had taken part in a cover-up. It was his word against the president's. Then, in a quiet moment, a White House aide revealed that Nixon had a secret taping system in the Oval Office. Every conversation was on tape. The tapes could settle the matter, and everyone knew it.

Nixon's government now had to decide whether to hand them over. The Senate committee asked for them. So did the special prosecutorSPESH-ul PROS-ih-kyoo-terA lawyer appointed to investigate possible crimes by government officials without taking orders from the people being investigated., Archibald Cox, a lawyer appointed by the Justice Department to look into the case without taking orders from the president. Nixon refused. He claimed executive privilegeig-ZEK-yuh-tiv PRIV-uh-lijThe claim that a president may keep some conversations and records secret in order to receive honest advice., the idea that a president may keep some conversations secret in order to receive honest advice. Then, on October 20, 1973, Nixon ordered the attorney general to fire Cox. The attorney general, Elliot Richardson, and his deputy both resigned rather than obey. The press called these events the Saturday Night MassacreSAT-er-day NITE MASS-uh-kerThe night of October 20, 1973, when Nixon ordered the special prosecutor fired, and the attorney general and his deputy resigned rather than obey.. Public anger was huge, and the pressure on Nixon grew stronger. He appointed a new special prosecutor, Leon Jaworski, who also sued for the tapes.

The case went to the Supreme Court. In United States v. Nixon, decided in July 1974, the Court ruled unanimously, eight to zero, that executive privilege exists but is not absolute. When a president's claim of secrecy collides with the need for evidence in a criminal case, the Court held, the claim must give way. You will read part of the decision this week. It built on the principle you met earlier in the course, in Marbury v. Madison: the Court says what the law is, even to the president. This was a clear victory for the idea of a government of laws and not of men. It is worth noticing, though, that Nixon had a choice. He could have defied the Court, and then no army and no police officer would have forced him. What stopped him was the force of custom, public opinion, and the fear that Congress would act.

Congress was already acting. In late July 1974, the House Judiciary Committee voted on impeachmentim-PEECH-muntThe process by which the House of Representatives accuses an official of "high crimes and misdemeanors" so that the Senate can try the official., which is the process by which the House of Representatives accuses an officer of "high crimes and misdemeanors" so that the Senate can try him. The committee approved three articles, including obstruction of justiceub-STRUK-shun uv JUS-tisThe crime of illegally blocking or interfering with an investigation or a court case., meaning illegal efforts to block an investigation. Members from both parties voted for them. Then the Court ordered the tapes released. One of them, recorded six days after the break-in, showed Nixon ordering aides to tell the FBI to stop looking into the burglary for reasons of national security. It was the "smoking gun." Nixon's own supporters in Congress told him he would be convicted. On August 8, 1974, he announced his resignation, and on the next day he left Washington. He remains the only American president ever to resign.

Vice President Gerald FordJAIR-uld FORDThe 38th president. He became vice president in 1973 and president when Nixon resigned in 1974. took the oath. Ford was a decent and well-liked man from Michigan who had been appointed vice president only the year before, after Spiro Agnew resigned in a separate scandal over bribes. He told the nation that "our long national nightmare is over." A month later, he gave Nixon a full pardonPAR-dunA president's power to forgive a person for a federal crime and prevent charges or punishment., which is the power of a president to forgive a crime and to prevent charges. Ford said he wanted to spare the country a long and painful trial. Many Americans were furious, and they suspected a secret deal, though no evidence of one was ever found. The pardon probably cost Ford the election of 1976.

That election was won by Jimmy CarterJIM-ee KAR-terThe 39th president, from 1977 to 1981. He was a former governor of Georgia and a naval officer., a former governor of Georgia and a naval officer, who ran as an outsider and promised never to lie to the American people. His term was hard. Inflation and unemployment stayed high, and long lines for gas returned in 1979. In July of that year, after ten days of meetings at Camp David, Carter gave a television speech that surprised the nation. He said that America faced a "crisis of confidence," and that too many people had turned toward self-indulgence and consumption. Some praised his honesty. Others thought a president should offer hope and a plan, and they judged the speech a sign of weakness. Reporters later called it the "malaise" speech, though Carter never used that word. Within months, American hostages were taken in Iran, and the hard times worsened. You will read the speech this week.

So did the system work? One answer is yes. The press, the courts, the Congress, and public opinion all played their part, and a powerful president was forced from office without violence. Another answer is that the system came very close to failing. It took a lucky break, the discovery of the tapes, and the courage of a few officials. A classical republican would say that such a system depends on the habits of the people who work within it. Laws alone did not defeat the cover-up. Character did, and that includes the character of the men who refused to obey Nixon's order to fire Cox. You will weigh this question in this week's discussion.
