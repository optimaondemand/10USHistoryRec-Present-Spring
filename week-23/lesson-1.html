📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The winter of 1932 to 1933 was the lowest point of the Great Depression. Banks were closing across the country. In February, the governor of Michigan shut every bank in his state, and other governors followed. President Hoover and President-elect Roosevelt, who disliked each other, could not agree on a joint plan. The old president had little power left, and the new one could not act until his inauguration on March 4. The country waited.

The man who was about to take command was Franklin D. RooseveltFRANK-lin dee ROH-zuh-veltThe governor of New York who was elected president in 1932 and served until 1945.. He came from a wealthy New York family and was a distant cousin of Theodore Roosevelt. In 1921, at the age of thirty-nine, he was struck by polio and never walked unaided again. He refused to give up public life. He later served as governor of New York, and the long fight to rebuild his body had taught him how to keep going. His cheerful manner and his skill as a politician were as important as any of his plans. He also drew strength from his wife, Eleanor, who traveled the country and reported back on what she saw, and he chose talented people for his cabinet. One of them, Frances Perkins, became Secretary of Labor and the first woman ever to serve in a president's cabinet.

Just three weeks before he took office, his career nearly ended. On February 15, 1933, in Miami, a gunman named Giuseppe Zangara fired at Roosevelt as he sat in an open car. The bullets missed him. One of them struck Chicago's mayor, Anton Cermak, who died. It is worth pausing on that moment. If Zangara's aim had been a little different, the nation would have faced the banking crisis under Vice President John Nance Garner, a very different man. This is what historians mean by contingency. The great events of the Depression were shaped by chance as well as by plans.

Reform meant fixing the system so that disaster would not repeat. The Securities Act required companies selling stock to tell the truth about their business. The Glass-Steagall Act separated ordinary banks from risky investment firms. It also set up deposit insurancedih-POZ-it in-SHOOR-unsA government guarantee that repays a customer's savings if a bank fails. It was created in 1933., which guarantees that the government will repay a customer's savings if a bank fails. Your own family's bank account is protected by this idea today.

What was Roosevelt's method? He gathered a group of advisers, many of them college professors, whom reporters called the Brain Trustbrayn trustThe group of advisers, many of them college professors, whom Roosevelt gathered to help him plan the New Deal.. He tried many ideas at once. In a speech during the 1932 campaign, he explained his approach. "It is common sense to take a method and try it," he said. "If it fails, admit it frankly and try another. But above all, try something." That honesty won him trust, but it also meant the New Deal had no single theory. Some laws cut federal spending, such as the Economy Act, while others raised it. Some raised prices, which helped farmers but hurt consumers. A fair historian will not call the Hundred Days a perfect plan. It was a series of hurried experiments by people in an emergency.

Was the speed a strength or a danger? A republic must be able to deliberate and act. A country that cannot respond in a crisis has failed at one of its first duties. The Emergency Banking Act showed the benefits of quick action. The crisis ended, and the confidence of ordinary people returned. But a bill passed in an afternoon by members who had not read it is a risky way to make law. It places great trust in one person. Americans gave Roosevelt that trust, and for the moment they were glad they did. Whether the nation could keep the benefits of vigorous executive leadership without losing the habits of careful lawmaking would be a major question of the 1930s.

The Hundred Days ended with the banks open and hope rising, but the Depression was far from over. Unemployment remained about one worker in four.
