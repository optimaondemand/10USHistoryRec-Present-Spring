📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The winter of 1932 to 1933 was the lowest point of the Great Depression. Banks were closing across the country. In February, the governor of Michigan shut every bank in his state, and other governors followed. Michigan's governor acted on February 14, when the biggest banks in Detroit seemed about to fail, and he gave the state eight days of closed doors. People who could not reach their savings paid for groceries with scrip, IOUs, and whatever small change they had. By the day of the inauguration, almost every state had closed its banks or limited what a customer could take out. In the early hours of inauguration day itself, the governors of New York and Illinois closed their states' banks, and the New York Stock Exchange shut its doors. President Hoover and President-elect Roosevelt, who disliked each other, could not agree on a joint plan. The old president had little power left, and the new one could not act until his inauguration on March 4. The country waited.

The man who was about to take command was Franklin D. RooseveltFRANK-lin dee ROH-zuh-veltThe governor of New York who was elected president in 1932 and served until 1945.. He came from a wealthy New York family and was a distant cousin of Theodore Roosevelt. In 1921, at the age of thirty-nine, he was struck by polio and never walked unaided again. He could stand only with heavy steel braces locked at the knee, leaning on a cane and on the arm of a son or an aide. By an unspoken custom, reporters and photographers rarely showed him being lifted or pushed in a wheelchair. In 1926 he bought a run-down resort at Warm Springs, Georgia, where he swam in the warm water and built a center for other polio patients. He refused to give up public life. He later served as governor of New York, and the long fight to rebuild his body had taught him how to keep going. His cheerful manner and his skill as a politician were as important as any of his plans. He also drew strength from his wife, Eleanor, who was a distant cousin herself and whose uncle, President Theodore Roosevelt, had given her away at their wedding in 1905. She traveled the country and reported back on what she saw, and he chose talented people for his cabinet. One of them, Frances Perkins, became Secretary of Labor and the first woman ever to serve in a president's cabinet. Perkins had watched the Triangle Shirtwaist fire of 1911 in New York, in which 146 garment workers died, and she never forgot it. The new Treasury Secretary, William Woodin, was a Republican businessman who also wrote music.

Just three weeks before he took office, his career nearly ended. On February 15, 1933, in Miami, a gunman named Giuseppe Zangara fired at Roosevelt as he sat in an open car. Zangara, an unemployed bricklayer, stood on a wobbly chair to see over the crowd, and a woman beside him pushed his arm as he fired. The bullets missed Roosevelt. One of them struck Chicago's mayor, Anton Cermak, who died. Roosevelt had the wounded mayor lifted into his own car and held him on the ride to the hospital. It is worth pausing on that moment. If Zangara's aim had been a little different, the nation would have faced the banking crisis under Vice President John Nance Garner, a very different man. This is what historians mean by contingency. The great events of the Depression were shaped by chance as well as by plans.

In his inaugural address, Roosevelt told Americans that the only thing they had to fear was fear itself. He took the oath from Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes on a cold, gray day, and he said he would ask Congress for power as broad as if the country had been invaded by a foreign enemy. On Sunday, his first full day, he called Congress back for a special session to begin on Thursday, and Treasury officials worked through the weekend on a bill. Two days later, he declared a bank holidaybangk HAH-lih-dayA short, government-ordered closing of all banks. Roosevelt declared one in March 1933 to stop the panic., which closed every bank in the country for several days. On March 9, Congress met in a special session and passed the Emergency Banking Actih-MER-jun-see BANG-king aktThe law Congress passed in a single afternoon on March 9, 1933. It let the government inspect banks and reopen only the sound ones. in a single afternoon. The bill was so new that many members voted without having read it. Some of the men who wrote it were Hoover's own Treasury officials, who stayed on to help. In the House, the chairman held up the only copy, members debated for about forty minutes, and the bill passed with a shout. The Senate approved it that evening, 73 to 7. It allowed the government to inspect banks and reopen only the sound ones. It also let the Federal Reserve print more paper money, so that no bank that reopened would run out of cash. On Sunday night, March 12, Roosevelt spoke to the country by radio and explained in simple words how the plan worked. It was the first of his fireside chats. He assured listeners that it was safer to keep their money in a reopened bank than under the mattress. The next morning, banks in the cities with Federal Reserve banks opened first, and the rest followed over the next two days. When the banks reopened, people put money back in. The run was over. It was one of the quickest turnarounds in American politics.

Congress stayed in session until mid-June. In those Hundred DaysHUN-drid dayzThe first months of Roosevelt's presidency in 1933, when Congress passed fifteen major laws to fight the Depression., it passed fifteen major laws. Reporters borrowed the name from Napoleon's brief return to power in 1815. Roosevelt called his program the New Dealnoo deelThe name Roosevelt gave to his program of relief, recovery, and reform to fight the Great Depression.. The laws can be grouped under three purposes, which people at the time called relief, recovery, and reform.

Relief meant helping people who were suffering right away. The Civilian Conservation Corpssih-VIL-yun kon-ser-VAY-shun korA New Deal program that hired young unemployed men to plant trees, build trails, and fight soil erosion. About three million served. hired young unemployed men to plant trees, build trails, and fight erosion. They also fought forest fires and built bridges and campgrounds in the national parks. They earned thirty dollars a month, and most of it was sent home to their families. By rule, twenty-five of the thirty dollars went home. The Army ran the camps, with bunks, mess halls, and a bugle at dawn, and the first camp opened in Virginia that April. Over the years, the Corps planted more than three billion trees. About three million young men served in the Corps before it ended. The Federal Emergency Relief Administration sent federal money to states and cities to feed the hungry, at first matching each dollar a state spent with three more from Washington. Roosevelt put Harry Hopkins, the New York social worker, in charge, and Hopkins is said to have approved millions of dollars in grants in his first hours on the job, because hungry people could not wait. Reliefrih-LEEFMoney or goods given directly to people in need. now had a national source for the first time.

Recovery meant getting the economy moving again. The Agricultural Adjustment Actag-rih-KUL-chur-ul uh-JUST-ment aktA 1933 New Deal law that paid farmers to grow less, so that the prices of crops would rise. paid farmers to grow less, so that crop prices would rise. A farmer who signed up agreed to leave part of his land idle, and the government paid him for every acre he left unplanted. It was a harsh remedy, and in 1933 it led to the destruction of crops and millions of young pigs while people went hungry. The checks came from a tax on the mills and meatpackers that processed farm goods, and in the summer of 1933, farmers plowed under cotton that was already growing. The National Industrial Recovery ActNASH-un-ul in-DUS-tree-ul rih-KUV-er-ee aktA 1933 New Deal law that let businesses in each industry write codes setting prices, wages, and hours. It also protected workers' right to join unions. let businesses in each industry write codes of fair competition that set prices, wages, and hours. Many owners welcomed this, because it ended the price cutting that had been squeezing them. It also protected workers' right to join unions. The man who ran the new agency, General Hugh Johnson, wanted everyone to join one great national effort. A business that agreed to its code could hang a blue eagle poster in its window with the words "We Do Our Part," and shoppers were urged to buy only where the eagle hung. The same law set aside about three billion dollars for public works, to be spent under Harold Ickes, the Secretary of the Interior. The Tennessee Valley AuthorityTEN-uh-see VAL-ee aw-THOR-ih-teeA New Deal agency that built dams on the Tennessee River to control floods and make cheap electricity. built dams on the Tennessee River to control floods and make cheap electricity, in one of the poorest regions in the country. Before the dams, many farm homes in the valley had no electric light. The idea was an old one. During the Great War, the government had built a huge dam at Muscle Shoals, Alabama, and then argued for years over whether public or private owners should run it. Senator George Norris of Nebraska fought for public power, and Hoover vetoed his bill in 1931. Roosevelt signed a new one in May. Another step was quieter. In April, Roosevelt ordered Americans to turn in their gold coins and bars to the Federal Reserve in exchange for paper money, and he let the value of the dollar slide, hoping that rising prices would help debtors and farmers.

Reform meant fixing the system so that disaster would not repeat. The Securities Act required companies selling stock to tell the truth about their business. The Glass-Steagall Act, named for its sponsors, Senator Carter Glass of Virginia and Representative Henry Steagall of Alabama, separated ordinary banks from risky investment firms. It also set up deposit insurancedih-POZ-it in-SHOOR-unsA government guarantee that repays a customer's savings if a bank fails. It was created in 1933., which guarantees that the government will repay a customer's savings if a bank fails. At first the limit was $2,500 per account. Roosevelt himself was cool to the idea, fearing that it would reward careless bankers, but the public wanted it and Congress put it in the law. Its logic is simple. If every depositor knows the government will repay a failed bank, no one has a reason to rush the door, and the runs stop before they start. Your own family's bank account is protected by this idea today. A year later, a new agency, the Securities and Exchange Commission, was set up to police the stock market. Its first chairman was Joseph P. Kennedy, a financier who knew the market well and whose son would one day be president.

What was Roosevelt's method? He gathered a group of advisers, many of them college professors, whom reporters called the Brain Trustbrayn trustThe group of advisers, many of them college professors, whom Roosevelt gathered to help him plan the New Deal.. Among them were the Columbia professors Raymond Moley, Rexford Tugwell, and Adolf Berle. He tried many ideas at once. In a speech during the 1932 campaign, he explained his approach. "It is common sense to take a method and try it," he said. "If it fails, admit it frankly and try another. But above all, try something." That honesty won him trust, but it also meant the New Deal had no single theory. Some laws cut federal spending, such as the Economy Act, which cut the pay of federal workers and the pensions of some veterans, because Roosevelt had campaigned on balancing the budget and believed in it. Others raised spending. Some raised prices, which helped farmers but hurt consumers. A fair historian will not call the Hundred Days a perfect plan. It was a series of hurried experiments by people in an emergency.

Was the speed a strength or a danger? A republic must be able to deliberate and act. A country that cannot respond in a crisis has failed at one of its first duties. The Emergency Banking Act showed the benefits of quick action. The crisis ended, and the confidence of ordinary people returned. But a bill passed in an afternoon by members who had not read it is a risky way to make law. It places great trust in one person. Americans gave Roosevelt that trust, and for the moment they were glad they did. Whether the nation could keep the benefits of vigorous executive leadership without losing the habits of careful lawmaking would be a major question of the 1930s.

The session closed on June 16, the day Roosevelt signed the National Industrial Recovery Act and the Glass-Steagall bank law. The Hundred Days ended with the banks open and hope rising, but the Depression was far from over. Unemployment remained about one worker in four.
