📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Did the New Dealnoo deelThe name Roosevelt gave to his program of relief, recovery, and reform to fight the Great Depression. work? After 1933, the economy did begin to grow. Between 1934 and 1936, the nation's total output rose by roughly nine percent or more each year. Unemployment fell from about twenty-five percent to about fourteen percent in 1937. Then, in 1937 and 1938, the economy fell into a sharp recessionrih-SESH-unA period when a nation's output falls and many people lose their jobs. The United States had one in 1937 and 1938., a period when output drops and jobs disappear, and unemployment rose to nearly nineteen percent. Historians still argue about why. Some credit the New Deal's bank repairs and public spending for the recovery. Others point to a flood of gold coming into the country from a troubled Europe. Some argue that parts of the New Deal, like the codes of the National Industrial Recovery ActNASH-un-ul in-DUS-tree-ul rih-KUV-er-ee aktA 1933 New Deal law that let businesses in each industry write codes setting prices, wages, and hours. It also protected workers' right to join unions., actually slowed recovery by raising prices and limiting competition. The honest answer is that the programs helped some people a great deal, helped the whole economy somewhat, and did not end the Depression by themselves.

Workers took the New Deal's promises seriously. The recovery act had said that workers could organize and bargain, and in 1934 strikes broke out in Toledo, Minneapolis, and San Francisco. Some turned violent, and several strikers were killed. They showed that millions of workers wanted a real voice in their working lives, and next week's lessons will show how the government answered.

The limits of federal help could also be deadly. In the 1930s, hundreds of Great War veterans were sent to the Florida Keys to work on relief projects, building a highway across the islands. On Labor Day, 1935, one of the strongest hurricanes ever to strike the United States hit the Keys. The rescue train sent to carry the men out arrived too late, and hundreds of veterans died. Many Americans were angry, and some remembered the Bonus Army marchers who had been driven from Washington three years earlier. The disaster raised hard questions about who was responsible for the safety of citizens who worked for the government.

The New Deal drew critics from both sides. On the left, some people said that Roosevelt had not gone far enough. On the right, critics said the New Deal went too far. In 1934, a group of wealthy businessmen and conservative Democrats, including former presidential candidate Al Smith, founded the American Liberty Leagueuh-MER-ih-kun LIB-er-tee leegA group formed in 1934 by wealthy businessmen and conservative Democrats who argued that the New Deal threatened property rights and the Constitution.. They argued that the New Deal threatened property, freedom, and the Constitution. Roosevelt answered sharply. In 1936, he attacked what he called the "economic royalists" who opposed him. Voters gave him a verdict. Democrats gained seats in the 1934 midterm elections, which is unusual for the president's party. In 1936, Roosevelt won forty-six of the forty-eight states.

A deeper question ran under the debate. The Constitution divides power between the national government and the states, an arrangement called federalismFED-er-ul-iz-umThe system in which power is divided between the national government and the state governments.. Many New Deal programs were run through the states and cities. The states also differed from one another. Justice Louis Brandeis had argued in 1932 that this was a strength. In his words, "a single courageous State may, if its citizens choose, serve as a laboratory; and try novel social and economic experiments without risk to the rest of the country." In 1932, Wisconsin became the first state to pass unemployment insurance. Later, the federal government built a national system on lessons learned from experiments like that one.

Sharing power also had a price. Local control meant that local prejudices often shaped the results. Under the Agricultural Adjustment Actag-rih-KUL-chur-ul uh-JUST-ment aktA 1933 New Deal law that paid farmers to grow less, so that the prices of crops would rise., payments for cutting crops went mostly to landowners. In the South, many owners used the money to buy machines and to evict their tenants, including sharecroppersSHAIR-krop-erzFarmers who work land owned by someone else and pay for it with a share of the crop., who worked land for a share of the crop. Black farmers were hit hard. The Civilian Conservation Corpssih-VIL-yun kon-ser-VAY-shun korA New Deal program that hired young unemployed men to plant trees, build trails, and fight soil erosion. About three million served. kept its camps segregated. Roosevelt needed the votes of Southern Democrats in Congress, and many of them chaired powerful committees. To pass his bills, he often accepted rules that left much of the power in local hands. It was a trade, and it shows why historians describe the New Deal as a mixed achievement. It helped millions and left some of the neediest outside its reach.

The states also took part in a different decision that year. Prohibition ended in 1933. Congress proposed the 21st AmendmentTWEN-tee-furst uh-MEND-mentThe 1933 constitutional amendment that repealed the 18th Amendment and ended Prohibition. It is the only amendment ratified by special state conventions instead of state legislatures., which repealed the 18th, and then it did something unusual. It asked the states to ratify it not through their legislatures, but through special conventions elected by voters. It remains the only amendment ratified that way. The conventions voted to repeal in December 1933. Repeal reflected a lesson that Americans had learned over thirteen years. A law that a large part of the people refuse to obey weakens the respect for law itself. It also reflected the power of ordinary voters to correct a constitutional mistake, using the Constitution's own rules.

So what was the New Deal? Some Americans saw it as an emergency measure, a set of temporary steps to get the country through a crisis. Others saw it as the start of a permanent change in the relationship between citizens and their government. Both views had some truth. Some programs ended quickly. The Supreme Court would soon strike down others. Yet many lasted, including deposit insurance, rules for the stock market, and the Tennessee Valley AuthorityTEN-uh-see VAL-ee aw-THOR-ih-teeA New Deal agency that built dams on the Tennessee River to control floods and make cheap electricity., which still makes power in the South. After the 1930s, Americans expected the national government to take responsibility for the nation's economic security in a way they had not before.

That change raised constitutional questions that Roosevelt could not avoid. Could Congress really regulate the whole national economy? Could it give the president so much power? Next week, you will see how the Supreme Court answered, and what Roosevelt did when it said no.
