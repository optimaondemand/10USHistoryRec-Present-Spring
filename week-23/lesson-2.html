📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Did the New Dealnoo deelThe name Roosevelt gave to his program of relief, recovery, and reform to fight the Great Depression. work? After 1933, the economy did begin to grow. Between 1934 and 1936, the nation's total output rose by roughly nine percent or more each year. Unemployment fell from about twenty-five percent to about fourteen percent in 1937. Part of that fall came from a new kind of relief. In 1935, Roosevelt and Harry Hopkins created a huge jobs program that paid people wages for work instead of handing out aid. Millions of people drew pay from it, and you will read its story next week. Then, in 1937 and 1938, the economy fell into a sharp recessionrih-SESH-unA period when a nation's output falls and many people lose their jobs. The United States had one in 1937 and 1938., a period when output drops and jobs disappear, and unemployment rose to nearly nineteen percent. Industrial output fell by about a third in a few months. Two decisions are often blamed. Roosevelt, who had never liked deficits, cut federal spending in 1937, hoping the recovery would carry itself, and the Federal Reserve made banks hold more cash in reserve, which tightened lending. Critics gave the downturn a name, the "Roosevelt recession." Inside the White House, two camps had argued over the choice. Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau Jr. wanted a balanced budget at last. Hopkins warned that the recovery was not yet strong enough to stand on its own. In early 1937, Roosevelt sided with Morgenthau, and the cuts began. When output and jobs fell, he changed course. In the spring of 1938, he asked Congress for about three billion dollars more for relief and public works. Historians still argue about why. Some credit the New Deal's bank repairs and public spending for the recovery. Others point to a flood of gold coming into the country from a troubled Europe. Some argue that parts of the New Deal, like the codes of the National Industrial Recovery ActNASH-un-ul in-DUS-tree-ul rih-KUV-er-ee aktA 1933 New Deal law that let businesses in each industry write codes setting prices, wages, and hours. It also protected workers' right to join unions., actually slowed recovery by raising prices and limiting competition. The honest answer is that the programs helped some people a great deal, helped the whole economy somewhat, and did not end the Depression by themselves.

Workers took the New Deal's promises seriously. The recovery act had said that workers could organize and bargain, and in 1934 strikes broke out in Toledo, Minneapolis, and San Francisco. Some turned violent, and several strikers were killed. In Toledo, the National Guard was called out against strikers at an auto-parts plant. In San Francisco, police fired into a crowd of dock workers on July 5, killing two, and a few days later much of the city's work stopped in a general strike. They showed that millions of workers wanted a real voice in their working lives, and next week's lessons will show how the government answered.

The limits of federal help could also be deadly. In the 1930s, hundreds of Great War veterans were sent to the Florida Keys to work on relief projects, building a highway across the islands. On Labor Day, 1935, one of the strongest hurricanes ever to strike the United States hit the Keys. The men lived in flimsy camps. When the storm warning came, a train was sent to carry them out, but it started late and the storm arrived first. A wall of seawater more than fifteen feet high swept the train's cars off the track, and hundreds of veterans died. The writer Ernest Hemingway, who lived in Key West, went to see the bodies and wrote an angry magazine article asking who had murdered the veterans. The railroad that carried the rescue train never ran again. Its roadbed became the Overseas Highway, which opened in 1938. Many Americans were angry, and some remembered the Bonus Army marchers who had been driven from Washington three years earlier. The disaster raised hard questions about who was responsible for the safety of citizens who worked for the government.

The New Deal drew critics from both sides. On the left, some people said that Roosevelt had not gone far enough. He was shot and killed in 1935., the powerful senator from Louisiana, offered a plan he called Share Our Wealthshair our welthHuey Long's plan to limit private fortunes and give every family a basic income and a home.. His slogan was "Every Man a King," and his nickname was the Kingfish. As governor, Long had paved roads, built bridges, and given schoolchildren free textbooks, and he ran his state like a boss, with little patience for anyone who stood in his way. His plan would limit the size of private fortunes and give every family a basic income and a home. By 1935, tens of thousands of Share Our Wealth clubs had sprung up. Roosevelt's advisers feared that Long might run for president in 1936 and split the Democratic vote. In September 1935, a young doctor named Carl Weiss shot Long in the corridor of the state capitol in Baton Rouge. Weiss was killed on the spot by Long's bodyguards, and Long died two days later. A California doctor, Francis Townsend, himself sixty-six and out of work, said the idea came to him when he saw three old women searching garbage cans for food outside his window. He proposed that every American over sixty be paid two hundred dollars a month, if they spent it within the month. At the time, most older Americans had no pension, and the plan was to pay for it with a tax on sales. The Townsend PlanTOWN-zend planDr. Francis Townsend's proposal to pay every American over sixty two hundred dollars a month, on the condition that they spend it within the month. gathered millions of supporters. Economists who did the arithmetic found that it would have eaten up a huge share of everything the nation earned. Father CoughlinFAH-ther chahrlz KOG-linA Catholic priest from Michigan whose radio program reached millions of listeners. He first supported Roosevelt and then turned against him., a Catholic priest in Michigan, had one of the biggest radio audiences in the country. His first slogan for the New Deal was "Roosevelt or ruin." He first supported Roosevelt, then turned against him. In later years his broadcasts turned bitter and hostile to Jews, and in 1939 his church superiors ordered him off the air.

On the right, critics said the New Deal went too far. In 1934, a group of wealthy businessmen and conservative Democrats, including former presidential candidate Al Smith, founded the American Liberty Leagueuh-MER-ih-kun LIB-er-tee leegA group formed in 1934 by wealthy businessmen and conservative Democrats who argued that the New Deal threatened property rights and the Constitution.. The same Smith whom Roosevelt had nominated for president in 1928 now stood against him. Much of its money came from rich businessmen, including members of the du Pont family. They argued that the New Deal threatened property, freedom, and the Constitution. Roosevelt answered sharply. In 1936, he attacked what he called the "economic royalists" who opposed him. He used the phrase in his acceptance speech at an outdoor stadium in Philadelphia, where a leg brace gave way as he walked to the platform and he fell, scattering the pages of his speech. Aides lifted him up, and he gave the speech.

Voters gave him a verdict. Democrats gained seats in the 1934 midterm elections, which is unusual for the president's party. On the eve of the 1936 election, he told a crowd in Madison Square Garden that he welcomed the hatred of such men. His Republican opponent, Governor Alf Landon of Kansas, carried only Maine and Vermont. The followers of Long, Townsend, and Coughlin joined behind a third-party candidate, Congressman William Lemke of North Dakota, who won less than two percent of the vote. Many Black voters, who had long voted Republican as the party of Lincoln, voted Democratic in large numbers for the first time, because jobs and relief mattered more to them than old loyalty. In 1936, Roosevelt won forty-six of the forty-eight states.

A deeper question ran under the debate. The Constitution divides power between the national government and the states, an arrangement called federalismFED-er-ul-iz-umThe system in which power is divided between the national government and the state governments.. Many New Deal programs were run through the states and cities. The states also differed from one another. A state that wanted federal relief money had to put up part of the cost itself, and local boards usually decided who counted as needy, so help could be thinnest where the need was greatest. Justice Louis Brandeis, one of the most respected members of the Supreme Court, had argued in a 1932 dissent that this was a strength. In his words, "a single courageous State may, if its citizens choose, serve as a laboratory; and try novel social and economic experiments without risk to the rest of the country." In 1932, Wisconsin became the first state to pass unemployment insurance. Its law grew from years of work by economists at the state university, led by John R. Commons. Later, the federal government built a national system on lessons learned from experiments like that one.

    Sharing power also had a price. Local control meant that local prejudices often shaped the results. Under the Agricultural Adjustment Actag-rih-KUL-chur-ul uh-JUST-ment aktA 1933 New Deal law that paid farmers to grow less, so that the prices of crops would rise., payments for cutting crops went mostly to landowners. In the South, many owners used the money to buy machines and to evict their tenants, including sharecroppersSHAIR-krop-erzFarmers who work land owned by someone else and pay for it with a share of the crop., who worked land for a share of the crop. An acre left idle needed no one to work it, and the check went to the owner, who alone decided whether the family on that land would stay. Black farmers were hit hard. The Civilian Conservation Corpssih-VIL-yun kon-ser-VAY-shun korA New Deal program that hired young unemployed men to plant trees, build trails, and fight soil erosion. About three million served. kept its camps segregated. Black leaders pressed for fairer treatment. Mary McLeod Bethune, a Florida educator who had founded a school for Black girls in Daytona, became a friend of Eleanor Roosevelt and headed the Negro Affairs office of a New Deal youth agency. Roosevelt needed the votes of Southern Democrats in Congress, and many of them chaired powerful committees. Chairmen were chosen by seniority, and Southern Democrats, who faced almost no opposition at home, had served the longest. To pass his bills, he often accepted rules that left much of the power in local hands. It was a trade, and it shows why historians describe the New Deal as a mixed achievement. It helped millions and left some of the neediest outside its reach.

    The states also took part in a different decision that year. Prohibition ended in 1933. Congress proposed the 21st AmendmentTWEN-tee-furst uh-MEND-mentThe 1933 constitutional amendment that repealed the 18th Amendment and ended Prohibition. It is the only amendment ratified by special state conventions instead of state legislatures., which repealed the 18th, and then it did something unusual. It asked the states to ratify it not through their legislatures, but through special conventions elected by voters. The delegates ran pledged either for or against repeal, so each election was almost a direct vote on the question. It remains the only amendment ratified that way. Months earlier, in the spring, Congress had already made light beer legal again. The conventions voted to repeal in December 1933, and the vote of Utah's convention on December 5 supplied the thirty-sixth state, the number needed. Repeal reflected a lesson that Americans had learned over thirteen years. A law that a large part of the people refuse to obey weakens the respect for law itself. It also reflected the power of ordinary voters to correct a constitutional mistake, using the Constitution's own rules.

    So what was the New Deal? Some Americans saw it as an emergency measure, a set of temporary steps to get the country through a crisis. Others saw it as the start of a permanent change in the relationship between citizens and their government. Both views had some truth. Some programs ended quickly. The Civilian Conservation Corps lasted until 1942, when the war pulled its young men into uniform, and the jobs program of 1935 ended in 1943, as war jobs filled. The Supreme Court would soon strike down others. Yet many lasted, including deposit insurance, rules for the stock market, and the Tennessee Valley AuthorityTEN-uh-see VAL-ee aw-THOR-ih-teeA New Deal agency that built dams on the Tennessee River to control floods and make cheap electricity., which still makes power in the South. A good example is the Rural Electrification Administration, created in 1935. Private power companies had refused to string lines to farms, since too few customers lived too far apart, so the agency lent money to farm cooperatives to build their own. In 1930, only about one farm in ten had electric power. By the early 1950s, most farms did. After the 1930s, Americans expected the national government to take responsibility for the nation's economic security in a way they had not before.
