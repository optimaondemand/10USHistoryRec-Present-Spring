📖 TODAY'S LESSON

A country that shares the same radio programs does not necessarily share the same values. In the 1920s, Americans argued fiercely over drink, race, religion, and who counted as a "real American." Four stories show how deep the divisions ran.

A law needs the support of the people it governs, and when many citizens treat a law as a joke, respect for all law suffers.

The second story is darker. The Ku Klux Klankoo kluks klanA hate group revived in 1915 that attacked Black Americans, Catholics, Jews, and immigrants. It grew to millions of members in the 1920s. was revived in 1915 at Stone Mountain, Georgia. In the 1920s it spread far beyond the South, into Indiana, Ohio, Oregon, and other states. It claimed several million members at its peak. It attacked Black Americans, Catholics, Jews, and immigrants, and its members used whippings, beatings, and murder. Many Klan groups also presented themselves as ordinary civic clubs, with parades, picnics, and talk of patriotism. Its leaders earned money by selling memberships and robes. The Klan collapsed in the late 1920s after scandals, including the 1925 murder conviction of an Indiana Klan leader named D. C. Stephenson. Judged by the republican tradition, the Klan was a clear failure of civic culture. It claimed to protect the community, but it set citizen against citizen and put violence above law.

The American Civil Liberties Union offered to defend any teacher who would test the law. Business leaders in Dayton, Tennessee, hoping for publicity, persuaded a young teacher named John Scopes to be the defendant. Radio carried the trial across the country. At one point, Darrow called Bryan to the stand as an expert on the Bible, and Bryan struggled under his questions. The jury took only minutes to convict Scopes, and he was fined one hundred dollars. The Tennessee Supreme Court later threw out the conviction on a technicality. Bryan died five days after the trial. Reporters from the big cities mocked the townspeople, and many rural Americans felt mocked in turn. The law stayed on the books for more than forty years.

The fourth story is about a movement of pride among Black Americans. Marcus GarveyMAR-kus GAR-veeA Jamaican-born leader who taught Black pride and self-reliance and founded the Universal Negro Improvement Association. came to New York from Jamaica in 1916. He founded the Universal Negro Improvement Associationyoo-nih-VER-sul NEE-groh im-PROOV-ment uh-soh-see-AY-shunThe organization Marcus Garvey founded to promote Black pride, Black-owned business, and self-reliance., which preached pride in African heritage, Black-owned businesses, and self-reliance. Some of his followers also hoped to build a new life in Africa. Garvey started a shipping company called the Black Star Line and sold stock to thousands of Black buyers. The ships were old and the business failed. In 1923, Garvey was convicted of mail fraud. He went to prison in 1925. President Coolidge later cut his sentence short, and Garvey was deported in 1927. Black leaders such as W. E. B. Du Bois, who worked for full citizenship and equal rights inside the United States, criticized Garvey sharply. But Garvey's message of dignity reached many people who felt shut out of the national story. The movement shows that the Harlem RenaissanceHAR-lum REN-uh-sahnsA flowering of Black writing, art, and music in the 1920s centered in the Harlem neighborhood of New York City. and the Jazz Age were not the only things happening in Black America. Some Black Americans saw hope in the nation's promises, and some had stopped believing in them.

These four stories point to a split in the country. The 1920 census showed that, for the first time, most Americans lived in towns and cities instead of on farms and in villages. Cities were crowded and full of immigrants. They were home to jazz, speakeasies, and new ideas about manners and religion. Many rural and small-town Americans felt that their way of life was being pushed aside. They answered with Prohibition, with fundamentalism, and, in some places, with the Klan. These were not the same thing, and it would be unfair to treat them as one. Prohibition, for example, had many sincere and decent supporters. But all of them were ways of asking who owned the country's future.

The presidential election of 1928 put the split on the ballot. The Democratic candidate, Governor Al Smith of New York, was the son of immigrants, a Catholic, a product of city politics, and an open opponent of Prohibition. Many city voters admired him. Many rural Protestants distrusted him, and some attacked him because of his faith. Herbert Hoover, a Protestant and a supporter of the "noble experiment" of Prohibition, won in a landslide. Smith lost, but he also pulled millions of new voters from the cities into the Democratic Party, a change that would matter in the next decade.

So which was the real 1920s? The Jazz Age was real. The mass culturemas KUL-churA culture shared by a whole nation through radio, movies, newspapers, and magazines instead of by one town or group. you studied in the last lesson was real. So was the culture war. Historians often try to fit a decade into a single label, but a decade rarely fits. A better approach is to ask who was living through each version. A young clerk in New York, a farm family in Tennessee, and a Black family in Harlem could all have lived the 1920s very differently, and all of them would have been telling the truth. What held the country together through these fights was the Constitution's framework of ordinary law. How well that framework would hold up under a far greater strain is the subject of the next two weeks.
