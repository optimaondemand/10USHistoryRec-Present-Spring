📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In November 1920, a radio station in Pittsburgh called KDKAkay dee kay AYThe Pittsburgh radio station that broadcast the 1920 presidential election results, one of the first regular radio stations in the country. did something new. It told a few thousand listeners who had won the presidential election, as the results came in. The station belonged to Westinghouse, a company that made electrical equipment, and an engineer named Frank Conrad had been playing records and talking over the air from his garage. The company saw that if people could hear programs, they would want to buy sets. Few people owned a set. But within ten years, about four in ten American homes had one, and many families bought theirs on the installment planin-STAWL-ment planA way of buying in which the buyer pays a small amount down and the rest in monthly payments. If the buyer misses payments, the seller can take the product back.. Within two years of KDKA, hundreds of stations were on the air, run by department stores, newspapers, colleges, and churches. Radio was among the most wanted of the new consumer durableskun-SOO-mer DOOR-uh-bulzGoods meant to last for years, such as cars, radios, refrigerators, and washing machines..

Radio was a broadcastBRAWD-kastA radio or television message sent out to everyone at once., which means a message sent out to everyone at once. Before radio, news traveled by newspaper, mail, and train, and it arrived at different times in different places. Early sets were simple crystal sets that one person listened to through headphones. By the middle of the 1920s, sets with loudspeakers let a whole family gather around the radio in the evening, the way families once gathered around the fireplace. Now a farm family in Kansas and a shop clerk in Boston could hear the same voice at the same moment. In 1926, a company called NBC linked stations into the first national network, and CBS followed in 1927. The stations were tied together by telephone wires, so a program that began in New York could be heard in a dozen cities at once. Listeners heard news, sermons, comedy, baseball games, and dance bands. Baseball announcers described games to people who could not see them, and farm stations read crop prices and weather reports so that farmers no longer had to wait for the newspaper. Even preachers took to the air. In Los Angeles, the evangelist Aimee Semple McPherson opened a station in 1924 and reached listeners far beyond the walls of her huge church. Advertisers paid for most of it. The first paid radio advertisement, in 1922, was a short talk selling apartments in a new housing development in Queens, New York. Before long, companies were sponsoring whole programs. That made American radio different from radio in Britain, where the government ran the service. Congress did step in on one point. By 1926, so many stations were crowding the same wavelengths that signals drowned one another out. The Radio Act of 1927 treated the airwaves as public property and set up a commission to license stations, but it left the programs in private hands.

Radio also changed politics. In 1924, the party conventions were broadcast for the first time, and millions listened. The Democrats' convention in New York needed 103 ballots to choose a candidate, and listeners followed it for about two weeks. In 1925, millions heard President Coolidge take his oath of office. Voters could now judge a candidate's voice as well as his words.

Movies were an even bigger draw. Tens of millions of Americans went to the theater each week. In big cities, the new movie palaces had thousands of seats and live orchestras. Audiences made celebritiessuh-LEB-rih-teeA famous person whose face and private life are known to many strangers. of actors like Charlie Chaplin and Mary Pickford, people whose faces and private lives were known to strangers across the country. When the silent-film star Rudolph Valentino died suddenly in 1926 at the age of thirty-one, crowds lined the New York streets for hours to pass his coffin, by some counts more than a hundred thousand people. Many of them had never met him. Vaudeville, the traveling variety shows that had been the country's favorite stage entertainment, began to empty out, because movies and radio offered the same sort of acts for far less money. In 1927, the film The Jazz Singer added spoken words and songs to a feature film. Most of that film was still silent, and only the songs and a few lines were spoken, but audiences were thrilled. These new talkiesTAW-keezMovies with recorded sound and spoken words. They replaced silent films after 1927. almost ended silent films within a few years. In 1928, a Walt Disney cartoon called Steamboat Willie starred a mouse named Mickey and became the first cartoon with a sound track that matched the pictures. Hollywood, California, became the capital of an industry that sent the same stories to every town in the nation. The studios also worried about their image. After a few scandals, they hired a Washington politician named Will Hays in 1922 to police the morals of their own films, partly to head off censorship laws.

Together, radio, movies, newspapers, and magazines created a mass culturemas KUL-churA culture shared by a whole nation through radio, movies, newspapers, and magazines instead of by one town or group.. That means a culture shared by the whole nation, instead of by one town or one group. Americans in different places began to sing the same songs, laugh at the same jokes, and cheer for the same heroes. Chain stores and national brands, advertised in magazines and on the radio, put the same goods on the shelves of towns far apart. Magazines did their part. Time began in 1923 to sum up the week's news in a few pages, and the Reader's Digest, started in 1922, shrank articles from other magazines to pocket size, so that a busy reader in any town could keep up with the same stories as everyone else. Sports offered heroes of their own, and newspapers and radio announcers turned athletes into household names. Babe Ruth had been sold by the Boston Red Sox to the New York Yankees in 1920 for $100,000, a huge sum then. In 1927, he hit sixty home runs in a single season, and fans followed every game by radio and newspaper.

The greatest hero of the decade came from the air. On May 20, 1927, a 25-year-old airmail pilot named Charles Lindberghchahrlz LIND-bergThe pilot who flew alone from New York to Paris in 1927 and became the best-known hero of the decade. took off from New York in a small plane called the Spirit of St. Louis. A hotel owner had offered a $25,000 prize to the first flier to cross the Atlantic nonstop between New York and Paris, and several experienced teams were competing for it. The flight would cover about 3,600 miles. He flew alone, with no radio and no parachute. He had stripped away everything he could to carry more fuel. A fuel tank sat in front of him, so he could not see straight ahead and had to look out the sides or use a small periscope. Several other fliers had died trying to cross the Atlantic, and two French fliers had vanished over the ocean just days before.

Thirty-three hours later, at about ten at night, he landed at an airfield outside Paris to an enormous cheering crowd. He had struggled through the night to stay awake. More than a hundred thousand people were waiting at the airfield, and they rushed the plane so fast that the police could not hold them back. Lindbergh won because of careful planning, steady nerves, and the stubbornness to stay awake and keep going. He is a good example of the "great man" in history, a person whose courage and skill shaped what others thought was possible. When he came home, New York gave him a parade, and newspapers across the country printed his name above everything else for weeks. Millions of Americans who had never met him felt proud to share his country.

Another part of mass culture was music. JazzjazA style of music born in the Black neighborhoods of New Orleans. It mixes blues, ragtime, and church music and leaves room for improvising. was born in the Black neighborhoods of New Orleans. It mixed the blues, ragtime, and church music into a new sound with plenty of room for improvising. Players took a tune and made up new versions on the spot, each trying to outdo the last. As Black families moved from the rural South to northern cities, a movement later called the Great Migrationgrayt my-GRAY-shunThe movement of millions of Black Americans from the rural South to cities in the North and West, which grew rapidly during and after the Great War., they brought their music with them. In all, more than a million Black Americans left the South in the years around the war. Chicago and New York became centers of jazz. Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington became stars. Armstrong, a trumpet player from New Orleans, moved to Chicago in 1922 to play in King Oliver's band, and Ellington led an orchestra at the Cotton Club in Harlem, where his music went out over the radio. Records and radio carried the music to white and Black listeners alike. In 1920, a singer named Mamie Smith recorded a blues song that sold so well that record companies discovered a market of Black buyers and began making records just for them. The writer F. Scott Fitzgerald gave the decade its nickname, the "Jazz Age." His 1925 novel The Great Gatsby described rich men's glittering parties and the emptiness behind them. Young women who wore short skirts and bobbed hair, called flappersFLAP-erA young woman of the 1920s who wore short skirts and bobbed hair. Flappers became a symbol of the Jazz Age., became its symbol. Women had won the right to vote across the nation with the 19th Amendment in 1920, and many young women took new freedoms in work, dress, and public life. Older Americans often disapproved. The popular picture of the flapper was exaggerated, but it showed that styles and manners really were changing.

Not all of the new music came from the big cities. In the summer of 1927, a record producer named Ralph Peer rented a vacant warehouse in Bristol, a town on the line between Tennessee and Virginia, and offered to pay local musicians to record. Among those who answered were a family group called the Carter Family and a former railroad brakeman named Jimmie Rodgers. Their records sold across the South and beyond. What later came to be called country music grew in large part out of those sessions. The same machines that carried jazz north now carried mountain ballads and railroad songs to listeners who had never seen a mountain.

In Harlem, a neighborhood of New York City, Black writers, artists, and musicians produced a flowering of creative work called the Harlem RenaissanceHAR-lum REN-uh-sahnsA flowering of Black writing, art, and music in the 1920s centered in the Harlem neighborhood of New York City.. Among them were the writers Zora Neale Hurston, Claude McKay, and Countee Cullen, and the painter Aaron Douglas. Among its poets was Langston HughesLANG-stun hyoosA poet of the Harlem Renaissance who wrote about Black life and American hopes., who wrote in clear, strong language about Black life and American hopes. At the Savoy Ballroom in Harlem, dancers invented a wild new dance in the late 1920s and named it for the famous flier. It was called the Lindy Hop.

Hughes came to notice almost by accident. He worked as a busboy in a Washington hotel, and in 1925 he left three of his poems beside the plate of the poet Vachel Lindsay, who was eating there. Lindsay read them aloud to an audience that night, and newspapers reported that a busboy poet had been found. Hughes's first book of poems came out the next year. In his poem "I, Too," he answered those who would keep him out of the national family. "I, too, sing America," he wrote. Hughes and his friends insisted that Black Americans were not outsiders to the national story. They were part of its making. This was a bold claim in a country where segregation was still the law across the South and the custom in much of the North. Black performers often played to white audiences in clubs where Black patrons were not welcome.

Was this new shared culture good for the Republic? The classical republican tradition says that a political community needs citizens who feel a common affection for one another beyond their families and neighborhoods. Mass culture may have helped. Millions of people now had common stories, common songs, and a common set of heroes. When Lindbergh landed in Paris, a whole nation felt pride at once. That is a kind of bond.

Yet the bond had limits. Mass culture was bought and sold. Advertisers shaped what people heard, and entertainment companies chased profit before anything else. Many local traditions faded as national tastes took over. And some Americans were kept from the shared experience, or were welcome only as entertainers. Some critics said that cheering for the same hero is a thin kind of unity. Others replied that in a country as large as the United States, any common feeling is a good thing.
