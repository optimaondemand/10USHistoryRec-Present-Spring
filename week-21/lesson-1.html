In November 1920, a radio station in Pittsburgh called KDKAkay dee kay AYThe Pittsburgh radio station that broadcast the 1920 presidential election results, one of the first regular radio stations in the country. did something new. It told a few thousand listeners who had won the presidential election, as the results came in. Few people owned a set. But within ten years, about four in ten American homes had one, and many families bought theirs on the installment planin-STAWL-ment planA way of buying in which the buyer pays a small amount down and the rest in monthly payments. If the buyer misses payments, the seller can take the product back.. Radio was among the most wanted of the new consumer durableskun-SOO-mer DOOR-uh-bulzGoods meant to last for years, such as cars, radios, refrigerators, and washing machines..

Radio was a broadcastBRAWD-kastA radio or television message sent out to everyone at once., which means a message sent out to everyone at once. Before radio, news traveled by newspaper, mail, and train, and it arrived at different times in different places. Now a farm family in Kansas and a shop clerk in Boston could hear the same voice at the same moment. In 1926, a company called NBC linked stations into the first national network, and CBS followed in 1927. Listeners heard news, sermons, comedy, baseball games, and dance bands. Advertisers paid for most of it. That made American radio different from radio in Britain, where the government ran the service. Radio also changed politics. In 1924, the party conventions were broadcast for the first time, and in 1925 millions heard President Coolidge take his oath of office. Voters could now judge a candidate's voice as well as his words.

Movies were an even bigger draw. Tens of millions of Americans went to the theater each week. Audiences made celebritiessuh-LEB-rih-teeA famous person whose face and private life are known to many strangers. of actors like Charlie Chaplin and Mary Pickford, people whose faces and private lives were known to strangers across the country. In 1927, the film The Jazz Singer added spoken words and songs to a feature film. These new talkiesTAW-keezMovies with recorded sound and spoken words. They replaced silent films after 1927. almost ended silent films within a few years. Hollywood, California, became the capital of an industry that sent the same stories to every town in the nation.

Together, radio, movies, newspapers, and magazines created a mass culturemas KUL-churA culture shared by a whole nation through radio, movies, newspapers, and magazines instead of by one town or group.. That means a culture shared by the whole nation, instead of by one town or one group. Americans in different places began to sing the same songs, laugh at the same jokes, and cheer for the same heroes. Sports offered heroes of their own, and newspapers and radio announcers turned athletes into household names. In 1927, Babe Ruth hit sixty home runs in a single season, and fans followed every game by radio and newspaper.

The greatest hero of the decade came from the air. On May 20, 1927, a 25-year-old airmail pilot named Charles Lindberghchahrlz LIND-bergThe pilot who flew alone from New York to Paris in 1927 and became the best-known hero of the decade. took off from New York in a small plane called the Spirit of St. Louis. He flew alone, with no radio and no parachute. Thirty-three hours later, he landed in Paris to an enormous cheering crowd. Several other fliers had died trying to cross the Atlantic. Lindbergh won because of careful planning, steady nerves, and the stubbornness to stay awake and keep going. He is a good example of the "great man" in history, a person whose courage and skill shaped what others thought was possible. Millions of Americans who had never met him felt proud to share his country.

Another part of mass culture was music. JazzjazA style of music born in the Black neighborhoods of New Orleans. It mixes blues, ragtime, and church music and leaves room for improvising. was born in the Black neighborhoods of New Orleans. It mixed the blues, ragtime, and church music into a new sound with plenty of room for improvising. As Black families moved from the rural South to northern cities, a movement later called the Great Migrationgrayt my-GRAY-shunThe movement of millions of Black Americans from the rural South to cities in the North and West, which grew rapidly during and after the Great War., they brought their music with them. Chicago and New York became centers of jazz. Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington became stars. Records and radio carried the music to white and Black listeners alike. The writer F. Scott Fitzgerald gave the decade its nickname, the "Jazz Age." Young women who wore short skirts and bobbed hair, called flappersFLAP-erA young woman of the 1920s who wore short skirts and bobbed hair. Flappers became a symbol of the Jazz Age., became its symbol. Women had won the right to vote across the nation with the 19th Amendment in 1920, and many young women took new freedoms in work, dress, and public life. Older Americans often disapproved. The popular picture of the flapper was exaggerated, but it showed that styles and manners really were changing.

In Harlem, a neighborhood of New York City, Black writers, artists, and musicians produced a flowering of creative work called the Harlem RenaissanceHAR-lum REN-uh-sahnsA flowering of Black writing, art, and music in the 1920s centered in the Harlem neighborhood of New York City.. Among its poets was Langston HughesLANG-stun hyoosA poet of the Harlem Renaissance who wrote about Black life and American hopes., who wrote in clear, strong language about Black life and American hopes. In his poem "I, Too," he answered those who would keep him out of the national family. "I, too, sing America," he wrote. Hughes and his friends insisted that Black Americans were not outsiders to the national story. They were part of its making. This was a bold claim in a country where segregation was still the law across the South and the custom in much of the North. Black performers often played to white audiences in clubs where Black patrons were not welcome.

Was this new shared culture good for the Republic? The classical republican tradition says that a political community needs citizens who feel a common affection for one another beyond their families and neighborhoods. Mass culture may have helped. Millions of people now had common stories, common songs, and a common set of heroes. When Lindbergh landed in Paris, a whole nation felt pride at once. That is a kind of bond.

Yet the bond had limits. Mass culture was bought and sold. Advertisers shaped what people heard, and entertainment companies chased profit before anything else. Many local traditions faded as national tastes took over. And some Americans were kept from the shared experience, or were welcome only as entertainers. Some critics said that cheering for the same hero is a thin kind of unity. Others replied that in a country as large as the United States, any common feeling is a good thing.

The question matters because the next lesson shows what happened when Americans disagreed about what their shared life should be. The same decade that produced the Jazz Age also produced fierce fights over alcohol, race, religion, and who counted as a "real American." You will decide this week which description fits the 1920s better.
